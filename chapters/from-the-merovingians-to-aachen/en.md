# Franks – Twelve loaves for a denarius

A customer arriving at a baker’s stall in 794 with a single denarius could have found a remarkably precise answer to the question of what it ought to buy. The king’s ordinance specified twelve wheat loaves. Choose oats instead and the same silver penny should buy more than twice as many pieces. We still have the text of the ordinance. From this particular stall, we have scarcely a crumb. What business did a Frankish king have with the weight of a loaf? And what connects that purchase with the crown Charles received in Rome six years later?

## The son beside the king {#saint-denis}

When Charles stood before Pope Stephen II at Saint-Denis in 754, his father Pippin was already king. The pope anointed the father and both his sons, Charles and Carloman. Readers arriving from [the chapter about the last Merovingian](chapter:the-last-merovingian-king#saint-denis) will recognise the church as a burial place of the previous dynasty. The monks of Saint-Denis kept charters that could substantiate their monastery’s property claims in court. Those documents also survived the change of ruling family.

The anointing gave both boys a place in the ceremony. When Pippin died in 768, both succeeded him. Carloman died three years later. Someone who had seen the boys standing together in 754 could hardly have guessed simply from the ritual that one of them would come to be known as Charlemagne. The historian Conor O’Brien has compared the accounts of such anointings. The importance that a later author attached to anointing could tell us something about its meaning for the author’s own generation.

## How much should a loaf weigh? {#frankfurt}

Bishops and magnates assembled at Frankfurt am Main in 794. Among the measures recorded there is a rule about the sale of grain and bread. In a good harvest or a bad one, a seller was to charge at most one denarius for a given measure of oats, four for the same measure of wheat. Grain from the king’s own stocks was to be offered at lower rates. Once the grain had been milled and baked, the comparison called for another measure. Each piece was to weigh two pounds, whether made of wheat, rye, barley or oats. The pound in question was a medieval unit of weight.

Why fix both the price and the weight? Twelve tiny loaves would feed a household less well than twelve large ones. The second figure made it possible to check the quantity too. The end of the provision is particularly revealing. A man who held a benefice from the king had a duty to see that the unfree people dependent on it had enough to eat. Only after their needs had been met could he sell the surplus at the stated rates. The provision cannot tell us whether any particular child on a royal estate went to bed hungry. It does tell us whom the king meant to hold responsible.

## Who placed the crown on Charles? {#rome}

At Christmas in 800 Charles was in St Peter’s in Rome. The Frankish Royal Annals place him at prayer at the apostle’s shrine. As he rose, Pope Leo III set a crown on his head. The Romans acclaimed him as emperor. In this account each group has a visible part. Attention falls on the pope’s hand and the voices of the people in the church. The annals put the occasion under the year numbered 801, which there begins at Christmas. In our calendar the date is 25 December 800.

Einhard, who had known Charles, later placed the emphasis elsewhere. Charles had said that he would have stayed away from church that day had he known of the pope’s intention. This is a report of a remark, written by a member of his circle after his death. It leaves open how much had been agreed beforehand and who had been consulted. Read alongside the annals, it lets us follow the change in meaning when a writer turns his attention from the people in the church to the attitude of the man they acclaimed.

## A burial beneath the dome {#aachen}

When Charles died at Aachen in January 814, the palace chapel was already standing. Its octagonal central space remains visible in the cathedral today. A gallery rises above the surrounding aisle, with a dome overhead. Einhard says Charles brought columns and marble from Rome and Ravenna for the building. Some of what looked new at Aachen had seen service in another city. Readers familiar with [Theoderic’s Ravenna](chapter:theoderic-and-ravenna) meet it again as a place from which precious building materials crossed the Alps.

Einhard also describes a decision that had to be taken on the day Charles died. No one, he says, knew of instructions from Charles about where to bury him. Those present chose the church he had built himself. A building whose stone bore witness to journeys across the mountains became a place of remembrance for a Frankish ruler.

## When a brother swears in my language {#strasbourg}

On 14 February 842 two of Charles’s grandsons met at Strasbourg. Louis and Charles the Bald made an alliance against their brother Lothar. Nithard included the words of the oaths in his Latin history. Louis pronounced the Romance version and Charles the Germanic one. Each brother’s followers could hear what the other had undertaken to do. Then the followers swore, each group in its own language. Their promises too had conditions. If their own king broke the agreement, they undertook, in the circumstances set out in the oath, to withhold their support against his brother.

A manuscript in Paris still lets readers follow the changes of language on a single leaf. In the detail shown here the Romance phrase **Pro Deo amur** appears near the top of the column. The map marks Strasbourg, where people needed to understand the words they heard. The library preserves the work of a later scribe. Both have a part in what we know of that February day.
