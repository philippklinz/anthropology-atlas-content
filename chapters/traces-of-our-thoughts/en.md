# Traces of Our Thoughts

A few red lines on stone. The outline left around a hand. Figures on a cave ceiling. A bone flute. Objects like these bring us closer to people whose names, words and songs have been lost.

This chapter connects four findsites in Africa, Southeast Asia and Europe. It overlaps in time with “When We Were Not Alone”. We explore surviving forms of expression; their earliest known traces do not date the beginning of human thought.

## Blombos: Recognizing intention

Around 73,000 years ago, a small silcrete fragment entered the archaeological layer at Blombos Cave in present-day South Africa. Nine red lines cross its smoothed surface. Analysis and drawing experiments support the use of a pointed ochre implement.

Open the image and follow the lines. Material evidence can help establish that they were drawn deliberately. What the pattern meant remains unknown. A drawing does not by itself establish writing. The well-known engraved ochre pieces from Blombos are different objects.

## Muna: Leaving the shape of a hand

A hand stencil preserves pigment around the unpainted shape of a hand. At Liang Metanduno on the Indonesian island of Muna, only a small fragment survives. A study published in 2026 establishes a minimum age of 67,800 years for the motif beneath calcite sample LMET2.

“Minimum” matters here: researchers dated calcite that grew over the image later. The image can therefore be older. The study makes a reasoned attribution to Homo sapiens, while acknowledging that the makers’ biological identity cannot be directly established.

## Sulawesi: When do figures tell a story?

At Leang Karampuang, researchers identify a pig and at least three human-like figures. They interpret the arrangement as a composed scene. The oldest dated calcite overgrowth above one figure supplies a minimum age of 51,200 years.

The tracing helps reveal forms that are difficult to see. What is happening between them? The original study leaves the specific action unresolved; hunting is not clearly established. Interpreting the composition as a scene offers a glimpse of storytelling through images, without preserving a particular story for us.

## Geißenklösterle: An object for sound

A cave near Blaubeuren yielded remains of bird-bone and ivory flutes. Their Aurignacian archaeological context takes us back roughly 40,000 years. The photograph shows a swan-bone flute, including surviving openings and breaks.

An instrument can survive in a way that a played melody cannot. We do not know the songs or the occasions on which they were played. A modern replica could explore possible sounds; it would not reproduce a preserved ancient concert.

## What can we compare?

Compare materials, techniques and dating evidence: pigment on a portable stone, hand stencils, figures on a wall and worked bones for sound. The sites belong to different contexts. Displaying them together does not establish a journey or direct transmission between them.

The viewing window runs from 80,000 to 35,000 years BP. It bounds the time shown, not the possible age of every image. In the atlas, BP refers to 1950; rounded study ages are placed on this comparison scale. Sources and more detailed dating notes accompany the objects and events.
