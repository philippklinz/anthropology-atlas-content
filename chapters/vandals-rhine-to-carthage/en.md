# From the Rhine to Carthage

Someone who crossed the Rhine as a child could have had grown children by the time Carthage fell. More than thirty years separated the two events. During those years people changed allies, lost relatives and started families. We can scarcely trace any one life all the way between the two places. At a few stops along the way, however, we can still recover names and hear what mattered to particular people.

## At the Rhine

An entry in Prosper’s chronicle records Vandals and Alans crossing the Rhine into Gaul on the last day of the year. The usual reading gives 31 December 406. Historians have also proposed the end of 405. Prosper gives no crossing point. Mainz appears among the cities attacked in a letter by Jerome. He was writing from Bethlehem to a widow in Gaul. Hoping to dissuade her from marrying again, he reminded her how uncertain her future was. It is difficult to trace individual groups’ movements from his list of cities.

## In the provinces of Hispania

The chronicler Hydatius records Alans, Vandals and Sueves entering the Iberian Peninsula in 409. His entry for 411 distinguishes several groups. He places Vandals and Sueves in Gallaecia in the northwest, and Siling Vandals in the southern province of Baetica. The inhabitants of the Roman provinces continued to live in their towns and in the countryside. A colour on the map can indicate where the chronicler placed a group. His account tells us little about how many people there belonged to it.

War altered these arrangements. Goths under King Wallia fought in Hispania on Rome’s behalf. After their king Addax died, surviving Alans sought protection from the Vandal king Gunderic. Hydatius later describes Gunderic taking Hispalis, modern Seville. His brother Geiseric succeeded him as king. In 429 Geiseric and his followers crossed from the coast of Baetica to North Africa. Hydatius expressly mentions the families who went with them. He leaves the port of departure unnamed.

## Inside the besieged city

Hippo Regius, near modern Annaba in Algeria, lets us follow the crossing from the other shore. Bishop Possidius was staying with his friend Augustine when the city came under siege in 430. His Life of Augustine preserves a correspondence. Could clergy flee if their congregations stayed behind? Augustine wrote that people should be free to seek safety but that someone must remain to care for those who stayed. He died during the siege. The letter preserves a choice that people in a threatened community had to make.

## Carthage and its hinterland

Geiseric’s forces took Carthage in 439. Its port had shipped North African grain to Rome. The western imperial government lost access to lands that yielded substantial tax revenue. A treaty in 442 settled the division of African territories between king and emperor.

Another source brings us down to the scale of a single farm. In 494 Processanus and Siddina sold cultivation rights on a plot with olive and fig trees. Their contract survives on wood. Donatianus, the scribe, recorded that each seller made a mark because they could not write. He dated the transaction by the regnal years of the Vandal king Gunthamund. The language of the contract was Latin, and the farmers’ cultivation rights went back to Roman times. On the same tablet we meet a new ruler and a familiar way to record a claim on land.
