# Waterworks and Walls in Early China

Dams, pipes and walls show how people built in three different landscapes. The first stop is in the lower Yangtze region, the second on the plain of present-day Henan, and the third on the northern Loess Plateau. Compare the sites on the map. At each stop, look at what was excavated and what choices the evidence can help explain.

## Liangzhu

Where would you hold back water in hilly terrain? Northwest of Liangzhu, high dams fill gaps between hills. Low dams and levees extend the system down on the plain. The exposed section of the Laohuling dam shows stacked blocks of clay. Builders had wrapped clay in grasses and layered the bundles as they built. The photograph shows the section behind glass today.

Plant material from the bundles and charcoal from the layers provided radiocarbon dates. Most dates for the high dams fall around 5,200–4,800 calibrated years before 1950, or about 3250–2850 BCE in round figures. Most dates for the low dams and levees fall around 3050–2850 BCE. The study places construction of much of the city around 2950–2650 BCE. Compare the high-dam zone and the city on the map. Parts of the hydraulic system were probably in place before much of the city was built. The date windows overlap because construction phases are dated approximately.

## Pingliangtai

On the flat plain of present-day Henan, heavy summer rain presented a different problem for earthen buildings and paths. Ditches ran beside the houses. They carried water toward the southern gate. There, ceramic pipes lay beneath the path to the settlement's main street. The excavation photograph shows two rows of pipe sections in ditch G29. Most of the studied pipe sections were about 30–40 centimetres long.

Settlement evidence spans roughly 2250–1950 BCE. Most dated deposits associated with the pipes fall into the later span of about 2150–1950 BCE. Layers near the southern gate show that ditches were dug again and pipes laid repeatedly. The excavators interpret the system as a combination of work around individual houses and shared maintenance of outlets. Who set the rules for that work remains open.

## Shimao

On the northern Loess Plateau, people built up a hilltop settlement between about 2300 and 1800 BCE. Two stone-faced walls divide the terrain into inner and outer areas. A cliff above the Tuwei River completes the enclosure. Inside, stone walls retain built-up terraces. The slope helps explain why some walls support the ground while others limit access to the settlement.

The size of around 400 hectares describes the extent of the archaeological site. The number of people living there in any given phase and the way they organized the work are separate questions. Together the three sites show how construction methods, the lay of the land and traces of repairs can help us read the evidence.
