# Gathering, Storing and Sowing

A handful of grain can tell a story of gathering, sowing or both. Foragers collect plants that grew without their having planted them. Cultivators influence how plants grow, for example by sowing grain. Domestication changes the inherited traits of a plant population over generations. A plant can therefore be cultivated while retaining the traits of its wild relatives.

We compare three sites in Southwest Asia between 11,700 and 9,500 years BP. In the dates below, cal BP means calibrated calendar years before 1950. The stops belong to different landscapes and communities and together form no itinerary.

## Dhra′

Dhra′ lies to the east of the southern end of the Dead Sea in present-day Jordan. Among the remains of round buildings, archaeologists found a structure about three metres across. Notched upright stones stood in rows. Some had previously been used for grinding. Charred wood and plant impressions in mud add to the evidence.

The excavation team reconstructs a raised floor resting on the stone supports. The space underneath could have protected stores from damp and animals. Construction of the first phase is dated to about 11,300–11,200 cal BP. Plant remains at the site are consistent with the use of barley. A later study interprets the size of the grains as a possible sign of cultivation before domestication. The architecture helps answer how people could have stored food. The origin of those stores requires other evidence.

## Tell Qarassa North

In southern Syria, little fragments of ears of grain helped answer a different question. When wild cereals ripen, the stalk that holds the grains usually breaks apart and spreads the seeds. In many domesticated forms the ear stays intact. Threshing breaks the ear apart by force, leaving different traces where it breaks.

For the early pre-pottery excavation area of Tell Qarassa North, researchers examined 732 such fragments from emmer, einkorn and barley. They could classify the break shape on 305 fragments. These included both wild-type and domesticated-type forms. The team interprets the evidence from around 10,700–10,200 cal BP as supporting cultivation and measurable changes in cereal populations. Wild plants can show a similar trait at individual break points. The proportions, where the fragment was on the ear, and the other associated finds all matter.

## Chogha Golan

In the foothills of the Zagros in present-day Iran, layers accumulated one above another. The illustration shows the present-day landscape and a plan of the mound on the left. On the right, a profile shows eleven archaeological horizons. Older layers generally lie deeper. In these layers, wild barley, other grasses and pulses are visible across a long period. Researchers interpret the evidence for regular sowing of particular plants differently. Gathering from wild stands remained important.

Near the top of the sequence, around 9,800 cal BP, emmer appears with clear features of domesticated ears. Find horizon II in the illustration, one of the late archaeological horizons. The finds reveal which plants people used. How domesticated grain reached the site is a separate question.

Compare the three kinds of evidence. The structure at Dhra′ suggests how people stored food. At Tell Qarassa, fragments reveal changes in the plants themselves. The sequence of layers at Chogha Golan reveals changes across many generations.
