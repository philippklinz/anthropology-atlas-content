# Dublin on the Irish Sea

Hundreds of chips of amber lay on the floor of a house in Dublin. A single chip might have escaped notice. Scattered across one floor, they mark a place where someone worked amber. The worker's name is lost. A Dublin moneyer who lived a few decades later can still be named from a penny found in England. For some who reached this shore, all that survives is an annalist's entry recording that they were brought here as captives. Each of these traces leads to the same river, the Liffey.

## A camp by the black pool {#longphort}

The Annals of Ulster mention two camps for ships in 841, one at Linn Duachaill and another at Duiblinn. The entry names the communities exposed to raids from the latter camp, among them the people of Leinster and the Uí Néill. The same Irish word, *longport*, describes both bases. The following year the annalist adds a piece of news in a handful of words. The newcomers were still at Duiblinn.

The Poddle, a tributary of the Liffey, formed a dark pool south of the present riverfront. Its Irish name survives in the name Dublin. The other name for the settlement, Áth Cliath, refers to a ford made passable with hurdles, panels of woven rods. A crossing for people on land and sheltered water for mooring boats were two different attractions of the riverside. Archaeologists have proposed several sites for the early ship camp, including the south bank of the old pool. The annal gives no means of tracing its perimeter or finding its landing place.

## Four months at Dumbarton Rock {#dumbarton}

In 870 two leaders named Amlaíb and Ímar laid siege to the stronghold that the annals call Ail Cluaithe. Its twin summits rise above the north shore of the Clyde at Dumbarton. This was a seat of the kings of Strathclyde. Four months passed before the attackers took and plundered the stronghold. The annalist fits those months and their outcome into a single entry.

Archaeologists looking for the defences in the 1970s opened trenches on the eastern side of the rock. One exposed the bedding trench for a timber rampart face, burnt wood and stone vitrified by intense heat. Three radiocarbon dates from the burnt timbers together span the sixth to ninth centuries. There certainly was an early medieval fort here. The timbers leave us with too wide a span to say that the fire was the work of Amlaíb and Ímar.

## The people made to cross the sea {#captives}

Under 871 the two leaders reappear in Dublin. The annalist gives their fleet two hundred ships and names Angles, Britons and Picts among the people carried to Ireland in captivity. Two hundred is a reported count of ships. We have no tally of those aboard them or of how many of the captives had lived at Dumbarton. The leaders had names the annalist knew. The people with them appear only in groups. For Amlaíb and Ímar the passage was a journey home. For their captives it meant being taken away from theirs.

There is other evidence for the trade in enslaved people at Dublin. The entry for 871 takes us only as far as the landing. For any one person the prospect of sale, continued captivity or a return home remains an open question. The men holding the harbour could lose it in their turn. An army from Brega and Leinster drove the defenders from Dublin in 902. The fugitives left ships behind. Under 917 the annals name a new ruler entering Dublin, Sitriuc, grandson of Ímar.

## The amber on the workshop floor {#fishamble}

A street climbed from the river towards the ridge where Christ Church Cathedral stands today. Excavations along Fishamble Street uncovered fifteen successive levels of building. Wattle fences marked off narrow plots. As the posts rotted and fences needed replacing, new ones often went up along the old line. The edge of a neighbour's property can still be followed through several rounds of rebuilding. A common house plan had a hearth down the middle with raised spaces for sitting and sleeping at either side.

In the fourth building phase, around the middle of the tenth century, a house known to the excavators as FS 20 occupied part of Plot 2. Its amber finds suggest that someone used the house as a workshop. Among the finds on the street were lumps of raw amber and half-made pendants and beads. A finished piece of jewellery could have been made far away. A scatter of tiny flakes struck off in shaping a piece makes it possible to locate someone's work within a particular house. Plot 5 in the same building phase yielded a writing tablet, a little carved head and a coin of the English king Eadred, who reigned from 946 to 955. The coin helps place that deposit in time. The tablet tells us nothing about the language spoken by the person who used it. At [Coppergate in York](chapter:york-between-kings-and-craftspeople#coppergate), too, workshop debris has helped to identify how people earned a living.

## A hostage whose name survives {#hostages}

The Annals of Tigernach do give one Dublin captive a name. In 979 men from Dublin took Domnall Cláen, king of Leinster. The next year Máel Sechnaill, king of Tara, and Eochaid, king of the Ulaid, came to Dublin. After a siege of three days and nights, the hostages went free. The annals include cattle, treasure and relief from tribute among the terms the victors obtained. The writer even compares the captives' plight to the biblical captivity in Babylon. Máel Sechnaill emerges from this account as the deliverer of his people.

We can follow Domnall beyond his release. Just three years later, in 983, he and Máel Sechnaill were on opposite sides. Máel Sechnaill fought alongside Glún Iairn, a son of Dublin's King Amlaíb, against Domnall and Ímar of Waterford. The king who had helped to get Domnall out of captivity was now his opponent.

## Faeremin's penny {#mint}

English coins already circulated in Dublin when King Sihtric Silkbeard began striking silver pennies there in the 990s. His mint took up designs used for King Æthelred II in England. The piece shown here was found in Shropshire and is dated to about 1000–1010. It measures just under two centimetres across. A crease runs through the silver. A head in profile can still be made out on one face, a long cross on the other. The finds record attributes this penny to Faeremin, a Dublin moneyer.

Even the tools travelled between workshops. By comparing the marks they left on coins, the numismatist Mark Blackburn traced individual English dies to Dublin, one of them from York. Decades earlier Amlaíb mac Gofraid, a previous king of Dublin, had issued [a penny bearing a raven at York](chapter:york-between-kings-and-craftspeople#raven-penny). Faeremin's Dublin penny brings another man into view, one who answered for the money that left the mint.

## Two stems from the town and a longship in the fjord {#dublin}

Look for ships in Dublin and parts of them turn up in drains, paths and waterfront structures. Two stem timbers from Fishamble Street are numbered T357 and T364. Ship archaeologists drew them to the same scale as the corresponding timbers of Skuldelev 2, a longship found in Roskilde Fjord. The drawings show how the ends of the planks came together at the stem. Shipbuilders in Denmark used related forms.

The strongest evidence for eastern Ireland comes from the longship's own tree rings. The oak for its keelson, the timber supporting the mast, was felled in May or June 1042. The precise site of the shipyard is still unknown. The [surviving hull of Skuldelev 2](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#dublin) shows how long shipwrights kept it in repair. The loose timbers in Dublin tell of a different choice. An old ship could be taken to pieces and go on serving the people of the riverside long after it last floated.
