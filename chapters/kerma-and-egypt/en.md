# Kerma and Egypt

More than 3500 years ago, Kerma stood on the Nile in present-day Sudan as the capital of a Nubian kingdom. Egyptian inscriptions call the realm Kush. A large mudbrick building shows how people shaped their city. Downriver, cooking sherds from Askut and a stela from Buhen tell us about people who lived where different powers met.

## Kerma

Near the third Nile cataract, the **Western Deffufa** rises above Kerma. Its high walls are made of mudbrick. Excavators identified at least twelve successive building phases in the masonry. Around its base they uncovered a religious quarter with chapels and workshops. The excavation interprets the Deffufa as the city’s main temple.

The **c. 1750–1500 BCE** window shows the Deffufa in the later history of the kingdom. The building has a longer history. Kerma also had houses, storerooms and workshops. Seal impressions and objects with connections to Egypt, inland Africa and the Red Sea offer clues to what people stored, administered and exchanged there.

## Askut

Farther north, a fortress on Askut Island was built by Egypt during the Middle Kingdom. Excavated layers give a closer view of the people who lived there. Many cooking sherds carry traces of soot. Researchers compared their fabric, shape and manufacture. Typical vessels in the Nubian tradition were made by hand. Many Egyptian examples were made on a potter’s wheel.

In the Second Intermediate Period, dated here to **c. 1650–1550 BCE**, the team classified **28 of 46 cooking sherds** as Nubian in style. The earlier comparison was **132 of 350**. These are sherd counts. Several pieces could come from a single pot. The comparison shows how frequently Nubian cooking pottery appears in the two samples and brings daily life at the fortress into view.

## Buhen

Buhen lay farther downriver still. A photograph from 1960 shows its fortress before the reservoir flooded the site. Archaeologists found the round-topped **Stela of Sepedher**, now at the Penn Museum, near the northern temple. The hieroglyphs identify him as commandant of Buhen. Sepedher describes building a temple to Horus to the satisfaction of the ruler of Kush.

The excavators found the stela in a layer they assigned to the 18th Dynasty. Scholars date the inscription itself to the late Second Intermediate Period. The layer and the inscription can therefore speak to different moments in the history of the site.

A local office, an Egyptian god and a ruler of Kush all appear on this one stela. At Askut, pots show how people used several traditions in daily life. Together the sites reveal different kinds of contact along the same river.
