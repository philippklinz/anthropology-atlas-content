# The Stone Heads of San Lorenzo

On the Gulf Coast of what is now Mexico, Olmec sculptors carved heads of basalt taller than a person. A head in a museum shows the finished work. Rocks from the mountains and the traces of a workshop show what making it took.

## San Lorenzo

Colossal Head 1 comes from San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán in what is now the state of Veracruz. The museum in Xalapa records its height as 2.60 metres. The front view shows the headband, cheeks and large ear ornaments. From the side you can see how far back the headdress reaches. Who the head depicts and what the headdress meant at the time remain open questions.

The museum dates the object to about 1200–900 BCE. This date range refers to the sculpture. The map point locates the San Lorenzo findsite. The photograph was taken in the museum in Xalapa.

## Los Tuxtlas

Rocks can be compared. Howel Williams and Robert Heizer looked in the Tuxtla mountains for stone resembling the monuments. In the southern foothills near Cerro Cintepec and Soteapan, they found naturally rounded basalt boulders, some several metres wide. By comparing the monuments' stone with samples from the area, they used differences in the size and abundance of crystals to distinguish rock groups.

The two researchers assigned the first five colossal heads of San Lorenzo to the basalt group from this area. On the map the findsite and the source region lie several dozen kilometres apart. The paths taken by the people moving the stone remain unknown, as does how much of the block they removed before moving it.

## Llano del Jícaro

At Llano del Jícaro, traces of ancient stone carving survived. Susan Gillespie's team found nine worked boulders. One of them, Worked Stone 3, is a large slab. Stone flakes and a hammerstone battered around its edge came to light beside it. A hammerstone striking basalt can dislodge large pieces. Repeated smaller blows can then even out the surface.

Pottery and the shapes of the stones help place the activity in time. The excavation report assigns the stoneworking to the Formative and uses the broad frame of about 1150–500 BCE for the Olmec period. Sherds from later occupation survive in some parts of the site. The exact year in which the slab was worked remains unknown.

The unfinished stone shows that people shaped some monuments where suitable boulders lay. This explains how a stone source could also be a place of work. Whether the first head of San Lorenzo was worked at this particular workshop remains an open question.
