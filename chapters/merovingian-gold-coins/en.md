# Whose name was on Merovingian money?

A Frankish gold coin in Ghent University Library carries a head in profile. A string of beads crosses the forehead. The nose reaches into the lettering around the rim. Turn the coin over and you find a name. It belongs to **Madelinus**, the moneyer. The inscription leaves open whose face the die cutter meant to portray. The other side tells us where the piece was struck, at **Dorestad**. Face, jewellery and lettering fit on a disc twelve millimetres across. How far can we follow a person who left his name in so small a space?

## Three blank pieces in a purse {#sutton-hoo}

On the east coast of England in the early seventh century, mourners put a purse into a ship that had been hauled onto land for a burial. The chamber under Mound 1 at Sutton Hoo contained 37 struck gold coins, three coin-shaped gold blanks and two small ingots. The inconspicuous blanks reward a closer look. They bring the number of round pieces up to forty. Each weighed about as much as a coin. Someone putting such a collection together could make up a sum by weight even without a stamped piece of the right value. Whether this was the intention here is open to question.

The struck pieces came from a variety of mints in the Frankish kingdoms. We do not know who carried them across the sea, or when. They still tell us something about the date of the grave that the splendid purse alone could hardly establish. The latest coin had to have been struck by the time someone placed it on the ship. The British Museum puts the deposit roughly between 610 and 635. Sutton Hoo therefore appears on the map in several possible years. There was one burial.

## Another town on a moneyer's coin {#maastricht}

Cross the sea and turn south to the Meuse. In 2009 a gold coin came to light in a field near Thorn in the Netherlands. The local museum dates it to around 625. One side carries a form of the name Maastricht. On the other is Madelinus again. We know the names of twelve moneyers who struck coins at Maastricht. They worked in a town with a range of trades. People shaped antler, worked glass, fired pottery and made textiles. Neither die tells us how many combs or how much cloth a gold piece would buy. The names stamped into the metal answer a different question. They let us pick out individual makers in a town full of workshops.

## Two sides of a coin from Dorestad {#dorestad}

Take another look at the photograph. On the reverse of the Ghent coin a cross rises from a base. Below it are two rows of three pellets each. Around the rim the moneyer's name ends with an extra M. The obverse names Dorestad, near present-day Wijk bij Duurstede. In the 630s and 640s two river towns thus belong to the same story. To draw Madelinus's personal journey between them, we would need some evidence of where he stopped and when. The matching inscriptions cannot supply it. Coins carrying his name were probably struck after his death as well. A name could stay in circulation longer than its owner lived.

## A different die near Leiden {#oegstgeest}

At Oegstgeest on an old branch of the Rhine, excavators recovered a coin with a much simplified head. The cross is recognisable too, although the lettering on both sides departs from the familiar inscriptions. It weighed 1.22 grams and contained 39 per cent gold. The specialists compared its gold content with that of other specimens. They suggested a striking date around 650–660. When the coin reached the settlement is a separate question. Its manufacture therefore has no pin at Oegstgeest on the map.

Another find gives us a clock for the settlement itself. A well cut through the south wall of an earlier house. Analysis of the wood dated the well to around 672, give or take seven years. House, well and coin can each be dated in a different way. Other wells at the same site even incorporated reused ships' timbers. Follow only the gold from the coast inland and you will miss the point at which an old boat found a new use in keeping a settlement supplied with water. A wooden household object had a second life too in [a village at the edge of the Swabian Jura](chapter:life-in-a-merovingian-village).
