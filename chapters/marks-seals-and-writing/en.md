# Marks, Seals and Writing

How do you keep track of a quantity after the person who counted it has gone? You can put aside one token for each item. You can also mark the quantity and the object with signs. Objects from Susa, Uruk and Abydos let us compare these ways of recording information.

## Susa

In present-day southwestern Iran, excavators found a hollow clay ball with small clay tokens. The ball is only just over five centimetres across. The photograph shows the envelope and the tokens displayed below it at the Louvre.

The impressions on the envelope were made with an engraved cylinder rolled over soft clay. The same pattern could then be left on different objects. Look next at the small tokens. Some are rounded and others are flatter. Researchers interpret tokens in sealed clay envelopes as a way of keeping accounts. The goods associated with the tokens of this particular envelope remain uncertain. The Louvre dates the object broadly to c. 3500–2900 BCE.

## Uruk

Tablet VAT 14674 comes from Uruk in present-day Iraq. It can look large on a screen, but it is only about four centimetres high. Find four similar impressions on the left. Three signs of different shapes sit on the right. The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative catalogue distinguishes the number notation from the other signs in the entry.

Proto-cuneiform is the name for this early Mesopotamian sign system. On such tablets, people could record quantities alongside other information. CDLI dates this tablet to the Uruk IV script phase, about 3350–3200 BCE. The spoken language behind this particular entry remains undetermined. Recognising a pattern of number signs is easier than reconstructing a spoken sentence.

## Abydos

In Upper Egypt, excavators found small bone or ivory tags in Tomb U-j. Many carry short groups of incised signs. A hole allowed a tag to be attached to an object. The same tomb also preserved vessels with larger painted signs and fragments bearing seal impressions.

Some of the incised shapes resemble later hieroglyphs. Could the signs have named where a gift came from? Researchers have proposed several readings. Estimates of the burial context's age also differ. The map window spans two approximate dates from the literature, about 3320 and 3200 BCE.

Compare the way each object held information. At Susa, small tokens went inside a clay envelope that bore seal impressions. At Uruk, groups of signs shared one surface. At Abydos, signs cut into a tag could be attached to another object. The surviving surfaces and shapes show how a record could be attached, kept and recognised.
