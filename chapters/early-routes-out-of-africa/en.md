# People reach new landscapes

A jaw beside the Mediterranean. Human skeletons in caves. A finger bone beside a vanished lake. These finds place Homo sapiens outside Africa long before written records. We explore four sites between approximately 200,000 and 80,000 years ago: Misliya, Skhul, Qafzeh and Al Wusta.

## Misliya: A jaw changes the picture

Part of a human upper jaw with teeth survived on the western slopes of Mount Carmel, in present-day Israel. Anatomical comparisons with other human fossils supported assigning Misliya-1 to our species. Several dating methods together constrain its age to approximately 194,000–177,000 years.

This establishes the presence of early Homo sapiens outside Africa. The jaw does not tell us when they departed, their route, or how many generations were involved. It was found alongside stone tools, including evidence of the Levallois technique already known from African sites.

## Skhul and Qafzeh: More than isolated bones

Skhul is also on Mount Carmel; Qafzeh lies farther inland near Nazareth. Both yielded remains of several people. The introductory timeline uses the approximate synthesis ranges of 130,000–100,000 years for Skhul and 100,000–90,000 years for Qafzeh. Their detail panels explain why other dating approaches produce different or broader ranges.

Some finds are interpreted as intentional burials. They raise questions about treatment of the dead alongside questions of dispersal. Bone positions and layers provide clues, but do not reveal the people’s thoughts or religious beliefs. The cave photograph shows Skhul today, not its ancient surroundings.

## Al Wusta: A finger in the desert

Farther southeast, in present-day Saudi Arabia, researchers found a finger bone only 32.3 millimetres long. Its shape supports identification as Homo sapiens. Al Wusta now lies in the Nefud Desert, but sediments and animal fossils reveal a former freshwater lake and grassland.

This matters for dispersal: increased rainfall could make water and food available in landscapes that now seem inhospitable. The environment temporarily offered different possibilities. Wetter phases did not affect all of Arabia simultaneously or continue indefinitely.

## Two numbers, two different statements

Uranium-series analysis of the finger bone itself produced 87,600 ± 2,500 years, with uncertainty stated at 2σ. This is a minimum age. Uranium can enter bone after death, so its radioactive clock need not have started at death.

Researchers also assessed other fossils, sediments and their stratigraphic sequence. That combined model supplies the approximate 95,000–86,000-year frame used here. The figures do not contradict each other: one constrains a minimum age, while the other combines several lines of evidence. Hatching represents dating uncertainty, not a demonstrated period of continuous occupation.

## Four findspots, many unresolved journeys

The map points are evidence of presence. They are not stops on one documented journey. Many millennia separate Misliya and Al Wusta. Whether particular finds represent several dispersals or longer occupation, and how the groups were related, remains unresolved.

This chapter follows a bounded part of our species’ early dispersal. It does not claim to show every early site, or suggest that Africa then became empty of people. Later widespread expansion across Eurasia and beyond is another story.
