# Hürrem Sultan’s kitchen in Jerusalem

How much bread could a hungry person expect at the door of a charitable foundation in Jerusalem? In the sixteenth century Hürrem Sultan, wife of the Ottoman sultan Süleyman, had the portions written down. A man staying in the hostel was entitled to a loaf. The clerk who kept the foundation's records received two. Each of the four hundred poor people for whom the kitchen was meant to provide got a loaf too, but shared a bowl of soup with another person. Behind these brief directions are three different arrangements for getting through the day. To see how a manuscript could put a meal on the table, we can follow its provisions from a street in Jerusalem to the farming country around Lydda.

## Two women on the same street {#tunshuq}

In the Old City of Jerusalem a steep lane leads west from the valley floor. Late in the fourteenth century a woman lived there whose name came to be attached to the hill. According to the Jerusalem historian Mujir al-Din, Sitt Tunshuq al-Muzaffariyya had lived in the city since about 1391. Her family connections remain uncertain. When she died in 1398, she was buried across the street from her house. Someone standing in the lane today can still look from the entrance of the residence to the building over her tomb.

The house belonged to Mamluk Jerusalem. The middle of three doorways admitted visitors to a long hall. Four freestanding piers supported its vaults. A courtyard and a reception room lay upstairs. A substantial amount of roofed space was already available, then, in a house built for a woman who lived and received visitors here. In the sixteenth century Hürrem's foundation incorporated the older property into its premises. Around a century and a half separated the two women. Their buildings stand within a few paces of one another because the founders decided to reuse an existing residence. The entrance to its great hall and the working spaces of the later kitchen each have their own history.

## Who had a claim on the harvest? {#jindas}

A document from 1552 takes us out to Lydda, present-day Lod, and the neighbouring villages. Revenues from this district were assigned to support Hürrem's institutions in Jerusalem. Dues that other people had previously controlled would now have a new recipient. Consider the village of Bayt Dajan. With a complete interest in a village reckoned at twenty-four shares, the deed assigned eighteen and a third to the foundation. Anyone who stopped reading after the village name would miss the rights of all the other beneficiaries. The income from Jindās, just east of a bridge across the river now called the Ayalon, also went to several foundations.

Setting down those rights required the vocabulary of people who knew the land. Roy Marom and Ran Zadok worked from a copy made in 1927 of the Arabic deed of 1552. In it they identified 195 names for places and smaller localities. The entries include parcels of farmland as well as towns and villages. Bayt Iksā, for instance, appears alongside a *mazraʿa*, a tract of farmland named al-Kharrūba. Village and fields were some distance apart on the ground. They belonged together on the page because their dues were assessed jointly. To supply bread in Jerusalem took people who could identify plots, bring in crops and collect dues. In [Bursa's silk market](chapter:early-ottoman-bursa-and-its-markets#bursa) a charitable endowment drew money from rooms rented out to traders. Here some of the income came from working the soil.

## A book for a kitchen {#charter}

The foundation charter is kept in Istanbul under inventory number 2192. Made during Hürrem's lifetime, the manuscript is twenty-eight centimetres high. Gold ruling frames nine lines of Arabic writing to a page. At the front is Süleyman's tuğra, the interwoven strokes of the sultan's monogram. Hürrem's own seal appears at the end alongside the signatures of witnesses. The manuscript supplies its own date. The colophon gives the Islamic year 964, spanning parts of 1556 and 1557.

One can open this splendid volume to answer a practical question. Who was to look after the buildings when the founder was dead? Folios 9b to 10a name the institutions in Jerusalem. Further on, folios 12a to 14b set out expenses for staff and for maintaining and cleaning the premises. The people who gilded the pages were decorating a legal instrument to which someone could appeal about wages for ordinary work. For an older foundation in a different political setting, compare [the deed for a school in Konya](chapter:divrigi-and-the-workshops-of-konya#foundation-deed). Each document helps us find employees whose work tends to disappear behind the founder's name.

## Who ate first? {#portions}

What was cooking depended on the hour and the calendar. Morning soup called for rice, chickpeas and onions. Seasonal additions could include squash or lemon. For the evening meal the cooks prepared cracked wheat with clarified butter, chickpeas, onions and cumin. On Thursday evening, which marked the start of Friday, the menu changed. Mutton with rice came to the table, accompanied by *zerde*, a sweet rice dish made with honey and saffron. A person who came only on those evenings would have known a rather different kitchen from someone who counted on a loaf every day.

There was also an order of service. The employees ate first, followed by the people staying in the hostel. The poor came in several shifts after them. For many of the people at the door the prescribed portion of soup was half a ladle. In the charter that meant one ladle poured into a bowl to be shared between two people. Four hundred is the number of poor people the kitchen was supposed to feed. Establishing how many actually came on a given winter morning would take a different kind of record. The arrangements for [feeding guests at an Anatolian inn](chapter:roads-and-caravanserais-of-rum#karatay-deed) offer a comparison. That founder made separate provision for the travellers' animals as well.

## Cotton in court {#cotton}

Half a century later four farmers from Jindās appear in a Jerusalem court register. In December 1606 members of the Jumāq family sued the four farmers for their share of a cotton harvest. The supervisor of the Jerusalem kitchen endowment had instructed the villagers to sow cotton. He had withheld the share claimed by the other party. The family produced an older endowment deed. The defendants themselves backed the family’s claim and corrected an important figure at the same time. The actual yield had been seventy *qinṭārs* of cotton. The initial reckoning had assumed sixty of these units of weight. The judge ordered that the family receive the portion due to it.

A tax register for 1596/97 had recorded wheat, barley, sesame, goats and beehives at Jindās. The later suit brings both the cotton and the evidence of four of its growers into view. Here is another history of the bowl of soup in Jerusalem. Keeping it filled required income on which several parties had a claim. Sometimes the only people who could put a number to that claim for a judge were the ones who had brought in the harvest.
