# Across Seas and Land Bridges

How do people reach a landscape beyond the sea? What changes when the sea temporarily exposes land? This chapter visits Sahul and North America. It continues the early dispersals and overlaps with our chapters on encounters and forms of expression.

Choose one of the three stops on the map. Points mark archaeological evidence, dashed arrows possible directions of movement. Ochre marks selected former land connections. Each feature has a source; an arrow’s direction does not by itself establish an actual journey.

## Sahul

At low sea level, Australia and New Guinea formed the landmass Sahul. In front of it lay Wallacea, the island region in present-day Indonesia and Timor-Leste. Even during the ice age, not all channels disappeared: people had to cross water.

Compare the northern and southern alternatives. In the illustrated model, A leads via Sulawesi and the Moluccas towards Misool. B follows the island chain through Timor towards the northwestern Sahul shelf. These lines illustrate two possibilities discussed in a 2017 study. Their date, 65,000 BP, belongs to the model scenario, not an attested departure. Among other things, the study asks which islands might have been visible from one another. Later models also consider ocean currents.

The Madjedbebe point in present-day northern Australia stays put when you switch routes. Early tools and other traces of human activity were actually excavated here. The excavators model the start of an artifact-rich phase at approximately 65,000 years; the association between artifacts and old sediments has been scientifically debated. The site is not a demonstrated first landing place. Neither arrow therefore continues to it as a supposedly known journey.

Open the research illustration too: its blue buffers represent inter-island visibility. They do not show population size or established migration corridors.

## Beringia

Far to the north, exposed land once connected Asia and North America. Much water was stored in ice sheets and sea level was lower. Beringia was a broad landscape. Today’s narrow strait reveals only a small part of that setting.

The ochre partial area schematically marks the connection around 25,000 BP. It is not a surveyed palaeocoastline. The light basemap continues to show today’s land areas for orientation.

A land connection does not yet explain how people continued south. Large ice sheets made travel difficult. The illustrated coastal hypothesis relates to an environmental window around 24,500–22,000 BP that a 2023 study identifies as comparatively favourable. These are modelled conditions, not a measured travel duration. An open interior ice-free corridor at that time is not presented here as an equally viable alternative.

## White Sands

The map now moves to present-day New Mexico. Human footprints survived in sediment near former Lake Otero. A chronology published in 2026 dates a sequence of 20 track-bearing horizons to approximately 23,500–21,700 BP.

Look at the photograph of the impressions. People were here. Different dating methods help establish when they left their traces. The findsite alone, however, does not tell us how they reached North America. A visible gap therefore remains between the coastal arrow and White Sands.

## What does the map tell us?

Keep three questions separate while exploring: **Where is there a find? Which routes might have been possible? What did the landscape look like at a particular time?** Each requires its own evidence.

The landscape snapshots represent particular moments. A 65,000-year-old scenario does not automatically become the coastline for every later millennium. Arrow width and spacing measure neither population size nor speed. BP means years before 1950; this chapter’s shared viewing window spans 71,000–20,000 BP.
