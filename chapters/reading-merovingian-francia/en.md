# The Merovingians and the Frankish kingdoms

In June 751 a man who would soon be king heard a case at Attigny. The judgment calls him Pippin, mayor of the palace. When the scribe dated the document, he counted the years of another man, King Childeric. An abbot kept the parchment because it established who held a disputed property. His reason for keeping it is the reason we can still see how two men in different offices fitted into a single day. There are many small overlaps like this across the three centuries of the Frankish kingdoms. They are a good place to begin exploring the map.

## A seal for a king {#tournai}

The **Merovingians** were the Frankish royal family of Childeric I, his son Clovis and their descendants. They took their name from Merovech. When Gregory of Tours wrote about the family's beginnings in the sixth century, he called Merovech the father of Childeric. As for how he was related to an earlier king, Chlodio, Gregory presented one account as something some people said. For Childeric himself there is a different kind of clue on the Scheldt. A signet ring from a grave near Saint-Brice at Tournai bore his name and his royal title.

Tournai lies in present-day Belgium. It makes a good starting point for anyone used to picturing the Frankish kingdoms within the modern borders of France. The [grave at Tournai](chapter:childeric-at-tournai#burial) also contained gold coins and tiny insects with red wings. Almost the entire find was later lost in a theft. We can still see the ring in an illustration because a scholar had it drawn and published in the seventeenth century. Part of what we know of a fifth-century king hangs on what a draughtsman and printer recorded.

## Two paths from the same battlefield {#vouille}

At **Vouillé in 507**, you can change sides in the middle of a story. The [Visigothic chapter](chapter:visigoths-toulouse-to-toledo#vouille) introduces the men from the Auvergne who fought with Alaric II and the treasure Clovis later took from Toulouse. The [Frankish chapter](chapter:clovis-and-his-bishops#vouille) turns to people taken captive in the campaign. Who would vouch for the release of a prisoner whose family had little chance of being heard at court? Clovis's letter called for evidence under a bishop's seal. Both chapters open the same battle on the map. Gregory's reference to a milestone leaves several possible locations near Poitiers.

## Four brothers and four royal seats {#four-seats}

When Clovis died in 511, four sons inherited shares of his rule. Towns along with their surrounding districts could go to different brothers. When Gregory names four royal seats for the next generation, Paris and Orléans matter as much as Reims and Soissons. All four can be picked out on the map at the same time. How did families divide rights over people, dues and property as sons grew up or a brother died? The chapter on [the partitions of the Frankish kingdoms](chapter:partitioning-the-frankish-kingdoms#four-seats-561) follows the solutions successive generations arrived at.

## Three places in 587 {#andelot-poitiers}

In August Radegund was buried at Poitiers. She had established a community of women and placed herself under its elected abbess, Agnes. The women now faced the question of who would uphold their rule and protect their property after the founder's death. In November King Guntram, his nephew Childebert II and Childebert's mother Brunhild met at Andelot to settle rights within their family. For Brunhild this also involved Cahors. The town and its territory had been among the marriage gifts received by her late sister. The treaty assigned them to Brunhild.

For this one year the map shows a funeral at **Poitiers**, a meeting at **Andelot** and a town named in the agreement, **Cahors**. Brunhild could affect the lives of people at Cahors without ever setting foot there that day. You can move from [Radegund's funeral](chapter:radegund-and-poitiers#funeral) directly to [the treaty of the kings and queen](chapter:merovingian-queens-and-the-treaty#andelot-and-cahors). Gregory of Tours appears in both chapters. He attended the funeral himself. He included the text of the agreement in his histories.

## A judge from the district {#paris-edict}

When Chlothar II issued an edict at Paris in October 614, one king again ruled the formerly divided kingdoms. One of its provisions takes on a different meaning when read from the point of view of someone who had a grievance. A judge was to come from the district where he held office. If he wrongfully took something from someone, he was to make good the loss out of his own property. A claimant had good reason to know where the judge held land.

## Things happening far apart at the same time {#maastricht}

**Around 625** a Frankish king confirmed a gift to a church. His charter located the plot within the walls of Paris. On the Meuse at about the same time, we can pick out a man whose name was struck into gold. His name, Madelinus, appears on a gold coin struck at Maastricht. Other pieces carry the name together with that of Dorestad. Anyone trying to trace a journey between two names on the map soon runs up against a limit here. We can compare the inscriptions. We cannot tell what route the moneyer took between the towns. The chapter on [Frankish gold coins](chapter:merovingian-gold-coins#dorestad) lets you inspect both sides of a surviving piece side by side.

## The bed in grave 27 {#lauchheim}

In the winter of **703/704** oak was felled at Lauchheim for a burial chamber. A bed was adapted to serve as a coffin. A wedge driven into a joint suggests that the bed had already seen use in a household. Here we can say more about the age of the chamber timbers than about the person who once slept in the bed. The chapter on [the village on the Jagst](chapter:life-in-a-merovingian-village#the-bed-in-grave-27) takes you the short distance between cemetery and farmsteads on the map.

## Two royal families at Saint-Denis {#saint-denis}

Half a century earlier a king's son had made provision for his parents at Saint-Denis. In 654 Clovis II had his wish for prayers commemorating Dagobert and Nanthild set down in writing. A century later Pope Stephen II anointed Pippin and his sons Charles and Carloman at the same church. Between these occasions the name of the ruling family changed. The place connects them. You can jump from [Dagobert's burial](chapter:dagobert-and-saint-denis#saint-denis-639) to [the anointing in 754](chapter:the-last-merovingian-king#saint-denis).

## What an abbot can tell us about a change of king {#attigny}

Abbot Fulrad of Saint-Denis had reason to keep the records of two judgments. Each time his concern was for land. At [Attigny](chapter:the-last-merovingian-king#attigny-soissons) in June 751 he appeared before Pippin, mayor of the palace. At [Verberie](chapter:the-last-merovingian-king#verberie) in March 752 he met the same man. This time Pippin bore the royal title. In the first judgment a reader had to reach the dating clause at the end to find the king’s name. At Verberie Pippin’s royal title stood at the head of the judgment.
