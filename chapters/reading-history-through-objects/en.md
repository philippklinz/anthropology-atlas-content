# The lives behind historical objects

In the Roskilde Fjord, archaeologists uncovered a long oak timber that had once supported a ship’s mast. In one place its outermost growth ring survived. When researchers compared its rings with those from other oaks, the trail led across the Irish Sea to the Dublin region. The timber gives us neither the name of the shipwright nor that of anyone who pulled an oar. Yet it is possible to date the spring when someone took an axe to the tree to the year 1042.

## A piece of wood, a scrap of writing

In a Cornish church someone found space in the margin of a Gospel book to record [the manumission of a woman and her son](chapter:kings-and-households-in-england#bodmin). A community could read from a book in church one day, and a family could later call on a note in its pages as evidence of their freedom. In a very different setting, a [Jewish community in Fustat](chapter:cities-and-courts-of-the-caliphates#fustat) preserved letters and inventories. There we meet a teacher who had to explain to a father how his child’s writing board came to be broken.

A charter concerning [the stone bridge at Regensburg](chapter:towns-and-rulers-of-the-empire#regensburg) names the man who looked after the crossing. At [Nishapur](chapter:cities-and-courts-of-iran#nishapur), excavations in a residential quarter turned up a turquoise bowl. Its glaze is still there to examine. The people who ate from it have left no names on its surface. It is worth moving between a written record and something made for everyday use. Both the bridge charter and the bowl take us into the life of a town.

An envoy could also record things for which a treaty had little room. At [the court at Karakorum](chapter:routes-across-the-mongol-world#karakorum) a traveller’s account introduces a silversmith. In the Balkans, another embassy passed through the ruins of a city. That is where the seven stops on the map begin.

## What an envoy saw on the road {#naissus}

In 449 [an embassy to Attila’s court](chapter:embassies-and-hunnic-power#naissus) passed through Naissus, modern Niš. Priscus travelled with a Roman embassy. His account records the effects of war that the party encountered along the way. The town through which they passed can be placed on the map with greater confidence than the royal residence at their destination. Turn back to the [Danube crossing in the Gothic series](chapter:danube-crossing-and-adrianople#lower-danube) and the date changes too. The crossing of 376 took place long before the envoys were on their way to Attila. A further reading follows the [Vandals](chapter:vandals-rhine-to-carthage) as far as Carthage.

## Who could read a picture? {#palenque}

At [Palenque](chapter:maya-writers-and-cities#pakal) an inscription preserved the date on which Pakal took power. A royal name and a date can bring one individual into remarkably clear focus. The people who cultivated the fields of the court or prepared maize in a kitchen seldom receive the same attention in such texts. This is a good moment to move to another site in the same chapter. At [Joya de Cerén](chapter:maya-writers-and-cities#ceren), volcanic ash preserved traces of houses and work. Two kinds of evidence open onto different lives on the same map.

## A price someone could check {#frankfurt}

How does a baker enter the chapter about the Franks? An assembly at Frankfurt in 794 prescribed how many loaves of a given weight were to sell for a denarius. It set different rates for different grains. Before asking who was crowned emperor, the [chapter on the Frankish court](chapter:from-the-merovingians-to-aachen#frankfurt) lets readers consider what a denarius meant to someone who wanted to buy rye bread.

## What a tree ring tells us about the Irish Sea {#dublin}

Near Roskilde, archaeologists recovered the remains of several ships. The long vessel known as Skuldelev 2 contained timber whose tree rings closely match sequences from Irish oaks. One piece even retains the outer ring that was still growing when the oak was cut. That makes 1042 a felling date. The yard where the shipwrights launched the vessel is harder to locate. The [Irish chapter](chapter:iona-and-the-gaelic-seas#dublin) considers sites around Dublin. In the [Viking chapter](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#dublin), the same timber leads back to its eventual discovery in the fjord.

## The cargo of a sunken ship {#sinan}

A trading vessel on passage in 1323 sank off the Korean coast. Finds from the wreck near Sinan supply information unlikely to have survived in a royal decree. Wooden tags carry dates or clues to the people for whom goods were intended. The [Chinese port of Qingyuan](chapter:cities-and-records-in-medieval-china#qingyuan) was a point of departure, [Sinan](chapter:goryeo-and-its-workshops#sinan) the findspot. The name of a [temple community in Japan](chapter:court-and-coast-in-medieval-japan#tofukuji) leads to people expecting part of the cargo. It never reached them. The interrupted voyage gives us a way into three chapters.

## One church, two histories {#constantinople}

A building can outlast more than one chapter. In 1453, after the Ottomans took Constantinople, the use of Hagia Sophia changed. The [Byzantine chapter](chapter:lives-in-the-byzantine-empire#hagia-sophia-1453) reaches the building through the history of the city. Its [Ottoman counterpart](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#hagia-sophia-1453) opens on the same place and date. A change of ruler, the transformation of a place of worship and the several names by which a city is known can each be considered in turn.

## Who heard about the meeting on the causeway? {#tenochtitlan}

In November 1519 Moteuczoma and Cortés met on the approach to Tenochtitlan. Cortés wrote about the meeting to his king. Decades later, Nahua authors working in quite different circumstances recorded their account. Who speaks? Who interprets? Who is expected to read the story? With those questions in mind, move between [Tenochtitlan](chapter:life-in-the-mexica-capital#causeway-1519) and the [Iberian chapter](chapter:kingdoms-and-crossings-of-iberia#causeway-1519). The map marks the southern approach to the city. The precise words that passed between people on the causeway remain elusive.
