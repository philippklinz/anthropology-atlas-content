# The last Merovingian king

Two men appear on a sheet of parchment from June 751. At first sight they might seem to be competing for the same place. At the top, Pippin sits in the palace at Attigny hearing a dispute. At the bottom, the scribe dates his judgment to the ninth year of King Childeric. That made sense to the scribe. Decades later, a historian setting out to explain how Pippin’s son Charlemagne came to power would find rather more to say about it. Between one document and the next we meet two women who made decisions about land they owned, an abbot who pleaded cases before different courts, and a king whose successors had much more to say about him than he left us himself.

## Grimhild calls for a charter {#wissembourg}

In 743 Grimhild gave the monastery of Wissembourg in Alsace property near Weitbruch and Herrlisheim. She gave the welfare of her soul as her reason. In the surviving copy her own name heads the witness list. The entry records who asked for the transaction to be put in writing. The list includes no husband granting Grimhild permission to make the gift. The monks could henceforth invoke her decision. The charter has little to say about her family circumstances. On the question of whose wishes counted in the transfer of this property it gives a remarkably clear answer.

At the end, the dating clause counts the years from the time when two brothers, Carloman and Pippin, the sons of Charles Martel, took power. Members of their family had already gained considerable influence at the Frankish court as mayors of the palace in the late seventh century. The office existed under Merovingian kings. In 743 the brothers themselves supported the installation of another king from that dynasty, Childeric III.

## Two names in the decisions of Soissons {#soissons}

A year later Pippin opens a very different document, the decisions of an assembly at Soissons. He speaks of deliberations with bishops and leading Franks and styles himself duke and prince. The date falls in King Childeric’s second year. Here both men fit into a single sentence.

## What the abbot wanted at Attigny {#attigny-soissons}

On 20 June 751 the property at issue was Corbery. Abbot Fulrad of Saint-Denis claimed it for his monastery. The other party was another religious house. A man called Legitemus represented Ragana, abbess of Sept-Meules. Fulrad could point to a gift made by a married couple. The court examined the earlier documents and awarded the property to Saint-Denis. The original parchment recording the judgment survives. Its opening line identifies Pippin as mayor of the palace. Its last names the king. A reader intent on royal titles alone might miss the reason anyone kept this sheet in the first place. The abbey could produce it again if someone else laid claim to the property.

## An elevation at Soissons?

The entries in the **Royal Frankish Annals** covering this period were composed around 790, close to the court of Pippin’s son Charles. In their telling, envoys asked Pope Zacharias who deserved the title of king. The pope chose the man who wielded power. The Franks raised Pippin to the kingship at Soissons, the annalist says, and Boniface anointed him. Childeric had his hair cut and was sent to a monastery. Even the year, however, presents a difficulty. The annals put the elevation under 750, although Pippin was still hearing cases as mayor in June 751. The map therefore marks Soissons under the conventional year 751 as the location given by that later narrative. The day of the elevation and Boniface’s part in it remain open questions.

## Pippin gives judgment as king {#verberie}

On 1 March 752 we meet Fulrad again, this time at Verberie on the Oise. Once again the question was ownership. A woman named Loba had given Saint-Denis an estate at Avezé, among other property. Her son Gislemar still held it. In court he acknowledged his mother’s gift. The written account even preserves a small gesture. Gislemar relinquished his claim by means of a stalk. At the head of the judgment stands Pippin’s new title, **rex Francorum**, king of the Franks. The king had changed between the two hearings. The abbot could still argue his case on the strength of transactions made under the Merovingians.

## Whom the pope anointed at Saint-Denis {#saint-denis}

In 754 Pope Stephen II anointed Pippin and his two sons, Charles and Carloman, at [Saint-Denis, where Dagobert lay buried](chapter:dagobert-and-saint-denis#saint-denis-639). Papal letters also recall this anointing. Stephen was seeking Frankish help against the Lombard king in Italy. For Pippin’s sons the ceremony meant that a pope publicly associated them with the kingship in their father’s lifetime. Pippin had been using the royal title for years by then.

The miniature in the **Grandes Chroniques de France** was made around 1274, over five centuries later. At the top Pippin kneels while a pope places a crown on his head. Art historian Anne D. Hedeman identifies this as Stephen II at Saint-Denis. Below, a man stands before three monks. Hedeman identifies him as the deposed Childeric III. Amédée Boinet and François Garnier see Pippin’s brother Carloman, who had entered monastic life before the change of dynasty. The two scenes bring together events from different years.

When Einhard wrote his life of Charles after the king’s death, he pictured the old kings travelling to the assembly in an ox-drawn cart. Even their replies to envoys, he wrote, were dictated by their mayors. Einhard knew Charles personally. He had yet to be born when Childeric was removed. His cart tells us a good deal about the way one of Charles’s intimates wanted posterity to remember the family. To see what Childeric’s reign looked like in everyday business, it is worth taking another look at the bottom of Fulrad’s parchment.
