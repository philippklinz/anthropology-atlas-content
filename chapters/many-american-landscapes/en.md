# The Americas: Many Paths, Many Environments

How do people make a living in a landscape they are still getting to know? A sheltered cave, a pond edge and an open plain each offer different possibilities. The things that survive show which of those possibilities people used.

This chapter compares three sites between about 14,700 and 13,800 years BP. On the map you can jump from Oregon through Florida to the Argentinian Pampas. Each point represents a separate archaeological context. When and how people first entered the Americas remains a separate question.

## Paisley

In the Summer Lake basin of present-day Oregon, caves open in a rocky hillside. The photograph shows a cave entrance and its setting in 2002. The dry deposits also preserved materials that quickly decay elsewhere.

A plant fibre fragment, probably bulrush, has twists and impressions consistent with twining. It could have been part of a basket or mat. The fragment's calibrated date is **14,660 to 14,080 BP**. This range expresses age uncertainty. To make such an object, people had to recognise useful plants, prepare their fibres and connect them. The exact process remains open.

The caves also yielded coprolites, or preserved faeces. Researchers compared their fat-derived compounds and identified three early samples as human. Other samples showed animal signatures or remained ambiguous. Different methods help researchers resolve which traces people left behind.

## Page-Ladson

In present-day Florida, researchers recovered a stone tool worked on both faces from a submerged layer. Seven radiocarbon dates from immediately around the find place its context at about **14,550 BP**. The spot was then a periodically accessible pond margin.

Researchers interpreted marks on a mastodon tusk as traces of human processing. People may have hunted the animal or used a carcass they found. Divers recover the finds today. The site shows how much the archaeological record we can see depends on where and how we look.

## Arroyo Seco 2

Near Tres Arroyos in the Argentinian Pampas, researchers excavated stone tools and bones of large animals. Look at the figure of a radius from an extinct horse. The enlargements show notches and flakes missing from opposite sides.

The study places this bone at **14,147 to 13,814 BP**. The measurement tells us when the animal lived. The researchers interpret the fracture shape, opposing notches and archaeological context as evidence that people broke the bone. They also consider other causes, including carnivore damage. The exact sequence of hunting or processing remains unknown. Human burials at the same site are substantially later.

## Three places, different kinds of evidence

Compare the three stations. At Paisley the fibre artefact itself was dated, at Page-Ladson material alongside the stone, and at Arroyo Seco the bone bearing the fracture traces. In each case the measured age answers a different question first.

**BP** means calibrated calendar years before 1950 here. Sources also often report the measured radiocarbon age. The basemap shows today's coastlines and countries for orientation.
