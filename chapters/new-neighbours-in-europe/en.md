# New Neighbours, New Villages

At Lepenski Vir, teeth record childhoods spent in different places. South of Vienna, marks in the soil trace the outlines of long houses. At Lake Constance, a fire preserved stores of grain. These places are far apart and belong to different centuries. Together they show what different kinds of evidence can tell us about neighbours and village life.

## Lepenski Vir

On a terrace in the Danube's Iron Gates, people built houses with trapezoidal floors and stone-lined hearths. The photograph shows the reddish surfaces under their modern shelter. When the Danube was dammed, much of the excavated settlement was moved to higher ground. The distinctive building phase falls approximately in the late seventh and early sixth millennia BCE.

Was the terrace a meeting place for people who had grown up in different areas? Researchers compared strontium ratios in tooth enamel with values typical of the Danube Gorges. Enamel forms during childhood. The food and water people consume carry strontium from their surroundings. Five people buried at Lepenski Vir during the transition phase had ratios outside the assumed regional range. This suggests that they grew up elsewhere. Their exact childhood homes and the communities they identified with remain unknown.

Fish remained important even after domestic animals were introduced. This affects dating. Older carbon from the river can reach human tissue through fish. Without a correction, radiocarbon measurements of these people's bones can make them appear older than they are.

## Brunn-Wolfholz

More than 600 kilometres to the northwest lies Brunn am Gebirge, near Vienna. The oldest part of site 2 dates to approximately 5670–5450 BCE. Archaeologists place it in an early phase of the Linear Pottery tradition, known as LBK. Coarse pots are especially noticeable here, with shapes that resemble examples from farther southeast in Central Europe.

How do you recognise a timber house after the wood has decayed? Excavators found long trenches beside former walls. Traces of posts also survived in better-preserved parts of the settlement. The distances between features reveal the scale. Many houses across the site complex were about 20 metres long and seven to eight metres wide. The evidence tells us less about wall height or roof shape. Imagine walking along the long side of a house. What traces of that route would still be visible in the ground after thousands of years?

## Hornstaad

Much later, between 3918 and 3902 BCE, houses stood beside Lake Constance at Hornstaad-Hörnle IA. Preserved timber makes such a fine chronology possible. Researchers match patterns of wide and narrow annual growth rings. The legend in the plan shows the dates attributed to building remains. Some house plans survive only in part.

In 3910 BCE a fire swept through most of the settlement. The burnt layer contained charred ears and grains, including wheat and barley. They offer a rare glimpse of stores after a harvest. The site also yielded fishing equipment. It is harder to tell who worked together on houses, in the fields or by the lake. Comparing the three sites helps because each preserves a different kind of evidence about daily life.
