# Living with Animals

Hunters look for animals where they live. People who keep animals have to provide water, food and space. When did this change begin? Three sites show how researchers investigate relationships between humans and animals through soil, bones and pottery fragments.

## Aşıklı Höyük

At Aşıklı Höyük in central Anatolia, successive layers of a settlement have formed a mound. Researchers found compacted animal dung in some of those layers. Its structure suggests that animals trampled it in place. Some deposits were in areas interpreted as enclosures. This provides a clue to animal keeping in places where little of a fence has survived.

In a companion study, the team measured water-soluble substances in the soil, including sodium, chloride and nitrate. The distribution of these salts and measurements of nitrogen suggest that much of the material came from urine. The amounts rose particularly between the earliest layers and Level 3. Humans also left urine, and using dung as fuel could spread its components around the site. The salts, dung deposits, possible enclosures and animal bones together suggest that people kept sheep and goats increasingly intensively.

The layers studied span approximately 8,450 to 7,350 BCE. Comparing them shows how animal keeping changed over many generations at this particular settlement.

## Ganj Dareh

In the Zagros Mountains of what is now western Iran, researchers studied goat bones from Ganj Dareh. Dated settlement layers span about 8,200 to 7,600 BCE. Bone development and size can help estimate age and sex in some cases. In the assemblage, males often died younger than females. That pattern fits a strategy of keeping a few males and more females for breeding.

Many bones and horns still had features seen in wild goats. DNA showed a more detailed picture. Many animals sampled at Ganj Dareh and nearby Tepe Abdul Hosein were genetically closer to later domestic goats, while some were closer to wild goats. These findings are consistent with people keeping herds while continuing to use wild animals. Bone shape, herd composition and genetic ancestry can change at different rates.

## Takarkori

Several millennia later, the Takarkori rock shelter in what is now southwestern Libya gives us a glimpse of milk use. Fat residues survived within the fabric of some pottery fragments. Researchers measured the ratios of two carbon isotopes in fatty acids and compared the results with modern reference fats. Some fragments showed patterns consistent with dairy fat from ruminants. Other patterns were closer to body fats or other animal residues.

Many of these samples come from Middle Pastoral layers. The timeline shows the study's dated context of approximately 5,210 to 3,660 BCE. The fats show that people used pottery to process milk or milk products. The measurements alone provide little basis for identifying a specific preparation or deciding which animal produced the milk in any one pot. ## Comparing the evidence

Dung and salts help us identify where animals spent time. Bones and DNA reveal patterns of herd management and ancestry. Fat residues show what people did with animal products. As you explore each site, consider how the find, measurement and interpretation fit together. The three locations show different ways of living with animals across several thousand years.
