# What an empress gave away and a warrior wanted remembered

A woman gives a temple the favourite possessions of her late husband. With the objects she hands over an inventory. Five centuries later a man commissions a pair of picture scrolls. He fought on Kyūshū against fleets sent by the Mongol ruler and wants it known who witnessed his actions. Each left a source for the history of Japan. An inventory addressed to a temple, a diary kept by a woman at court and the recollections of a warrior give names and leave names out for quite different reasons.

## Kōmyō parts with a lute {#nara}

In 756, forty-nine days after her husband Shōmu died, the dowager empress Kōmyō gave around 650 objects to the Great Buddha at Tōdai-ji in Nara. She hoped for the dead emperor’s rebirth in the Pure Land. Open the inventory and the occasion and intention of the gift come first. Following them are the things the emperor had once handled or looked at. It was possible to set down a widow’s memories of her husband and record a major public ceremony on the same sheet.

Among the surviving pieces is a five-stringed biwa, a lute inlaid with mother-of-pearl. A richly decorated board for the game of go survives too. It would have been easy to keep a celebrated lute alive in a story. Keeping the instrument itself took people who organised its safekeeping over many generations. The wooden storehouse at the Shōsōin was closed with an official seal. Opening the door was an act for which somebody had to take responsibility. The objects are kept in newer repositories today. The old storehouse still stands, and a reader can unroll the inventory of 756 in photographs.

## A book gets away from its author {#tsuchimikado}

In 1008 Empress Shōshi gave birth to a son in Heian-kyō, present-day Kyoto. She was staying at the residence of her father, Fujiwara no Michinaga. Among her attendants was the writer now known as Murasaki Shikibu, author of The Tale of Genji. Her diary catches a moment in the life of a book that is easily missed when reading a finished novel. The women in Shōshi’s household were putting books together. They selected papers and arranged for people to copy the text in suitable hands.

Michinaga sent fine paper, brushes, ink and an inkstone. His interest also caused the author some distress. Murasaki had asked for a working copy of her tale to be brought from home. Michinaga helped himself to it from her room. She worried that people would judge her work by an unfinished text circulating elsewhere. In a world of manuscript copies it could take just one eager reader to determine which version would be copied next. No manuscript of Genji in Murasaki's own hand survives. We do have her account of how it felt to lose control of one particular copy.

## Reading the burnt earth at Hakozaki {#hakozaki}

In 1274 troops from the Yuan empire and Goryeo reached Hakata Bay on Kyūshū. Fighting took place around Torikai. Hakata itself and neighbouring Hakozaki were caught up in it, and an account records the burning of Hakozaki shrine. Archaeologists excavating in Hakozaki found burnt earth and pottery bearing the marks of intense heat. The deposits can be dated between the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. A fire in 1274 would fit. The sherds themselves carry no year.

Takezaki Suenaga, a warrior from Higo Province, needed his service in battle to be recognised. He travelled to Kamakura to press his claim to a reward and subsequently commissioned artists to record his deeds in pictures accompanied by text. The scrolls include his exploits during the second invasion in 1281. What may look like a rare chance to inspect two armies in detail began as a work in which a patron took pains to make his own actions visible. Some passages have since been shuffled, others have disappeared. Suenaga's recollections and the scorched pottery at Hakozaki take us to the same coast by very different means. For the work that went into getting the fleet across the water, turn to [Happo, the port from which it sailed](chapter:routes-across-the-mongol-world#happo).

## A temple name goes to sea {#tofukuji}

In 1323 Hakata was the probable destination of a trading vessel from Qingyuan. Religious establishments had their names on some of its cargo. One name points inland to Kyoto and the Zen temple of Tōfuku-ji. A fire in 1319 had left the buildings badly damaged. Raising money and obtaining goods for rebuilding may have been among the purposes of the voyage. The name of the temple can still be read on a wooden tag. The proceeds that someone might have hoped to raise from the sale of those goods have to be inferred.

Hakozaki shrine also appears on the tags. A generation after the fires of 1274 the shrine had a stake in a commercial venture across the sea. The ship sank on its way to Japan. The point at Kyoto identifies the institution for which some of the goods were intended. A bronze weight helps identify [the likely port of departure](chapter:cities-and-records-in-medieval-china#qingyuan). The tags themselves came to light at [the wreck site off Sinan](chapter:goryeo-and-its-workshops#sinan).
