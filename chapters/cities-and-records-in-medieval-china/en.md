# A bridge for Quanzhou, twenty bowls for Kaifeng

A waiter remembered what everyone at his tables had ordered and carried the bowls out along one arm. Far to the south, builders shifted granite beams that no human shoulder could bear. We know something of both accomplishments because two cities of the Song empire preserved memories of the work. Kaifeng had a former resident who missed the restaurants of his youth and wrote about the people in them. Quanzhou has a bridge that still shows how much the trade of a port could depend on getting across a single river. The things that kept one city running were quite different from those that preoccupied the other.

## When could the boat slip out from under the stone? {#luoyang}

A traveller going north from the port of Quanzhou into Fujian once had to take the ferry at Wan’an. The crossing was near the mouth of the Luoyang River. Anyone who proposed replacing the ferry with a bridge had to find a way to plant piers in tidal water. Builders laid stones along the riverbed to make a continuous foundation. The pointed ends of the piers divided the current. Long beams of granite laid across them look, from above, rather like wooden planks.

Local accounts explain how the builders could have lifted those beams. They brought the stone in on boats at high water and manoeuvred it into position above the piers. As the water ebbed, the stone settled onto its supports and the relieved boats could slip away. The crossing was completed in 1059 after six years of work. A temple at its southern end commemorates Cai Xiang, the official who directed the project. The bridge also recalls the judgement of people less conspicuously remembered by name. Somebody had to know how much water would lift a laden boat high enough and how it would behave in the current at that moment.

## Carrying a tableful of orders in your head {#kaifeng}

Meng Yuanlao arrived with his family in Kaifeng, the Eastern Capital, in 1103. The city he would later describe had imperial entertainments, but it also had ginger noodles, roast mutton and cold noodle dishes. His account of eating out follows an order from the table to the kitchens. Each guest might want something slightly different. A man went from table to table with chopsticks and paper to collect the requests. The server who received them stood by the kitchens and called the dishes in order, loud enough for the cooks to distinguish his voice among the others.

Meng credited these men with carrying three bowls in the left hand and as many as twenty up the right arm, from wrist to shoulder. There is a sharp observation in the diner's admiration. A feat of balance counted for little if the wrong person received the bowl. A dissatisfied customer could complain. A worker might suffer a deduction from his pay or lose his job. We have none of their names from this passage, but a remarkably good idea of the kind of memory the work required.

Meng went south after the Jurchen conquest. He began compiling his recollections when younger people could scarcely believe what the old capital had been like. There were districts where he had to rely on what others told him. The date on the map marks his arrival in Kaifeng. No date survives for the particular evening on which somebody watched a waiter carry twenty bowls.

## A calendar cut into a hillside {#jiuri}

The earliest surviving Song inscription about prayers for favourable winds on Mount Jiuri near Quanzhou dates to 1174. The last was cut in 1266. Six record prayers for departures in winter, three for ships returning in summer, and one mentions both occasions. Their timing makes sense at sea. The monsoon changed direction with the season. Even a merchant whose goods were packed and ready could have a long wait before a ship could leave.

The people named at these rites included officials in charge of overseas trade. Quanzhou had had its own maritime trade office since 1087. A man who conducted official business by the river could also go up the hill to pray to a deity for a safe voyage. We can read the officials’ names on the rock. For the families in the port, what mattered each summer was the return of particular sailors. The inscriptions keep no corresponding list of those who came home.

## A port name on a weight {#qingyuan}

In 1323 China was under the Yuan dynasty founded by the Mongols. Divers working off the Korean coast recovered a small bronze weight from this period. It bears the name Qingyuan, the jurisdiction that included present-day Ningbo. The ship also carried copper coins, ceramics from several kiln regions and wooden cargo tags. Some of the tags record the third year of the Zhizhi era, which dates the loading of goods to 1323.

How far does a name on a weight take us? The weight could have changed hands or gone on earlier voyages. Researchers therefore compared the origins, proportions and shapes of the pottery on the ship with contemporary finds in three Chinese cities. Ningbo was a closer match than Fuzhou or Hangzhou. The point on the map marks the most likely departure port. Hakata in Japan was its likely destination. Its actual journey ended [at the wreck site off Sinan](chapter:goryeo-and-its-workshops#sinan). A temple name on the wooden tags even lets us take up the story [with a customer in Kyoto](chapter:court-and-coast-in-medieval-japan#tofukuji).
