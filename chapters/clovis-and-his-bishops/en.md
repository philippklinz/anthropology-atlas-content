# Clovis and his bishops

Anyone looking for a relative carried off during the war against the Visigoths might come to depend on a man with a signet ring. In a letter to the bishops, the Frankish king Clovis set out what they could do for certain captives. A bishop who knew that someone had been taken while under the king’s protection was to send the court a letter authenticated with his ring. He was to vouch before God for the truth of what he wrote. That procedure has left us a curious kind of evidence. We can still read what the king was prepared to accept as proof. His letter never tells us how many people made it home that way.

## What a bishop expected of a king

Remigius of Reims had advice of his own for Clovis. A stranger who came before the king should feel welcome in his presence. He asked him to look after widows and orphans and to spend some of his inherited wealth on ransoming captives. Set that request alongside the precious objects found in [the grave of Clovis’s father Childeric](chapter:childeric-at-tournai). Someone who possessed gold could display it, give it away or spend it on a person’s freedom. For Remigius, making such decisions was part of a king’s work. So was something no treasure could buy. Clovis ought to know how to joke with the young and talk things over with the old.

When Remigius gave this advice remains a matter of debate. The difficulty begins in the opening lines of the surviving Latin manuscript. Many readers understood the bishop to be congratulating Clovis on taking over the Roman province of Belgica Secunda. Others have connected his words with a military undertaking. It would be a precarious business to draw the boundaries of a kingdom from that line. Remigius’s request that the king make himself available to people coming to his court is altogether clearer.

## Christmas, but in which year? {#baptism}

A second voice comes from the Rhône. Avitus, bishop of Vienne, wrote to congratulate Clovis on his baptism. He had missed the ceremony himself. In his letter it was the church calendar that mattered. Christians celebrated the birth of Christ at Christmas, and on that same feast, he wrote, the king had begun a new life through baptism. Avitus gave no year. Decades later Gregory of Tours told the story through Clovis’s wife Clotilda, who wanted him to share her faith. In Gregory’s account the king promised to be baptised if Christ helped him in a battle against the Alamanni. As the king approached the font, Gregory invited his readers to see in him another emperor Constantine.

The familiar date of 496 rests on a reconstruction of Gregory’s account. The historian Danuta Shanzer proposed Christmas 508 instead. She suggested that the captives whose release Avitus welcomes had been taken in the war of 507. His letter gives no names with which to establish that link. Whether Clovis had already been baptised when he went to war against Alaric therefore depends on an open question about the sources. At Reims, archaeologists found the remains of a baptistery under the cathedral. The building tells us where people received baptism. Archaeology cannot supply the name of an individual baptised there.

## Near Vouillé and after the battle {#vouille}

In 507 Clovis defeated Alaric II near Poitiers. Alaric died in the battle. In [From Toulouse to Toledo](chapter:visigoths-toulouse-to-toledo#vouille) you can meet the men from the Auvergne who fought alongside him on this same ground. For the Frankish king the problems of conquest began as soon as his own army was on the move. Gregory tells of a soldier who took hay from a poor man. Clovis had allowed the troops to take grass for fodder and water. The soldier reasoned that hay was grass and, in Gregory’s story, the king killed him for it. This was how the bishop of Tours conveyed the seriousness of Clovis’s undertaking to protect the country of his own saint, Martin.

Clovis’s letter turns from what the soldiers could seize to whom they might take. He recalls his order to spare, among others, clerics, certain widows and the people enslaved by churches. When a case was disputed, the crown needed a bishop who knew the people involved and could put his seal to a letter. Even a victorious king could hardly tell from his court which household someone had belonged to before the war.

## Gold in Tours {#tours}

In 508 public recognition of the victor took a different form in Tours. Gregory writes that the emperor Anastasius sent Clovis documents concerning the consulship from the East. The Frankish king wore purple and a diadem in the basilica of Saint Martin. Then he rode through the city, distributing gold and silver himself. The precise rank the emperor conferred is still debated. Those who stood beside the road could at least see whose hand the gifts came from.

## The bishops at Orléans {#orleans}

Four years after the battle Clovis summoned bishops to Orléans. At the council in 511 was Cyprian of Bordeaux. He had already met other bishops at Agde in 506, when it was Alaric II who had authorised their assembly. Five others can be traced at both councils. Their experience travelled with them through the change of rulers. The bishops at Orléans even laid down what churches should do with the revenues from royal donations. Ransoming captives was among the uses they listed. Remigius had looked to the king’s personal wealth to pay such ransoms. At Orléans the Church committed part of its own income to that purpose.
