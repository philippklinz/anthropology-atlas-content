# York between the mint and the workshop

On a woollen sock found in York, you can still see where the ball of someone’s foot wore through the fabric. Somebody had sewn on a patch. The stitches remain, even though the patch itself has gone. We have no name for the wearer or the person who mended it, and no way of knowing what language they spoke at home. For a contemporary called Æthelferth the evidence runs the other way. His name is on silver pennies made in York, but we cannot point to the street where he worked. Between the sock and the penny lies the history of a town whose inhabitants saw a succession of kings come and go within a few generations.

## Fighting on both sides of the walls {#york-wall}

An army moved north from East Anglia and reached York in the autumn of 866. The town stood on the Ouse near its confluence with the Foss. Northumbrian kings had already made it a centre of power, and it was the seat of an archbishop. In March 867 two northern kings, Osberht and Ælle, fought for possession of it. According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, some of their men broke through the defences. People died on both sides of the walls. Both kings were killed, and the survivors made peace with the army.

The chronicler had a point to make about the two men. In his account the Northumbrians had deposed Osberht and accepted Ælle despite his lack of a hereditary claim. A later writer at Durham understood the two kings to have been brothers. Even the timing of their quarrel is harder to establish than the outcome of the fighting. David Rollason observes that a chronicler writing in Wessex had good reason to portray events in the north as a lesson in the dangers of civil strife. By the time a reader gets to the deaths of the two kings, the chronicler has already offered a judgment on whose kingship was legitimate.

## Four backyards and a mended sock {#coppergate}

At 16–22 Coppergate, archaeologists uncovered four narrow properties with their ends to the street. Their boundaries were taking shape about 900. Post-and-wattle houses later stood at the front. Behind them, paths of wattle or planks led towards garden plots, rubbish pits and cesspits on the slope towards the Foss. The wet ground excluded much of the oxygen that ordinarily helps wood, cloth and plant remains to decay. One backyard yielded the woollen sock now catalogued as find 1309. It came from a deposit dating to roughly 930/935–975.

The person who made it began at the toe, pulling woollen yarn through loops already formed using a single needle. The lines of work turn back several times to shape the heel. Madder coloured the band around the ankle red. Beneath the ball of the foot the wool had worn thin and felted. A line of sewing marked the edge of what had probably been a rectangular patch. The patch may have been made of a plant fibre that decayed in the ground. What survived was the thread that once held it in place.

Iron needles clustered in the building on plot C. Some may have been made there. An unused offcut of silk also suggests that people were cutting cloth in the building. Different kinds of evidence would lead to different conclusions if each were found in isolation. At [a beadmaker's workshop in Ribe](chapter:ribe-and-hedeby#beadmakers), production waste poses a similar question. For another port where archaeologists could follow property boundaries from house to house, turn to [Fishamble Street in Dublin](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#fishamble).

## A king on one face, a moneyer on the other {#raven-penny}

In 939 the English king Æthelstan died. Amlaíb mac Gofraid, king of Dublin, gained York, where his name appeared on coins in the form Anlaf. A silver penny in the Cambridge coin register weighs 1.17 grams. On the front a bird stretches out its wings and turns its head to one side. Numismatists call it the Raven type. The surrounding inscription gives Anlaf the title of king. Turn the penny over and a small cross occupies the centre. The inscription around it names Æthelferth, the moneyer. A craftsman's name travelled with the king's, reaching people neither man would ever meet.

What might someone in York have seen in the bird? Ravens were associated with Odin in Norse tradition. They also figured in stories about Oswald, a Northumbrian king venerated as a saint. The bird is a poor guide to the beliefs of whoever accepted the coin in payment. Even the royal name has to be read with care. When Amlaíb mac Gofraid died in 941 his cousin, also called Amlaíb, succeeded him. The Raven issue may have continued for a time under the second king. A little over half a century later the names of kings and moneyers began appearing on silver struck in [Dublin's own mint](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#mint).

## Who could expel a king in 954? {#edred}

For another change of ruler the Chronicle offers very few words. Its annal for 954 says that the Northumbrians drove out Erik and that King Eadred took over the government of the Northumbrians. Two years before, it had credited the Northumbrians with accepting Erik as king. Northerners appear as political actors in both entries. The writer supplies no list of the households that took part and no account of how the decision was enforced. Other entries mention Northumbrian councillors, giving us a glimpse of powerful people who could negotiate with a king.

The post-and-wattle houses at Coppergate were occupied across this period. No excavated floor can be assigned to the day Erik was expelled. People dropped things into pits, filled them in and dug up older material again when they put up the next building. An annal can tell us which king lost power in a particular year. The succession of deposits tells us what happened to a small patch of ground over several lifetimes.

## The threshold of a house after Erik {#cellars}

When the wattle buildings gave way to houses with substantial plank walls, the builders sank the lower parts of the new structures into the ground. On plot A the south doorway and its threshold survived. The excavation photograph shows timbers running across the opening and the uprights on either side. The builders put older wood to work around the entrance. Among the pieces the excavators identified the remains of a barrel lid.

Growth rings in wall planks from this house gave a felling date in the winter of 961/62. Some of the posts yielded wider ranges. Those dates offer a far better starting point for the history of this particular house than the change of ruler in 954. There is a parallel across the Irish Sea. In the [longship built in the Dublin region](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#dublin), one oak can be dated to a few weeks in 1042. In York people stepped over a timber threshold. In Dublin shipwrights fitted a beam to support a mast. In each case enough wood survived for someone a thousand years later to put a date to the work.
