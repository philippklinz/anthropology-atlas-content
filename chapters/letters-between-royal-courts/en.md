# Letters Between Royal Courts

An Assyrian king sends a messenger with gifts to Egypt. About eighty years later, the Egyptian and Hittite kings agree to make peace. A tablet that fits in a hand, pieces of a treaty copy and an inscribed wall show how royal courts dealt with one another over long distances.

## Amarna

Letter EA 15 is 7.7 centimetres high. In the opening lines Aššur-uballiṭ I identifies himself as the sender. The recipient is addressed as the king of Egypt. The Metropolitan Museum identifies Akhenaten as the probable recipient and dates the tablet to around 1347–1330 BCE.

The letter names a chariot, two horses and a small bead of lapis lazuli as greeting gifts. The messenger is expected to learn about the king and his land during the visit and then return. The script consists of wedge-shaped impressions in clay. The language is an Assyrian form of Akkadian. The map point takes you to Amarna, the probable findsite of the received tablet. The messenger’s route is unknown.

## Hattusa

Pieces of a different document came to light in the Hittite capital in Anatolia. Clay tablets from Hattusa preserve an Akkadian version of the peace treaty between the Egyptian king Ramesses II and Hattusili III. The rulers agreed on peace and promised to help one another. Some clauses also address how the two sides should treat people who had fled into the other country.

The texts describe a version on silver. The fragments in the museum show how scribes recorded the treaty on clay. Compare the dense rows of wedge signs with the small tablet from Amarna. The same script served both a letter bearing gifts and an agreement between kingdoms.

## Karnak

The Egyptian version stands on the outer west wall of a court at the temple of Karnak. It is written in hieroglyphs. The inscription names the twenty-first year of Ramesses II’s reign. In the chronology used here, that corresponds to around 1259 BCE. The date in the timeline refers to the agreement. The dates when individual surviving versions were written or carved are less precisely known.

The inscription describes the silver tablet that the Hittite court sent to Egypt and the seals on it. One belonged to Queen Puduḫepa. The text on the temple wall tells us what her seal looked like. When you switch from Hattusa to Karnak you move between two surviving versions of the same agreement. The change of location follows the findsites of the documents.
