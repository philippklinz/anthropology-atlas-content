# Persians – Shapur’s inscription and the copyists of Tabriz

In 1309 a man in Tabriz set down which books he wanted copied at his expense. Four years later he went so far as to specify the leather that binders should use on some of those books. Rashid al-Din wrote in Persian about the history of the world and worked for rulers descended from Chinggis Khan. Who were the “Persians” in this setting? The answer shifts as we move from one object to the next. The language of a writer, the patron of a mosque and the place where a bowl came out of the ground tell us quite different things.

## Who was remembered at a royal fire? {#naqsh-e-rostam}

At Naqsh-e Rostam, northwest of Persepolis, a stone tower stands at the foot of a tall cliff. Its builders lived centuries before Shapur I. In the third century CE the Sasanian king used its outer walls for an inscription. A reader walking round the building encounters three languages. Middle Persian and Parthian appear alongside Greek. It was perfectly possible to announce a victory over Rome in Greek. The king lists lands and successes and tells how he captured the Roman emperor Valerian in 260. This part of the text helps us date the inscription itself. By the time the words were cut, the emperor was already in Shapur’s hands.

The walls also record something of what happened to people caught up in the king’s wars. He tells of Roman officers deported to Persis. Their lives continued beyond the fighting, but here it is the victor’s voice that describes what happened to them. Elsewhere in the inscription the king makes a very different kind of provision. Shapur founded sacred fires in honour of members of his family. Lambs, bread and wine were assigned for daily offerings. His mother and daughters had a place in these arrangements too. A court making its own record could accommodate both the victory it wished to proclaim and work that people would have to do day after day to deliver the offerings.

## A turquoise bowl from Nishapur {#nishapur}

The Sasanian dynasty lost power in the seventh century during the Arab conquests. Centuries later Nishapur in Khurasan was a centre of trade and craft in the eastern Islamic world. In 1937 excavators in its Sabz Pushan neighbourhood found a tenth-century bowl. It measures just under sixteen centimetres across and a little over five in height. Its makers covered the earthenware body in an opaque turquoise glaze. The bowl is now in the Metropolitan Museum in New York, accession number **38.40.243**.

Similar pieces have come to light at Isfahan and Istakhr. What draws the eye is the colour. To learn how such a piece was made it pays to look below the surface. Later potters produced a similar appearance using a new, quartz-rich ceramic body known as stonepaste. A familiar taste in tableware could thus outlast a substantial change in how the vessels were made. Excavators in the old city of Nishapur also recovered gaming pieces, beads and cosmetic containers alongside household vessels. They help to furnish a picture of life in the city. Even so, we can hardly tell to which household the turquoise bowl belonged or what anyone last put in it.

## Two domes and two viziers {#isfahan}

In the Friday Mosque of Isfahan it is worth looking north and south before turning to the middle of the courtyard. Under the Seljuk sultan Malik Shah his vizier Nizam al-Mulk commissioned a domed chamber over the prayer niche. The foundation inscription gives no firm year. On the north side his rival Taj al-Mulk had another dome built. An inscription there supplies a date of 1088. The northern chamber then stood outside the older mosque. Anyone standing at that spot saw a quite different building from the one through which a visitor can walk today.

Both patrons employed builders who could span a broad space in fired brick. Massive, clustered supports carry the southern dome. Under the northern dome the brickwork breaks up into smaller arches and geometric fields. We can readily see how the southern chamber related to prayer. What the northern chamber was used for remains open to debate. It is easier to identify the two patrons than to describe what the people who frequented this place did there. Another Seljuk court enters the story in [a school at Konya](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#konya).

## Why a bookbinder needed goatskin {#tabriz}

At Tabriz the vizier Rashid al-Din endowed a complex under the Mongol Ilkhans that housed a library, papermaking and teaching. His endowment deed of 1309 provided for the regular copying of a Qur’an in thirty parts and of a work on traditions of the Prophet. An addition in 1313 brought his own writings into the arrangement. Each was to be produced in an Arabic and a Persian version. They included his history of the world.

For copyists and binders that meant quite specific work. The sheets were to be large, the text checked against the exemplar and the volume bound in goatskin. Registered copies were destined for schools in different cities. A person borrowing a volume to make a copy was to leave a deposit. These provisions tell us what the founder hoped his establishment would achieve. To discover whether each book ever reached its destination one would need the surviving copies and the marks their owners left in them. The deed already gives us an idea of how many hands it took to pass on a “Persian” book.
