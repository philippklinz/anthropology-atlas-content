# The voices on the Türk memorial stones

Twenty days at the stone and the wall. That is how Yollug Tegin measured his part in making a memorial for Prince Kül Tegin. He put his name on one of its narrow edges. On a broad face a different man speaks. Bilge Qağan, the dead prince’s elder brother, gives an account of how their family came to power. Walk round to the back and the emperor of China explains what the prince’s death means to him. On a single stone from the grasslands of what is now Mongolia, two courts found room to set down the terms of their relationship.

## The counsellor and the seven hundred {#tonyukuk}

Tonyukuk told his own story on two pillars at Bain Tsokto, southeast of present-day Ulaanbaatar. The Orkhon memorials lie several hundred kilometres to the west. Tonyukuk begins his life among the Tabgač, his name here for Tang China. After the eastern Türk realm fell in 630, the Tang recruited members of its ruling families into imperial service. It was only in the 680s that a group gathered around Ilterish succeeded in establishing another realm. Its ruler bore the title *qağan*.

Tonyukuk remembers seven hundred people coming in from the woods and the open country. By his reckoning, two went on horseback to every one who travelled on foot. They lived at one point on hares and larger game, and he takes care to say that there was enough for everyone to eat. He also remembers choosing the new ruler. Later, a scout brought news that kept him awake, and he went to the qağan to offer his advice. In old age Tonyukuk set his own name beside the ruler’s. Without both men, he declared, neither the realm nor its people would have survived. The pillars give no year for their making. Proposed dates run from 715 to as late as 732–734, leaving open whose recollections appeared in stone first.

## Who came to mourn Kül Tegin {#mourning}

Kül Tegin died in 731, the Year of the Sheep. On the north face of his memorial Bilge describes a moment when he could scarcely see or think. He also worried about the eyes of his sons, lords and subjects who had wept for his brother. A few lines earlier he recalls leaving Kül Tegin in charge of the camp while part of the army was away. Their mother, sisters and daughters-in-law were among those who stayed behind. As Bilge tells it, they owed their freedom to his brother’s defence of the camp.

For the funeral in the ninth month the text sets down who made the journey. A minister came from the ruler of Tibet. Envoys arrived from the Sogdian lands in the west, including Bukhara. The court even gives the office of two men sent by the Türgesh ruler. They were keepers of his seal. The Chinese emperor’s representative brought gold and silver. We do not hear what any guest said. What survives is the roll that Bilge’s court chose to inscribe. A reader who had never met Kül Tegin could still judge his standing by the offices and distant homes of those who mourned him.

## Two courts on one stone {#kul-tegin}

Kül Tegin’s memorial was completed in 732, the year after his funeral. A paved approach from the east led between two stone rams facing each other across the entrance. Excavators in 1958 found roof tiles and pieces of red plaster. There was a building to enter and colour to see. Yollug Tegin mentions a wall as well as a stone. Bilge, for his part, says he asked the Tang emperor to send artists to decorate the memorial hall inside and out. The twenty days Yollug spent on his own work belong to a much larger building project.

On the south face Bilge calls on his relatives, high officials and the people of the Tokuz Oguz to listen. In his view the uplands of Ötüken, where he kept his court, were the best place from which to rule. Silk and flattering words from China, he warned, could persuade people to leave their own ruler. On the west face the Tang emperor speaks in Chinese. Calling Bilge his adopted son, he extends a father’s grief to the younger brother as well. Changing languages changes the answer to a quite practical question. Which ruler owed care to whom? Both accounts stood on the monument the courts had helped to make.

## When a son spoke of his father {#bilge}

Within a few years another stele stood nearby. Bilge died in 734, the Year of the Dog. His son dates the funeral to the fifth month of the following Year of the Pig. Long stretches of the monument raised in 735 read like the one for Kül Tegin. On each, the family’s history opens with the sky, the earth and the ancestors Bumin and Istemi. To cite the stones as independent witnesses for that distant past would be to count the same telling twice.

Elsewhere, it matters exactly who calls whom kin. Bilge says that he gave his daughter in marriage to the Türgesh qağan and that the ruler’s daughter married his own son. The two women left no account of their feelings here, but the marriage terms show an alliance contracted in both directions. In the account of the funeral, the voice changes again. Bilge is now the speaker’s father. Riding horses and the pelts of sables and squirrels figure among the offerings. Yollug Tegin signs his name once more. This time he reckons his work at a month and four days.

## How a name became legible {#reading}

When Vilhelm Thomsen presented his decipherment in Copenhagen in December 1893, copies of the Orkhon inscriptions were already in print. A Finnish expedition had examined them in 1890. The Chinese text identified the man the monument honoured. Thomsen started by distinguishing signs for vowels from those for consonants. Among his early clues were Kül Tegin’s name, *täŋri*, meaning heaven, and *Türk*. A reading gathered strength each time the same signs made sense in another group of letters. At the Orkhon, *Türk* names the community Bilge called on to listen. Three centuries later, [Mahmud in Baghdad](chapter:mahmud-and-the-turkic-languages#baghdad) set himself a different task. He explained to readers of Arabic how usage varied between communities.

It was only in 1897 that the field researcher Elizaveta Klementz recorded Tonyukuk’s two pillars at Bain Tsokto. Seventeen photographs of paper impressions came out two years later. Such sheets preserve strokes as they were when the paper touched the stone. Where an edge has since weathered away, an old impression may still settle which word the counsellor used.
