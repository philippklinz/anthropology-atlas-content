# Bursa between the citadel, the dye shop and the silk market

The ground-floor rooms of the Emir Han in Bursa have no windows. They were built for storing goods. Upstairs, each room had a window and a hearth. Travellers could leave their baggage below and sleep above it. The rents helped support a foundation that ran, among other things, a public kitchen. A room in a trading inn could help pay for a meal in another part of town. As Bursa grew, some of its rents would travel farther still.

## The town behind the walls {#citadel}

Bursa was already an old town when Osman and his son Orhan began to gain ground in Byzantine Bithynia. Its people lived behind walls on a hill at the foot of Mount Uludağ. Their farms and the settlements around them lay within easier reach of an attacker. Osman laid waste to the surrounding countryside. It was his son Orhan who took the town after a long siege, usually dated to 1326. Ottoman chroniclers and early calendars also give earlier years. Some accounts have Osman live to hear the news, while another has him die during the siege.

A deed and a coin take us closer to the change of rulers. Orhan was already issuing an endowment deed in 1324. A few years later he was striking silver in Bursa. The traveller Ibn Battuta, from Tangier, knew the town under Orhan. He recalled its markets, gardens and running springs. Outside it sick people could bathe at two establishments, one for women and another for men, and stay three days at a hospice. His account also credits Orhan’s father with taking Bursa. The baths were still there for a visitor to see. The siege was already a matter of recollection.

## An inn and a village endowed together {#orhan}

In 1339 Orhan had a mosque built outside the walls. The endowment included a school, a bath, a kitchen and the Emir Han. It gave people a reason to come to the lower town, whether they needed to put a bale in storage or to use the baths. A commercial district grew up around the inn. Later, Murad I chose the hot springs to the west for a foundation of his own. Bayezid I built on land given over to gardens and orchards to the east. Bursa spread along the mountainside between these centres.

The official inventory of the Emir Han counts 36 storerooms below and 38 chambers above. There was a small stable behind the building. Rents helped pay for the upkeep of Orhan’s institutions. The village of Cumalıkızık to the east also belonged to the endowment. A share of the support for people who used these facilities thus came from the work of villagers beyond the town. The inn remains legible in its two storeys today. The kitchen and school made way for a later town hall. For a foundation whose surviving documents reveal how rural revenues sustained an urban kitchen, we can look ahead to [Hürrem’s endowment in Jerusalem](chapter:hurrem-and-the-kitchens-of-jerusalem#jindas).

## The names on the tiles {#green-mosque}

In 1402 Timur defeated Sultan Bayezid I at Ankara. Bayezid’s sons fought over the succession until Mehmed I prevailed in 1413. An inscription at the entrance of Orhan’s mosque recalls what those years meant for Bursa. A Karamanid ruler burned the mosque in 1413 and Mehmed had it restored in 1417. He also commissioned a mosque of his own farther east. Visitors to his Green Mosque found guest rooms with hearths around an interior court. The sultan had a separate gallery above the entrance.

The portal names the builder Hacı İvaz and gives a date that falls in December 1419 or January 1420. Work on the decoration continued after Mehmed’s death in May 1421. An inscription in the sultan’s gallery credits Nakkaş Ali and records completion in August 1424. Below, on the niche marking the direction of prayer, a group signed its work in Persian as the “masters of Tabriz”. The signature ties the group to this particular piece of work. It gives neither the location of their kiln nor the names of those who set the tiles in place. The art historian Patricia Blessing considers the possibility of tiles made to order. A design could travel from one building site to another without the entire workshop making the journey.

## Three women and a house with a cellar {#court}

In August 1480 Nefise, daughter of İsa, had a transaction concerning her house recorded in Bursa. It stood in the neighbourhood of the Kavaklı Mosque and had living space and a cellar. Gülşah acted at court on her behalf. The record identifies her as a woman freed from slavery by Hamza Bey. The woman on the other side of the transaction was also a freedwoman. Her name was Gülbahar and she managed the property of two orphaned boys, Ali and Mustafa, as their guardian. Nefise owned the house, Gülşah had the authority to sell it and Gülbahar represented the interests of the boys.

The sum in the record is 1,000 dirhams. This form of sale raised cash. The previous owner could rent the house back and recover it on repayment of the purchase price. A registered sale need not have meant that a household had to move. The clerk had reason to record who could act for whom. Nefise’s reasons for raising money were her own.

In other quarters, the court registers take us behind the doors of the bazaar. In 1479 Paşa Hatun rented a dye shop in the Tabib Hoca neighbourhood. In 1485 Fatma Hatun, acting as an endowment’s administrator, let the Doğangözü Han for 900 dirhams a month. The parties entitled to collect rent and those who undertook to pay it entered the written record. The people who tended the dye vats could go unnamed.

## What a bale of silk brought to Bursa {#bursa}

The Koza Han opened on 30 September 1491. Its main court had two storeys, with another court to the east for storage and stabling. At the centre of the main court a small prayer room stood above a fountain. Sultan Bayezid II had the inn built to provide income for mosques and schools in Istanbul. [Bayezid’s commission](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#bursa) bound the two cities together through the rents traders paid in Bursa.

Silk created quite different livelihoods in the same town. Raw silk from Iran came here to be weighed, taxed and sold to buyers from the west. Bursa’s weavers could also turn silk into luxury velvet. One bale might leave town much as it had come in. Another supplied the fibre for finished lengths of cloth that took their value from the work done at the loom. Some such fabrics reached European churches. A buyer could admire the sheen of silk and the glint of metal thread woven into it. The surviving textiles do not bear the names of those who worked the looms.
