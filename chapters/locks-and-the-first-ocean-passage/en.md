# Across the continental divide aboard Ancon

For guests setting out from Panama City to attend the canal opening on 15 August 1914, the first part of the journey was by train. A special left at five in the morning and picked up passengers at Balboa, Corozal, Pedro Miguel and Gatún. By a quarter to seven they had reached the docks at Cristóbal on the Caribbean coast. Fifteen minutes later *Ancon* cast off to carry them back by water to the coast they had left that morning. The railway had taken them across the isthmus on rails. To take them back, the steamer needed a lake high enough to float a ship across the continental divide.

## The last train out of the cut {#cut}

On 20 May 1913 two steam shovels, Nos. 222 and 230, met on the floor of Culebra Cut. Their crews had reached the intended bottom of the shipping channel. Yet the shovels stood forty feet above sea level. Once Gatún Lake filled, its surface would stand about eighty-five feet above the sea. The difference between those figures gave ships some forty-five feet of water in which to pass over the remains of the ridge. A ship would cross the watershed with much of the rock beneath it still in place.

Work in the cut depended on the trains. As in the [earlier French works](chapter:the-french-panama-canal-project#culebra), empty cars had to arrive fast enough to keep up with the shovels, and every load needed somewhere to go. Some rock went into Gatún Dam. In Panama Bay the builders used spoil to link a string of islands to the mainland, forming a breakwater. On 10 September locomotive No. 260 hauled out the last rock the crews would dig on dry ground. There was still material in the channel from a slide at Cucaracha that January. The decision had been taken to flood the excavation and clear the obstruction from the water. The haulage job passed from trains to dredges and barges.

## A working tug climbs to the lake {#trial-at-gatun}

The first vessel to climb the three steps at Gatún was a tug that usually hauled barges at the Atlantic entrance. Late on 26 September 1913, *Gatun* entered from the sea. The engineers recorded a total lift of just under sixty-five feet, or about twenty metres. [Gatún Lake was still filling](chapter:gatun-lake-and-the-displaced-towns#railway-diversion). Applying the eventual lake level to this trial would give the tug an extra twenty feet to climb. The next morning it came back down the same three steps.

The tug stayed afloat throughout. Fresh water flowing from a higher level raised the water surface inside a chamber, taking the hull up with it. On the way down, the hull settled with the falling water. Gravity supplied the energy for the lift. There were electric motors for the gates and for the small locomotives that would guide a ship on lines from the lock walls. The weight of the water and the buoyancy of the hull did the lifting. Each passage sent fresh water towards the sea. A century later, an important question in [building the new locks](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#basins) would be how much of that water could be used again.

## How a crane boat made the crossing {#crane-boat}

Some of the first vessels to gain access to the middle of the isthmus had helped clear the way themselves. In December 1913 the old French ladder dredge *Marmot* cut a first channel through the slide at Cucaracha. The French crane boat *Alexandre La Valley* had already come up from the Atlantic side. On 7 January 1914 it continued to the Pacific. The engineers recorded it that morning in the western chamber at Pedro Miguel together with a hopper vessel, No. 9, and a barge, No. 6. Its passage across the isthmus had taken place in stages in the course of its work.

At Pedro Miguel a southbound vessel left the level of Gatún Lake. One step took it down to Miraflores Lake. A short stretch of open water led to the two remaining steps at Miraflores. The bottom chamber opened on an ocean whose level changed appreciably throughout the day. How far a ship descended on that last step therefore depended on the Pacific tide. The original canal had three steps on each side of the divide. At Gatún all three formed a single flight. On the Pacific side a lake separated the first from the other two.

## A shipboard lunch and a turn at the outer buoys {#ancon}

Which voyage counted as the opening? On 3 August *Cristobal* made a trial run from Cristóbal to Balboa and went back the following day. On 15 August the pilot John A. Constantine took *Ancon* through on the official passage. Both steamers had carried cement from New York for the dams and locks. *Ancon* had already climbed the three steps at Gatún on a trial run on 11 June.

The *Panama Morning Journal* thanked the steward, Leddeke, for the lunch served after the Gatún flight. At a quarter past eleven *Ancon* passed Gamboa. The dredges in the cut had moved aside, their crews lining the decks. Leddeke got a name in the paper. The men on the dredges figured as a group. At Pedro Miguel a photographer watched from Cerro Luisa as *Ancon* entered the eastern chamber. The picture shows spectators on the lock walls and the steamer’s two funnels, one behind the other.

At 3:50 in the afternoon the ship passed the Balboa docks and continued out to sea. Only at the outermost buoys marking the Pacific approach did it turn. Back at Balboa the docks were so crowded that small vessels ferried the passengers ashore. When President Belisario Porras and his cabinet landed, the national band played Panama’s anthem. The following day six waiting vessels went through the canal, three in each direction.

## When ships had to turn back {#slides}

In September 1915 the banks on either side of Culebra Cut shifted again. Excavation had taken away some of the support for the high banks. Under the weight of a sinking slope, rock could be forced up through the channel floor. A photograph taken on 21 September shows two dredges at work on a new opening. One was *Marmot*, which had opened the channel at Cucaracha in 1913. In November a photographer on the west bank recorded an unbroken barrier across the line of passage. Material moving in from the two sides had met.

The annual report counted roughly forty southbound vessels that had already cleared Gatún and had to return to the Atlantic entrance. Their crews saw the same flight twice without ever reaching the other coast. On 15 April 1916 the canal authorities reopened the route to general shipping. The guests aboard *Ancon* who had watched the dredge crews line their decks in August 1914 had caught a glimpse of the people on whose work the next passage through those hills would depend.
