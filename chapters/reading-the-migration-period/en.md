# Reading the Migration Period on the Map

In 376 a person standing on the north bank of the lower Danube could look across the water into the Roman Empire. Heavy rain had swollen the river. The Roman historian Ammianus describes people crossing in boats, on rafts and in hollowed-out tree trunks. Some tried to swim and drowned. Long before anyone could start cultivating a field on the southern bank, a family's survival depended on who got a place in a boat. On a map of Europe the whole episode fits beneath the tip of an arrow.

## Who wanted to cross?

People waiting on the bank had different options. A group led by Alavivus and Fritigern sent envoys to the emperor Valens to ask for admission. They offered to provide soldiers for future campaigns. From a distance, the emperor could see an opportunity for his army. For those waiting, the urgent questions were how to cross and whether there would be enough to eat. Another delegation, this time from the Greuthungi, was turned away. Even along a single river in a single year, people faced very different prospects. Ammianus has much to say about hunger and the dealings of Roman commanders. The [chapter on the Danube crossing](chapter:danube-crossing-and-adrianople) follows the dispute over provisions to the gates of Marcianopolis.

## Why begin on the Vistula?

Set the map to **100 CE**. Near the lower Vistula in what is now northern Poland, we can read ancient geographical accounts alongside archaeological finds. A cemetery can be investigated grave by grave. A Roman-period writer, by contrast, leaves us a group name and an approximate location. It takes a historical argument to connect the two. Nearly three centuries separate this moment from the crossing of the Danube. In between, some of the evidence takes us to settlements by the Black Sea. At Olbia imported amphorae turn up alongside vessels of types made across the region. The finds raise questions about trade, workshops and the people who lived together here.

Centuries later, in the sixth century, Jordanes wrote about a Gothic homeland across the sea. He knew a world in which Gothic kings had already ruled on Roman soil. His narrative tells us about the past people attributed to those kings in his own day. We have no comparable account of a particular family's crossing centuries earlier. Two chapters take a closer look at the finds. One begins at [the cemetery of Malbork-Wielbark](chapter:goths-lower-vistula), the other with [glass and pottery between the Dniester and the Black Sea](chapter:goths-black-sea).

## Two scenes in the same year

Pause in **410**. In Italy, Alaric's army took Rome. At the same time, Vandal groups were already on the Iberian Peninsula. Move forward one year to **411**. The map can now distinguish the provinces within Hispania in which a chronicler locates particular groups. A presence near a besieged city, a group's appearance in a chronicle covering an entire province, and a ruler's control of territory are three different historical claims. Each mapped area therefore has its own explanation. Where years separate two documented situations, the gap leaves us with an open question. For Rome, [Zosimus and Jerome describe food, ransom and the lives of the inhabitants](chapter:alaric-rome-and-aquitaine). For Hispania and North Africa, [From the Rhine to Carthage](chapter:vandals-rhine-to-carthage) follows the chroniclers’ entries all the way to a surviving deed of sale.

## What lasted through a change of ruler?

At **Carthage** in 439, Geiseric took over an existing Roman city. For a closer look at life under the new rulers, move forward a little over fifty years and open a deed from the North African countryside. In 494 Processanus and Siddina transferred cultivation rights on a plot with olive and fig trees. The scribe dated the transaction by the reign of a Vandal king and recorded it in Latin. Both sellers made their marks. Here were two people whose most important decision that day concerned the piece of land they cultivated.

## What connects a book from Ravenna and a signature in Toledo?

Around **500** two very different kinds of evidence illuminate royal power in Italy. When Theoderic arrived in Rome, the senate and people came to meet him. A written account describes the ceremony. In Ravenna, buildings preserve evidence for everyday life around his court. The [chapter on Theoderic’s Italy](chapter:theoderic-and-ravenna) traces the work of keeping water flowing into the city under a Gothic king.

At about this time a workshop, probably in Ravenna, copied the Gospels in Gothic on purple parchment. The translation into Gothic dated from the fourth century. Generations separate the people who worked on that translation from those who made the surviving book. You can examine a page in the [chapter on Wulfila and the Codex Argenteus](chapter:gothic-gospels).

In **Toledo** in 589 a council recorded the profession of faith of the Visigothic royal couple. The copies name Queen Baddo alongside King Reccared and preserve her own subscription. From Ravenna the parchment itself survives. At Toledo we read the words through later copies. Both bring individuals into a history often told as a map of kingdoms. The chapter [from Toulouse via Vouillé and Recópolis to Toledo](chapter:visigoths-toulouse-to-toledo) follows the changing settings of Visigothic rule across that century.

The **Migration Period** is a name historians came to give to many stories of this kind. The dates 375 and 568 often used to frame it are a convention. This series starts earlier to make room for the evidence on the Vistula. When a patch of colour changes on the map, it is worth opening the source attached to it. A findspot, a political decision and a private contract can tell three very different stories about the same landscape.

A Roman envoy passed through Naissus in 449. The chapter on [the Huns on the Danube](chapter:embassies-and-hunnic-power#naissus) follows what he saw on his way to Attila’s court.
