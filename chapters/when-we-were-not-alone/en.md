## Neanderthals, Denisovans and Homo sapiens

Imagine a world in which our species is one of several human groups. Some of their paths cross. They have children together; many generations later, people still carry traces of those encounters in their DNA.

Four findsites tell this story. This chapter overlaps in time with “Early Routes out of Africa”: history continues in different places at the same time.

## 1 · Denisova: two parental lineages in one bone

A tiny bone fragment from an Altai cave belonged to a female individual who lived to at least 13 years of age. We do not know her name. Researchers call the specimen **Denisova 11**.

Its DNA reveals something extraordinary: **her mother was a Neanderthal and her father a Denisovan.** This concerns immediate parentage. Denisova 11 was not Homo sapiens.

The date is much less precise than the genetic inference: a published age model places the specimen at approximately **118,100–79,300 years BP**. This broad range does not mean that one person lived that long.

## 2 · Bacho Kiro: ancestors a few generations earlier

Later, Homo sapiens lived in this cave in present-day Bulgaria. A molar bears the specimen number **F6-620**. Its genome contains notably long segments of Neanderthal ancestry.

Why does their length matter? As genetic material passes through generations, such segments are reshuffled and broken up. A statistical model can therefore estimate how far back an ancestry connection lies.

The 2021 study infers comparatively recent Neanderthal ancestors here. This differs from Denisova 11: **an earlier ancestor is not necessarily a parent.** The timeline shows the collective context of the early sampled individuals, approximately **45,930–42,580 calibrated years BP**, not F6-620’s individual lifespan.

## 3 · Ranis and Zlatý kůň: relatives at two sites

Some people from Ilsenhöhle in present-day Thuringia were related to the woman whose skull was found at Zlatý kůň in present-day Czechia. The findsites are about **230 kilometres** apart.

The 2024 study connects **Ranis4**, among others, to this woman: they share inherited DNA segments. These support an estimated kinship relationship, but not one uniquely reconstructed family tree. They do not tell us whether these people ever met.

At Zlatý kůň, old adhesives complicate direct radiocarbon dating. Comparison with Ranis suggests an age around **45,000 years**; other genetic models yield about 47,000–48,000 years with large uncertainty. A laboratory result always needs an explanation of how it was obtained.
