# Radegund and the women of Poitiers

An abbess sends a friend milk and other delicacies. The friend thanks her in verse and complains that his doctor has forbidden him to eat everything that now tempts his eye. The poet was Venantius Fortunatus. The woman who received his little poem was Agnes. We usually meet her in a very different kind of text. In a letter addressed to bishops, Radegund, who founded the women's monastery at Poitiers, explains how she brought Agnes up and how the community elected her abbess. Who was answerable to whom here? And how did letters, provisions and news pass through the gates of a convent whose inhabitants were expected to stay inside?

## A queen places herself under an abbess

Radegund came from the Thuringian royal family. As a child she fell into the hands of the Franks when they conquered her homeland. She later married King Chlothar I. In his Life of Radegund, Fortunatus describes a public break with her marriage. In Bishop Medard's presence Radegund put on religious dress. She gave a precious royal garment to the church and a gold belt to the poor. In this account two objects allow us to follow a queen's passage into religious life. In a Poitiers miniature from around 1100, rows of faces crowd behind Radegund as she approaches the convent gate. Artists in her city still imagined the occasion as a public one some five centuries later. In her own letter Radegund says that Chlothar provided the resources for her foundation at Poitiers.

She calls Agnes both her sister and someone she raised as a daughter. As abbess, Agnes could now give orders even to the woman who founded the community. The sisters followed a rule drawn up at Arles for women. They lived together, gave up personal possessions and were to stay inside the house. Gifts from friends outside went through the community. The thank-you poem for the milk takes on another meaning in this setting. It names Agnes as the person who sent the gift and includes Radegund in its affectionate closing greeting.

## Who would carry the cross through town? {#cross}

Around 568 Radegund sent clergy east with letters of recommendation from King Sigibert. She wanted relics for her community. The following year a piece of wood venerated as part of Christ's cross arrived in Poitiers. This might sound like a straightforward welcome. According to Gregory, however, Maroveus, the bishop of Poitiers, avoided the request to carry the relics into the convent with due ceremony. Radegund appealed to Sigibert again. Eventually Eufronius, bishop of neighbouring Tours, came to Poitiers with his clergy. Gregory describes the singing of psalms, burning incense and the light of candles.

Fortunatus, Gregory and the nun Baudonivia knew Radegund in different settings. In his biography Fortunatus concentrated on her care for the sick, the poor and her fellow sisters. Baudonivia wrote after 600 for the community to which she herself belonged. She told at length of the embassies and the connection with the imperial court. In his Histories Gregory gives only the broad direction the travellers took, eastwards. The map therefore leaves their route open and locates the reception. The part that people in Poitiers could see and hear is particularly well documented.

## Where Radegund wanted to be buried {#funeral}

In her letter to the bishops Radegund asked to be buried in the church of Mary where several of the sisters already lay. She even considered what should happen if the building was still unfinished when she died. Her wishes were to stand just the same. When she died in August 587 Gregory came from Tours. In his book on the confessors he speaks in the first person about receiving the news and coming to the convent. Three months later [royal daughters' futures were being discussed at Andelot](chapter:merovingian-queens-and-the-treaty#andelot-and-cahors). In Poitiers, too, a woman had set down instructions that others could invoke after her death.

## The nuns leave Poitiers {#tours}

In 589 Clotild and her kinswoman Basina left the convent with a group of nuns. In Tours they asked Gregory to help them against the new abbess, Leubovera. Gregory casts himself as a mediator who urged them to go back. The women also had royal relatives to whom they could appeal.

## A piece of silk in court {#hearing}

The following year bishops heard the dispute in Poitiers. Gregory preserves their report to the kings. The women spoke of hunger, inadequate clothing and ill-treatment. Leubovera pointed to the high cost of food and denied that supplies had run seriously short.

One allegation brings an individual witness into view. Had the abbess used the fabric of an altar cloth for her niece? A nun named Didimia confirmed that she had brought a silk wrap from her relatives and given it to the abbess. In Leubovera's account, the material had furnished a covering for the altar. A piece left over had trimmed her niece's dress. The bishops left Leubovera in office. Their verdict tells us whose evidence prevailed at the hearing. The same lines preserve the complaints of women whose experience of life behind the gates was quite different.
