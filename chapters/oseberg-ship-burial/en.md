# Two Women in the Oseberg Ship Burial

There was a timber house in the middle of the Oseberg ship. Two uprights carried its ridge beam, with boards sloping down to either side. Beneath this roof two women were laid to rest. By then the ship had already carried people across water. Something of both uses survives in the wood. Ask how old the vessel is, and a sample from a plank and another from the roof of the burial chamber will give quite different answers.

## An oak tree on Karmøy {#storhaug}

One clue to the ship’s origins comes from western Norway. On the island of Karmøy beside the Karmsund strait stood a burial mound called Storhaug. It contained a large ship, a smaller boat and some loose pieces of timber. One piece still had the very outside of the tree. Its oak had been felled during the growing season of 779. The mound was probably raised that summer. The larger ship inside dated to about 770. What people committed to the grave on Karmøy included a vessel others had put time and skill into years earlier.

How does this help explain a find several hundred kilometres to the east? An oak lays down wide or narrow growth rings depending on local conditions. Niels Bonde and Frans-Arne Stylegar compared long runs of rings in the planks. Those in the Oseberg hull matched wood from Karmøy much better than they matched the timbers of the burial chamber at Oseberg itself. The oaks used for the hull had probably grown in the west. The builders selected other woods for particular jobs. They used pine for the mast and oars, and beech for parts of the carved upper edge at the bow and stern. They built the ship around 820. The map marks the mound where the comparative material came from. The location of the shipyard is still an open question.

## What people chose for the two women {#oseberg}

At Oseberg near Tønsberg, people set the ship, just over 21 metres long, in a specially dug trench. They used younger trees for the burial chamber. Five samples preserved the last ring below the bark, placing the felling in 834. The goods assembled for the burial included a wagon, beds, kitchen equipment and four sleighs. The three [sleighs with carved runners](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#oseberg) had seen service before they went into the ground. Pieces of cloth give a much closer view of what could happen to an object before someone chose it for the grave.

Someone had cut silk fabric into narrow strips. Some pieces had been sewn on with the reverse side showing. The museum suggests that this may have given a worn fabric a second life. There were woollen fabrics, embroideries and narrow woven bands, as well as weaving tablets, spindle whorls, needles and scissors. The fabrics themselves are evidence of work by people who spun thread, chose colours, worked patterns into cloth and mended it. A strip of silk could pass through several hands on its way here. Finding a needle beside someone at burial tells us very little about who used it in life.

On two of the largest pieces of figured weaving, people and animals move one behind another. Some figures are on foot, others on horseback or in wagons. It is tempting to see the funeral of the two women in this procession. There is no inscription naming them and no portrait we can identify. The figures might belong to a story or to a different ceremony altogether. Those who provided for the grave clearly commanded considerable resources. How the women were related to each other, and the position each may have held, remain unknown.

## A spade above the chamber {#mound-opening}

More than a century later, people dug into the mound from outside and cut their way through the roof. Their passage contained down from bedding, fragments of tapestries and wooden chests, and human bones. Part of one skeleton lay at the bottom of the passage. Bones of the other woman were scattered between it and the chamber. Anyone making an inventory today has only part of the original burial to work with. Even the place where an object lay might reflect this later visit.

The intruders in turn left tools behind. A broken oak spade was found above the chamber. Jan Bill and Aoife Daly examined the growth rings of implements left in the mounds at Oseberg and nearby Gokstad. For Oseberg they consider a date between 953 and 975 especially likely. They raised the possibility that damaging such a prominent monument was a way of discrediting an older claim to power. This was around the time [Harald Bluetooth laid claim to Norway](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#jelling). The stone on which he had that claim carved says nothing about Oseberg. Who ordered the grave to be opened remains unknown.

## The people in the excavation photograph {#excavation}

In 1903 the farmer Oskar Rom brought the archaeologist Gabriel Gustafson a piece of wood from his land. The main excavation began in June 1904. Olaf Væring photographed the team standing between the exposed ship and the edge of the pit. Among them were Aksel Stubstad, the foreman, and Paul Johannesen, a museum preparator. The engineers Johan Martin Glende and Beck measured the hull and the buckled chamber. Later scholars drew on their field measurements to reconstruct the shape of the roof before it had given way.

Cloth called for another kind of record. After the excavation, the illustrator Sofie Krafft immersed individual fragments of silk and drew the patterns she could see through the water. She recorded 41 of the 110 surviving silk pieces in this way. In the years that followed the patterns faded. In places her drawing now gives a better view of an ornament, while the original remains the best evidence for the thread. A reconstruction can bring the two together. The colour a particular piece of clothing once displayed is often still uncertain.

## Two aquariums without fish {#aquariums}

In 1912 Gustafson commissioned two aquariums for the Historical Museum in Kristiania, now Oslo. Each would display a wooden post carved into an animal’s head. Groundwater and a shortage of oxygen had helped preserve the carvings in the mound. Once lifted, the wood could shrink or collapse as it dried. Fine details on one head had grown indistinct after treatment with alum. Gustafson decided to keep four other heads and two carved sledge pulls in water until a better method could be found.

For decades someone had to look after the tanks. One sprang a leak during the Second World War, destroying the head it held. A woodcarver, Jørgen Eriksen, had already made a copy that still records the lost design. In the 1950s the chemist Annemor Rosenqvist used a gentler drying process on the five remaining pieces. They were ready for exhibition in 1957. The survival of a finely carved head depended on decisions made in workshops more than a thousand years apart. The carver judged how much wood to leave between the coils of an animal’s body. The conservator had to find a way to draw water out of that same wood while keeping those delicate ridges intact.
