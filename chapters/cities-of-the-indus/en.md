# Cities of the Indus

A small seal from a courtyard, a drain beneath bricks, and a reservoir over seventy metres long. Three finds show how varied life was in the cities of the Indus civilisation. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are in present-day Pakistan. Dholavira is on Khadir Island in present-day India. The Indus civilisation is attested at many settlements well beyond the Indus valley itself.

## Harappa

East of an excavated street, archaeologists found a mud-brick house with a large courtyard containing hearths and a brick bin. The excavators recovered the fired steatite seal H95-2491 from the courtyard floor context. Several signs and the image of an animal showing one horn were cut into the stone. Pottery dates this part of the courtyard sequence to about 2450–2200 BCE.

Press an engraved seal into damp clay and the pattern appears in relief, with the design reversed. Impressions like these survive on clay objects from Harappa. The individual who used this courtyard seal and what they used it for remain open questions. The Indus signs have no generally accepted reading. How the signs relate to spoken language is debated.

Other finds give us more information about coordination between settlements. Stone cubes from many sites follow comparable weight increments. Shared measures could have helped people weigh items and exchange them.

## Mohenjo-daro

Imagine fetching water in a densely built neighbourhood. The circular well shafts at Mohenjo-daro were made of wedge-shaped bricks. The photograph shows a well at the site. In excavation Block 8, Ernest Mackay described a well in a paved chamber. Steps were added at a later point as the surrounding floor level rose.

A section of drain survives in another room of the same block. Large bricks still lay across the channel as covers. Elsewhere, openings led out from houses into soak pits or channels along streets. Water could move from an indoor room into the street outside. The excavation report places the well and drain in different local building phases. The map sets both in the wider period of Indus urbanism.

## Dholavira

On Khadir, a different question arose. How do you collect rainwater when it flows along streams only at certain times? People built several deep basins within the settlement and designed them to collect runoff. The eastern reservoir measured about 73 by 29 metres at the top. Flights of steps led down into it. The builders cut into the rock and edged the basin with stone walls.

The excavator assigned an earlier basin to the phase around 2800–2500 BCE. He proposed that the large surviving form belonged to the phase around 2500–2100 BCE. Both figures describe local construction phases. In the photograph you can see people above an excavated basin, which gives a sense of its scale.

Harappa’s courtyard, Mohenjo-daro’s drain and Dholavira’s reservoir show cooperation on different scales. How people organised that work and how the cities were connected politically remain open questions.
