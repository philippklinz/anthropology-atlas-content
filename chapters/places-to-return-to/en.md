# Places to Return To

What makes a place worth returning to? A paved floor can outlast a single fire. Later, someone can build a hearth above the old one. Layers like these help us see how people used and reshaped a place. Three sites in the Levant show how differently such traces can survive.

## Shubayqa 1

You may have met the stone building at Shubayqa 1 in “As the Ice Retreats.” In what is now north-eastern Jordan, people built two hearths inside it. The upper hearth was installed in a later occupation phase in almost the same spot. Seven radiocarbon measurements on short-lived plant remains from the hearths give approximately 14,400–14,200 calendar years BP. BP counts years before 1950 here.

Charred lumps survived in the hearths. Under a microscope, researchers identified cavities and fragments of tissue consistent with bread-like foods. Fifteen of the 24 pieces they examined contained cereal tissue. Some also contained tissues consistent with underground plant storage organs. The team discusses flour from wild cereals and tubers as possible ingredients. How often people prepared these foods remains open.

Look at the aerial photograph. It shows the setting and the excavation around the stone building. The tiny remains from the hearths reveal things people did in such spaces. The sequence shows that people came back to the same place.

## Eynan

On the edge of the Hula Valley, Eynan, also called Ain Mallaha, lay close to a spring. People cut rounded building spaces into the slope and built stone walls against it. Researchers examined which floors, walls, hearths and postholes belonged together. In one area they found at least three buildings built one above another. People used the same hollow at different times.

The drawing is a reconstruction of Shelter 51. It shows a floor plan above and a proposed section below. The finely drawn stones and the heavy black roof beams represent different kinds of information. Researchers observed stone rows and postholes. They inferred the roof from traces like these. Different researchers have assigned the postholes to different buildings.

The timeline gives a broad view of the site's Natufian occupation phases. The building phase shown in the drawing belongs to the earlier part. Floors laid over older ones show repeated use. Walls on their own offer little precision about how much of the year any one person spent there.

## Wadi Faynan 16

Farther south is Wadi Faynan 16. The partly sunken Structure O75 was around 20 metres long. Benches in two tiers ran around parts of the walls. Several surfaces plastered with mud and a trough lay inside. In the excavation photograph you can see the exposed floor in front. Deposits from a later use of the area lie farther back.

The team sees the size and arrangement as clues to shared activities. Bird bones from the site also offer clues about seasons. From these finds, researchers developed a model in which a small group stayed longer and more people came together at particular times. What people did inside the building is still open.

Compare the three places. A replacement hearth records a different action from a new floor or a room with space for many people. In the next chapter, you can look for the clues that suggest a building was used for storage.
