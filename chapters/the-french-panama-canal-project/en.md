# The French canal project in Panama

On share certificate number 278792, two women reach across a shipping lane to clasp hands. Captions identify them as North and South America. At the foot of the sheet, someone in Bayonne has signed for two payments of 125 francs, nearly eight months apart. The person who took that money to the counter was paying for a canal whose route had been debated in Bogotá and Paris. Whether a ship would ever sail through it depended, among other things, on how far workers at Culebra could carry the earth they dug out.

## What Colombia granted {#concession}

When Eustorgio Salgar and Lucien Napoléon Bonaparte Wyse signed an agreement in Bogotá on 20 March 1878, Panama was a federal state within the United States of Colombia. Salgar represented the Colombian government. Wyse acted for a private association that had surveyed possible crossings. Congress amended their agreement. On 18 May, President Julián Trujillo signed the law into effect, and Wyse accepted the changes in writing. A reader who stops at the first articles in the printed contract will miss some of the most consequential terms.

The concession gave the company the right to build a canal and charge ships for passage. Its ninety-nine years would begin only when at least part of the canal opened or the company began collecting tolls. Colombia secured a share of gross receipts, initially five per cent, rising over the decades. A fifth of that share was earmarked for the state through which the canal passed. The [railroad across the isthmus](chapter:roads-rivers-and-the-panama-railroad#railway) already held rights of its own. A canal built within the area covered by those rights would require an agreement with the railroad company or compensation. The line on the map ran across land where people had long been doing business on other terms.

## How high should the water be? {#congress}

In May 1879 a congress met at the headquarters of the Geographical Society in Paris. Ferdinand de Lesseps, famous for his part in the Suez Canal, supported a cut at sea level. Ships were to pass from the Caribbean to the Pacific without using locks. This meant cutting through the continental divide deep enough to let a loaded ship pass. It also meant dealing with the Chagres, whose valley offered a route inland and whose flow swelled far beyond its dry-season level.

Godin de Lépinay, an engineer who had built a railroad in Mexico, proposed damming the Chagres on the Atlantic side and the Río Grande on the Pacific. Locks would lift ships to a high lake and lower them again on the far side. De Lesseps wanted the congress to endorse the route through Panama and attract investors to the project for which Wyse had obtained the concession. Cuban-born engineer Aniceto García Menocal presented a route through Nicaragua for the American delegation. On 29 May a majority endorsed the sea-level scheme through Panama. Pedro Sosa, an engineer from Panama, was among its supporters. Washington had instructed its delegates to contribute their professional judgment. Their presence committed the United States to no construction project.

## A share paid by instalments {#share}

On 8 and 9 December 1880 the public could subscribe for 590,000 shares in the canal company. A further 10,000 were set aside for the association that had contributed the concession. Together these represented 300 million francs of capital. The big lettering on the certificate gives that figure. Smaller lettering explains how much of this one share had initially been paid up. It was half the face value of 500 francs.

Spaces at the bottom record the third and fourth instalments. The receipts are dated February and September 1886. A stamp above the company name records that the right to subscribe to Panama bonds had been used up. The sheet bears traces of more than one kind of transaction. A share made its holder a part-owner. A bond made its holder a lender. In 1882 the canal company had issued bonds to pay for stock in the railroad. No name tells us who brought the instalments to the counter at Bayonne. Ownership went with possession of the certificate.

## Where could the spoil go? {#culebra}

In July 1886 a firm called Artigue, Sonderegger et Cie. took on excavation at the Culebra Cut. Work sites now followed one another nearly without a break from Matachín to Culebra. Here the capacity of an excavator's bucket was a poor measure of progress. Sticky clay had to be scraped from the tools. If there were no cars to take the spoil away, the next operation waited. Earth tipped too close to the cut could wash straight back into the channel during the rains. A gentler slope was likelier to hold, but someone had to dig out the extra earth.

Many of the workers came from Jamaica. Families had looked to the isthmus for employment since the railroad was built in the 1850s. For 1882 to 1884 the National Library of Jamaica gives almost 33,000 departures and nearly 15,000 returns. These figures count journeys. They cannot tell us how many people worked at Culebra on a particular day or why someone went home. They do help explain how the consequences of a missed wage payment or an illness could reach well beyond the work camps to the island.

## When the money ran short {#liquidation}

In 1886 chief engineer Léon Boyer warned Paris that time and money would run out before a sea-level cut could be finished. He died of yellow fever that May. In October 1887 the technical advisory committee proposed building a higher canal with locks. This would defer some of the digging. Floating dredges could work in the impounded reaches, gradually deepening the channel. Even now the company held out the prospect of the sea-level canal for which it had raised the money. Gustave Eiffel received the contract for the locks. Work under the revised plan began in January 1888.

The next appeal to investors fell short. On 4 February 1889 the Seine civil court in Paris ordered the company into liquidation. Some work on the isthmus continued until mid-May. Investors now had a different calculation to make. What would anyone pay for a partly excavated cut, a stock of machinery and the right to collect tolls on a waterway that had yet to earn any?

## The keys to the former Grand Hôtel {#transfer}

A successor company, formed in 1894, took over the property and resumed work on a limited scale. On 4 May 1904 US Army officer Mark Brooke received the keys and inventories at the company offices in Panama City. The former Grand Hôtel where he stood now houses the Canal Museum. The director instructed staff to hand over the stores and sent letters and telegrams to other sites along the line. Brooke's receipt was drawn up in French, English and Spanish.

The forty-million-dollar purchase also included railroad stock and engineering drawings and surveys. Profiles of the ground showed how much earlier crews had taken away. Some records were lost when offices were cleared for use, but many of the plans survive. When the [*Ancon* sailed through in 1914](chapter:locks-and-the-first-ocean-passage#ancon), part of its course lay in a cut where the French company’s crews had worked almost thirty years earlier.
