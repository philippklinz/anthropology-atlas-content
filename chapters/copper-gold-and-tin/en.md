# Copper, Gold and Tin

Gold rings in a grave, a copper axe in the Alps and an account of a tin shipment on a clay tablet. Great distances and many centuries lie between these finds. They tell us three different things about metal. What people gave at a burial, where the material for a tool came from and what it cost to ship a load.

## Varna

People made a cemetery near the Bulgarian Black Sea coast, north of modern Lake Varna. A model using calibrated radiocarbon dates places its use roughly between 4600 and 4340 BCE. That range gives the approximate window for the cemetery as a whole.

Grave 43 held gold rings, beads and thin decorative plates. Krauß and colleagues found that about a third of the cemetery's gold pieces, counted individually, came from this one grave. Other graves contained far less. Some pits held offerings without human remains. These differences give clues to which people or rites received special attention at burials. The objects alone tell us less about who controlled gold in life and what positions people held.

## Tisenjoch

The next stop takes you to the Ötztal Alps. In 1991, Ötzi and his equipment were found in a hollow near Tisenjoch. His axe has a copper blade and a yew handle. Radiocarbon dates from the handle place the axe roughly between 3350 and 3000 BCE. The picture shows a modern reconstruction.

The copper in the blade contains a tiny amount of lead. Researchers compared the proportions of lead isotopes in the blade with measurements from ores. The closest matches came from the area around Campiglia Marittima and the Colline Metallifere in southern Tuscany. That area lies several hundred kilometres from Tisenjoch. Whether raw copper or a finished blade made its way north remains open, as do its route and who passed it along.

## Kaneš

Merchants from Anatolia and the city of Ashur lived and worked in the lower town of Kaneš, the site now called Kültepe in Türkiye. The clay tablet in the photograph probably came from there. The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates it to about the 20th or 19th century BCE.

The palm-sized tablet records the accounts for two shipments in Old Assyrian cuneiform. It lists tin and textiles as well as seven black donkeys. The cost of animals was only part of the bill, which also included harnesses, fodder, wages and an export duty. Many of the amounts were stated in silver, measured in minas and shekels. Tin can be combined with copper to make tin bronze. Who ultimately bought the tin in this account and how they used it remain open questions.

At Varna, comparing many graves tells us how offerings were distributed. At Tisenjoch, a sample from an object offers clues about the source of its copper. The tablet shows what merchants recorded for their accounts. If you had only one of these finds, which question would you ask first?
