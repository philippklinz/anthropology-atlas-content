# How much water does the expanded Panama Canal need?

On 29 April 2016 the national lottery urn stood in the rotunda of the Panama Canal Administration Building. A girl named Margarita Azucena drew one of the two balls. The notary, Víctor Aldana Aparicio, opened it and passed it to the canal administrator, Jorge Quijano. The name inside would decide which vessel made the first passage through the expanded locks. One shipping line had offered a car carrier called *Thalatta*, the other a container ship called *Andronikos*. Quijano announced that the container line had won. By the time its ship arrived, it would have a different name. The decision to build the locks had fallen to voters across Panama almost ten years earlier.

## The question on the ballot {#referendum}

On 22 October 2006 Panamanians voted on a proposal to build a third set of locks. Since [the handover in December 1999](chapter:who-governed-the-panama-canal#handover), the republic had run the canal. Article 325 of its constitution gave voters a direct say in an expansion of this kind. The proposal first had to win the approval of the executive and the legislature. At least three months had to elapse between the legislative vote and the referendum.

The existing locks had two parallel lanes. The authority proposed adding a wider lane, with new locks at both ends, excavating approaches, deepening existing channels and raising the maximum operating level of Gatún Lake. President Martín Torrijos argued that the additional income could finance social programmes. The Coordinadora Campesina Contra los Embalses, a coalition that opposed new reservoirs, feared that flooding would drive people from their homes. There was a history to that concern. [Old Gatún had disappeared under the dam](chapter:gatun-lake-and-the-displaced-towns#gatun) when the original canal was built. The authority's proposal for the new locks specified that it could use existing reservoirs.

Of 2,132,842 registered voters, 924,029 cast ballots. There were 705,284 yes votes and 201,105 no votes. That gave the proposal 77.8 per cent of the valid yes and no votes. Counting blank and invalid ballots as well, 76.3 per cent of all ballots carried a yes. Turnout was 43.3 per cent.

## Water held over for the next ship {#basins}

On 5 February 2016 the canal authority reported that a basin beside the top chamber at Agua Clara, near Colón on the Caribbean side, held enough water for testing. The builders could now fill and empty it to check that it held water as designed. At Agua Clara and at Cocolí, on the Pacific side, each flight had three chambers. Each chamber had three basins alongside it. A ship would pass through the chambers. The eighteen basins did their work without a ship ever entering one.

To lower a vessel, water drains from its chamber and the hull settles with it. Some of that water can flow into a basin and stay there until a chamber needs filling again. The difference in water level drives the flow by gravity. With three basins per chamber the design allowed 60 per cent of the water used in a lockage to be used again. In its proposal the authority also calculated how much fresh water a whole transit would take. It estimated that the larger locks with basins would draw about 7 per cent less than the original locks. ## Pilots on a crane ship {#trial}

In March pilots and tug captains had a new place to practise. Two small lakes were linked by a channel representing Culebra Cut. There were locks, tugs and ships at one twenty-fifth of their working size, among them a model of the container ship *Maersk Edinburgh*. The instructors could produce wind and waves. On 20 June pilots Fernando Jaén and Ricardo Varela took a full-size vessel through Cocolí. Their ship, *Oceanus*, was a US-built floating crane. Over on the Caribbean side, the chartered *Baroque* made repeated trial passages at Agua Clara.

The engineer in charge of the expansion programme was Ilya Espino de Marotta. In 1985 she had joined the canal shipyard as its only woman engineer. She went on to manage resources and project controls before taking responsibility for engineering during the expansion. Her career spanned the change from US to Panamanian administration. In January 2020 she became the canal's first woman deputy administrator.

## A container ship crosses from sea to sea {#new-locks}

On the morning of Sunday 26 June 2016 the ship chosen in the draw entered Agua Clara under its new name, *Cosco Shipping Panama*. It was just under 300 metres long and more than 48 metres wide. At Cocolí buses began arriving with spectators soon after nine. By noon the crowd was in place, with hours still to wait. Around three the ship sounded its horn as it entered the upper chamber. It descended to the Pacific and continued towards Asia.

In 1914 the guests aboard [the steamship *Ancon*](chapter:locks-and-the-first-ocean-passage#ancon) had crossed the same lake on the first official passage. A century later the original locks still carried traffic alongside the expansion. The new lane opened the waterway to vessels too wide for those earlier chambers. By the end of that September the authority had recorded more than two hundred passages through the new locks.

## A dry year at Gatún Lake {#rainfall}

In a radar image assembled from hundreds of Copernicus Sentinel-1 observations, ships appear as coloured specks on the dark surface of Gatún Lake and off both coasts. Blue records observations from 2020, green from 2021 and red from 2022. The image traces traffic across three years. Records of rainfall and lake levels tell what happened when the rains fell short in 2023.

From May to December 2023, seven of the eight rainy-season months brought less rain than usual to the canal watershed. As the lake fell, the authority limited how many ships could cross and how deeply laden they could be. World Weather Attribution found that El Niño made a season this dry more likely. Some rain gauges near the lake showed a drying trend while other stations showed increases. The observations and climate models did not allow the researchers to attribute the local drying trend to human-caused climate change. Meanwhile, growing cities needed more water and leaks in ageing pipes were wasting some of it on its way to homes.

For its fiscal year from October 2023 through September 2024, the canal authority recorded 2,118 millimetres of rain across the watershed against a historical average of 2,660. Its water accounts assigned 542 million cubic metres to drinking water, about 17 per cent of the year's total. At Escobal, a fishing community on the lake, the authority installed a pilot treatment plant in April 2024 to test the cost and effectiveness of removing salt from water by reverse osmosis. There the pressing question was how to deliver drinking water to some 2,500 people living on the shore of the very reservoir that kept the ships afloat.
