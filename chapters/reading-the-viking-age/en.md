# People of the Viking Age

Someone in York mended the sole of a sock. The thread is still there. Around the same time a Danish king had his parents’ names cut in stone. Both belong to the Viking Age, from the late eighth into the eleventh century. Some of the people who lived around the northern seas in those centuries went raiding. Others waited for customers, buried their dead or crossed the sea against their will.

## A lane by a river {#ribe}

Before 720 people on a riverbank in western Jutland laid out narrow plots along a lane about two metres wide. An old wine barrel from the Mainz area gained a second life as the lining of a well in [Ribe](chapter:ribe-and-hedeby#market-plots). At a workshop, beadmakers heated glass that had once gone into a mosaic. A drink of water or a white glass bead could already have a rather long history behind it in this early market.

## Arrivals at Birka {#birka}

A little over a century later the Frankish monks Ansgar and Witmar reached the [harbour at Birka](chapter:birka-and-its-eastern-connections#birka-harbour). King Björn gave them permission to preach around 830. According to Ansgar’s pupil Rimbert, some of the Christians already there were captives. A [ring with Arabic lettering](chapter:birka-and-its-eastern-connections#ring) was buried near the settlement in the same century. Rimbert could tell his readers about a particular journey the monks made. What survives of the ring tells us where it came to rest. The hands it passed through on the way are another matter.

## What went into the grave at Oseberg? {#oseberg}

In 834 two women were buried in a ship at Oseberg near the Oslofjord. Their [four sleighs](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#oseberg) included working vehicles with carving right down to the runners. Some [silk strips from the chamber](chapter:oseberg-ship-burial#oseberg) had been sewn on with the reverse showing. The museum suggests that the visible face had worn thin and someone had turned the fabric. Alongside the work of preparing a funeral we can still see the earlier, quieter labour of keeping costly possessions in use.

## Whose return to Dublin? {#dublin}

The Irish annals record under 871 that Amlaíb and Ímar returned to Dublin after laying siege to [Dumbarton on the Clyde](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#dumbarton) the year before. The [entry for their arrival](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#captives) counts two hundred ships and mentions Angles, Britons and Picts among the captives. It gives none of their names. The very word “returned” tells us whose journey the writer followed. For the people carried away from home, the crossing was compulsory.

## Two names on a penny {#york}

Nearly seventy years later Amlaíb mac Gofraid, king of Dublin, ruled briefly in York. On a [silver penny attributed to his reign](chapter:york-between-kings-and-craftspeople#raven-penny) his name appears as Anlaf beside a bird. Turn it over and Æthelferth, the moneyer, appears beside a cross. Someone in the city wore a hole in that [sock from Coppergate](chapter:york-between-kings-and-craftspeople#coppergate) during much the same period. The stitching survives just where the ball of the foot had worn through the wool.

## A carver and his queen {#jelling}

Gorm set up [a memorial to his wife Thyra](chapter:jelling-and-the-royal-builders#thyra) around 950. Some fifteen years later Harald named them both as his parents on [his own Jelling stone](chapter:jelling-and-the-royal-builders#jelling). The grooves resemble those on a stone at Læborg, where the carver identifies himself as Ravnunge-Tue and calls Thyra his queen. If the attribution is right, this family monument preserves the workmanship of a man who may have served two generations of the family.

## On the shore of Newfoundland {#newfoundland}

Three trees felled in 1021 left worked timbers at [L’Anse aux Meadows](chapter:farms-across-the-north-atlantic#newfoundland). The site also yielded a spindle whorl and evidence of ironworking. Indigenous communities had been using this coast for thousands of years. At [Black Duck Brook](chapter:farms-across-the-north-atlantic#black-duck-brook) a scatter of stone flakes marks a work spot beside a hearth. The hearth has a much wider date range than the three trees. The two sites are close together, but their proximity cannot put particular people on the shore together.

## A longship seen from either shore {#shipwrights}

In May or June 1042 the oak that would support the mast of the longship Skuldelev 2 was felled in eastern Ireland. Its hull survives from Roskilde Fjord. [Starting with the ship](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#dublin), tree rings lead back to the Dublin area. [Starting on the Dublin waterfront](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#dublin), timbers recovered from paths and waterfront structures show what became of some ships taken out of service. The precise yard that built the longship has yet to be found.

## A wedding long after the Viking Age {#hvalsey}

More than three and a half centuries passed before Sigríður Björnsdóttir and Þorsteinn Ólafsson [married at Hvalsey](chapter:farms-across-the-north-atlantic#hvalsey) in southern Greenland in September 1408. We know the names of these two because people later testified that the marriage had been duly announced and celebrated. Some gave their testimony in Iceland years afterwards. Those sea crossings continued to matter to relatives and witnesses long after the couple left the church.
