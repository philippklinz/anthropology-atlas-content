# Byzantines – A dome, two imperial portraits and a basket of cherry stones

Two cups and a cooking pot lay in the stern of a merchant ship in the harbour of Constantinople. The wreck also held a thimble, a comb and a basket of cherry stones. We do not know whose hands touched them last. We do know a name that many inhabitants of the empire used for themselves. In Greek they called themselves **Rhomaioi**, Romans. Historians have come to call their society Byzantine. Three places give quite different answers to the question of how far the capital reached into its inhabitants’ lives. Two are within walking distance of each other on a street map of Istanbul.

## What the congregation saw in December 537 {#hagia-sophia-537}

The people who attended the dedication of Hagia Sophia on 27 December 537 saw a dome that no longer exists in that form. Five years earlier the Nika revolt had shaken Constantinople. The previous church had burnt down. Justinian entrusted the new building to Anthemios of Tralles and Isidoros of Miletos. Its builders reused columns from older buildings in the arcades. Coloured marble lined the walls. Anyone looking up saw gold and, at the very top, a cross. The figures in mosaic that many visitors now associate with the church appeared in later centuries.

The difficult part of the architects’ commission hung overhead. Four great arches had to receive the weight of the central dome. Stand on the floor today and the curve above you records a second attempt to make that arrangement work. The first dome fell in 558. Its higher replacement was ready in 562. Two different questions about the building therefore have different dates as their answers. Its ground plan goes back to Justinian’s commission after the fire. To account for the vault above it, we also need the history of a collapse.

## Who got a name in Ravenna? {#ravenna}

Ten years after the dedication in Constantinople, Bishop Maximian consecrated San Vitale in Ravenna. The congregation in this Italian city could look up and see their emperor making an offering. In the mosaic Justinian carries a gold dish. Empress Theodora holds a chalice in a panel facing him. Along the hem of her mantle the three Magi bring their gifts to Christ. A worshipper looking closely could discover two acts of offering in a single figure.

Justinian’s companions stand shoulder to shoulder. The word above one cleric’s head identifies Maximian. The inscription still helps to pick him out almost fifteen centuries later. Scholars have proposed names for other faces that remain open to debate. The scene arranged the world in a particular order for the people who worshipped here. Courtiers, soldiers and clergy accompany the imperial couple right up to the sanctuary. The picture would make a poor attendance register for the day of the dedication. Another Ravenna mosaic bears the marks of a change of rulers in the [chapter about Theoderic’s city](chapter:theoderic-and-ravenna).

## What there was room for beside the amphoras {#yenikapi}

In the Theodosian harbour a different kind of find brings us close to the people of the empire. Archaeologists excavating at Yenikapı gave a wreck the designation YK 12. The bottom of its hull survived so well in harbour sediment that it was possible to reconstruct a small coastal trader, about nine metres long. Amphoras lay in the hold. The stern contained a brazier, dishes and two vessels of a different type. Where these things lay in relation to each other tells us how people made room for themselves and their cargo.

Radiocarbon dates for the timbers span the years 672 to 870. The trees had already grown before the ship sank. For the date of the loss, a ninth-century coin and the dating of the amphoras are important pieces of evidence. The cherry stones raised a smaller question. Fresh cherries could give a clue to the season. The stones alone can hardly tell us how long they had been aboard before the final voyage. We know more about the contents of one basket than about the names of everyone on this ship.

## Whose church was Hagia Sophia? {#hagia-sophia-1453}

In 1204 a western crusading army captured Constantinople. Decades of Latin rule followed, bringing changes at the cathedral too. Imperial courts formed elsewhere, including at Nicaea and Trebizond. Byzantine imperial rule returned to Constantinople in 1261. A map showing one permanent capital throughout these years would miss a change that contemporaries experienced quite sharply.

In 1453 the Ottomans took the city. Sultan Mehmed II had Hagia Sophia converted into a mosque. The people who had celebrated the Christian liturgy here lost their cathedral. The dome, marble panels and columns remained on the same spot.
