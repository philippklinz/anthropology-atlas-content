# On the market street at Ribe and the waterfront at Hedeby

Someone buying a white glass bead in Ribe would have had little reason to guess where its colour came from. White mosaic cubes turn up among the sweepings of an early workshop. They look like just the material for the job. Yet the glass in the finished beads tells a different story. A comb found in Hedeby poses much the same puzzle. Few towns yielded so much waste from working antler, yet many early combs probably arrived there with their teeth already cut. In both places the trail begins with the things that people left lying where they worked.

## A lane beside the river {#market-plots}

Early in the eighth century a lane about two metres wide ran along the river at Ribe, in western Jutland. By 720 there were plots some six to eight metres wide on either side of it. A customer could pass the entire frontage of a stall in a few paces. The boundaries between neighbours left a mark that outlasted everything on display. Some of the wells were lined with old barrels from the Mainz area on the Rhine. A vessel made to hold wine could spend its second life underground, keeping the sides of a well in place.

A row of measured plots hardly tells us who stayed on after a market. Sarah Croix went back to the records of the excavation at Sct Nicolajgade 8 and examined traces of buildings alongside finds from domestic activities. She argues that some people were already living at the market permanently in its first decades. Others could have come for the season. Small silver coins dropped on the plots give a glimpse of what passed from hand to hand. In the earliest layers there are several kinds. From about 725 one predominates, with a face on one side and an animal on the other.

## Why the white beads are full of bubbles {#beadmakers}

Beside the former post office, excavators found a pair of small hearths close together. They belonged to an early workshop dated to about 700–720. In and immediately around the hearths lay more than a thousand pieces of glass, including unfinished pieces, drips and broken beads. Some of the mosaic cubes on hand had once carried gold leaf. Their transparent backing glass was an unexpected source of white. Reheating it at a relatively low temperature left tiny bubbles trapped inside. The bubbles scattered the light. Gry Barfod and her colleagues could trace the choice of materials through the elements in the glass and the structure of the beads themselves.

The later workshop, dated to about 760–790, produced a wider range, including yellow, green, red and nearly black beads. Nearby fragments of crucibles still held glass mixed with a yellow colouring agent containing lead and tin. It would be difficult, looking at a yellow bead on a necklace, to say where someone had mixed its colour. The residue in the melting pot brings that work back to Ribe. The [houses on Coppergate in York](chapter:york-between-kings-and-craftspeople#coppergate) offer another chance to look for the people who worked indoors through the scraps they left behind.

## A layer of silver under amber-coloured glass {#segmented-beads}

By the end of the eighth century finished beads from the Middle East were arriving in Ribe in quantity. Some had several lobes joined like a row of little balls. One kind enclosed a thin sheet of silver between two layers of glass. An amber-coloured outer layer made the silver look like gold, while colourless glass let the silver shine through. Pieces with a broken outer shell let us see how the material was put together. A different way to make glass look precious can be seen in the [violet setting of a ring from Birka](chapter:birka-and-its-eastern-connections#ring).

The increase in these imports has been dated to about 790, give or take ten years. The clue came from an abrupt rise in the amount of radioactive carbon in the atmosphere in 775. Researchers identified its signal in material from one floor layer at Ribe, giving them a fixed point for a sequence of deposits and 140 radiocarbon dates. A similar, later signal appears in [three pieces of worked wood from Newfoundland](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#newfoundland). There the rings could be counted out to the year each tree was felled. At Ribe the date estimates when the new goods became plentiful. An individual purchase might have taken place on any number of market days.

## A comb in someone's luggage? {#antler-combs}

About 120 kilometres to the south-east, at Hedeby on the Schlei, a comb could be assembled from several pieces of antler. The plates carrying the teeth were held between connecting plates, often decorated and fastened with rivets. The photographs show rows of small circles, incised lines and interlaced patterns. Comparing shapes and ornament helps date the combs even where successive rebuilding disturbed the layers in which they were found.

Mariana Muñoz-Rodriguez and her colleagues selected 49 examples of a comb type characteristic of the ninth century. A small sample from each supplied fragments of the protein collagen for comparison with reference material from known species. For 42 combs the identification as reindeer was secure and for two more it was probable. Among the readily identifiable waste left by people working antler at Hedeby, reindeer accounts for only about half of one per cent. Local workers used mostly red deer. This discrepancy suggests that many combs of the early type had already been cut and assembled elsewhere. A merchant could have brought them in as goods. A visitor might also have arrived carrying a comb for personal use. The antler connects the port with the reindeer range farther north. It leaves open the question of who owned a comb along the way.

## A pointed plank at the waterfront {#harbour-timbers}

On the waterfront at Hedeby excavators came across a length of split timber, pointed at its lower end. Its rings date the wood to 885. It had been driven in where the boundary of a plot met the bank revetment. Other posts continued in the direction of the jetties. Timbers such as these make it possible to follow people extending their working space out into the water. Two particularly busy periods of building fall in 881 and 885. Over time the jetties reached deeper water, where laden ships could berth. Shorter connecting structures eventually turned the space between jetties into a platform on which goods could change hands.

Late in the ninth century the seafarer Ohthere described the final leg of a voyage to Hedeby at King Alfred’s court. He reported a passage of five days from Sciringes heal. The surviving account measures the voyage in days and names lands and islands to either side of the ship. The harbour excavation supplies a different detail of the journey. Once ashore a crew needed somewhere to walk, while other people bringing in their cargo needed room beside them.

## A house across the Schlei {#schleswig-house}

In 1071 someone built a timber house in what is now the old town of Schleswig. Beneath it the Schild excavation uncovered nearly a metre of earlier occupation deposits. Dating the beginnings of Schleswig to the year of that house would leave out the people who had already left those deposits. In Plessenstraße, closer to the waterfront of the time, excavators found plots laid out at right angles to the water. Open passages or timber paths ran between them. Some of the wattle fences showed as many as seven successive phases. The wood needed replacing where the water rose and fell and exposed it alternately to wet and dry conditions.

A fence kept in place for years in such ground bears witness to a great deal of work. In that respect this shore has something in common with the narrow lane at Ribe. Much of what we know of the daily life of these trading places survives because someone lined a well, drove in a plank or came back to weave fresh branches into a fence.
