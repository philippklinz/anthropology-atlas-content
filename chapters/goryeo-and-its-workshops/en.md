# A clay lion and a nun in a printer’s record

Give the lion on the lid of an incense burner an open mouth and the smoke has a way out. Potters in the kingdom of Goryeo designed such a vessel some nine hundred years ago. A visitor from China thought a lion burner remarkable enough to describe for his emperor. In other cases we learn the names of the people behind an object only because somebody put them in writing. The record at the end of a Buddhist book from Cheongju preserves the name of a woman whose donation helped pay for the printing. More than two and a half centuries separate the two accounts. The remains of workshops in between tell us about many people whose names were never written on their work.

## What Xu Jing wanted his emperor to know {#gaegyeong}

In 1123 Xu Jing came to Gaegyeong, the Goryeo capital at present-day Kaesong, with a delegation from the Song emperor Huizong. He spent about a month in the kingdom. On his return he presented an illustrated account of the mission. The illustrations have been lost. For an object whose colour he particularly wanted to convey we are left with his choice of words.

Xu Jing describes a jade-green incense burner with an animal crouching above a lotus. To give his readers a sense of its appearance, he mentions the wares of Chinese kilns whose glazes they might recognise. The National Museum of Korea has a lion-lidded burner close to his description. Its mouth is ajar, its forepaws heavy and its ears hang down beside the mane. The opening in the muzzle is also part of the design that made the vessel work. With incense smouldering in the bowl, smoke rose through the lion’s head. The surviving object makes better sense of what Xu Jing saw. There is no way of telling from his words whether the piece now in the museum ever stood before him.

## Glazing a roof {#sadang}

At Sadang-ri near Gangjin, on the southwestern coast, excavators uncovered kilns, equipment and vast quantities of broken ceramics. The workshops here supplied the court among other customers. The green that had so impressed a visiting envoy emerged only when a pot was fired. To make inlaid work the potters cut lines and hollows in the drying clay, then filled them with a pale or dark slip. A translucent glaze went over the top. You could see both the pattern and the ground through the same lustrous surface.

Some finds were glazed roof tiles. The history known as the Goryeosa describes a pavilion built for King Uijong in 1157 with a roof covered in celadon tiles. Someone looking up from below would have seen the colours of many pieces side by side. One perfectly fired tile would only be a beginning when a whole roof had to be assembled. The written account dates the construction of the pavilion. The kiln material cannot be dated tile by tile with that precision. At Sadang-ri the map therefore opens a window onto twelfth-century production. We cannot trace a particular tile recovered at the workshop all the way to its place on the royal roof.

## The evidence packed around a bowl {#sinan}

In 1323 a merchant ship sank between the islands of Imjado and Jeungdo. Many of its dishes and bowls had come from kilns in China. Finding them off the Korean coast tells us where this voyage ended. For the [likely departure port of Qingyuan](chapter:cities-and-records-in-medieval-china#qingyuan) and for [the customers in Japan](chapter:court-and-coast-in-medieval-japan#tofukuji), we can turn to other evidence on board.

It is worth looking at the packaging too. Researchers identified different woods in the cargo boxes. The sequence of tree rings in twenty-one boxes matched reference samples of Japanese cedar from western Japan. A history of a bowl that took in only the kiln and the intended market would miss the fact that some of the trees for the crates had grown somewhere else again. The rings cannot name the people doing the packing, or tell us every journey a plank made before its final voyage. The boxes and their contents came to light after a fisherman found pottery in his net in 1975. Over several summers divers recovered timbers, tens of thousands of objects and roughly twenty-eight tonnes of coins.

## A crooked character at Cheongju {#heungdeoksa}

In 1377 the monastery of Heungdeoksa near Cheongju produced two volumes of teachings of Buddhist masters. The monk Baegun had compiled them and had since died. His disciples Seokchan and Daljam oversaw publication. Myodeok, a Buddhist nun, helped to pay for it. These names survive in the history of the book, now usually called Jikji. The printing record at the end identifies the monastery and the cast type. The surviving volume is the oldest known book printed with movable metal type that we can still examine.

In the surviving second volume a character meaning ‘person’ leans at an angle. Elsewhere a character is missing. When workers set a page in pieces of movable type, an individual piece might turn or be omitted. Such little irregularities let a modern reader work backwards from the finished page to the job of setting it. The following year a different monastery produced an edition from carved woodblocks. Its first volume has survived too. By comparing the editions, a reader today can recover a part of the text missing from the surviving metal-type copy. Myodeok’s donation helped the teachings pass into a form that other hands, working by another method, would take up in turn.
