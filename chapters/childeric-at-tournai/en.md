# Childeric’s grave at Tournai

There is a loop on the back of a small gold insect. Beside the deep red garnets of its wings, it is easy to miss. Yet the loop tells us what someone could do with the ornament fifteen centuries ago. It could be fastened to heavy cloth or leather. Did it glint on someone’s coat, or on the harness of a horse? Either mounting would fit the evidence. The insect came from a grave by the river Scheldt, one of the rare finds to give us the name of a king.

## Reading a royal ring {#burial}

Childeric’s lost gold signet from Tournai bore the words **CHILDERICI REGIS**, “of King Childeric”. Its portrait shows a long-haired man with a lance. His armour and the cloak draped over one shoulder would have been familiar to a Roman military commander. The grave also held an openwork gold brooch. Brooches of this sort fastened a cloak and formed part of the dress of high officials in the late Roman Empire. A visitor could recognise a man’s standing before a word had passed between them. Whether an emperor ever appointed Childeric to an office, and what title he might have held, are questions the inscription leaves open.

Childeric was the father of [Clovis](chapter:clovis-and-his-bishops). The burial is generally identified as his and dated to **about 481 or 482**. A king could entrust his seal to another man to act on his behalf. In this case the inscription, the date of the coins and the unusual wealth of the burial together make a strong case for the usual identification. The grave was across the Scheldt from the fortifications of the late Roman town. It tells us where people buried a dead man. To learn where the king usually held court, or how far his orders carried, we need other evidence.

## Four silver coins with holes

The burial contained roughly a hundred gold coins and two hundred silver ones. The gold included pieces struck in Constantinople with the likeness of the emperor Leo I. Coins from Ravenna had also found their way to northern Gaul. A coin might pass through many hands before someone put it in a grave. Its mint tells us something about the journey of the gold, though very little about the travels of the dead man.

The scholar Jean-Jacques Chifflet later had four of the silver coins illustrated. All four were pierced. They could have been threaded or sewn on. It is easy to lose sight of such uses if we simply add up the face value of the coins. The numismatists Svante Fischer and Lennart Lind have suggested that Clovis himself selected coins for the funeral. No eyewitness has left an account of how they were chosen or who saw the emperors’ portraits before the grave was closed.

## A labourer, a physician and a printer {#rediscovery}

On 27 May 1653, while digging foundations near the church of Saint-Brice, a labourer named Adrien Quinquin struck gold. People crowded round. The local chaplain gathered up objects after some had already changed hands. Chifflet, a physician and antiquarian at the court in Brussels, was entrusted with finds to examine. By the time he saw them they had been cleaned. He could offer little first-hand evidence of where each had lain in the ground.

## The insects in the Antwerp edition {#printing}

In 1655 the Plantin-Moretus press in Antwerp published his illustrated study. On page 322 the draughtsman arranged the insects in two rows. He gave the ones at the top eyes, while those on the bottom row have smooth heads. They are shown alternately upright and upside down. A reader in a distant city could compare the shapes for themselves. Chifflet also had a political interest in the finds. He served the Spanish Habsburgs and challenged the French Bourbons’ claim to be the direct successors of the Merovingians. Which emblems the early kings had worn mattered to him for reasons that went beyond antiquarian curiosity.

## A theft and an impression in wax {#theft}

Chifflet reported around three hundred gold “bees” at the time of the discovery. Roughly thirty reached Louis XIV in 1665. After thieves broke into the Paris Cabinet des médailles in 1831 only two were recovered. One measures just 1.6 centimetres and weighs barely two grams. The original gold signet disappeared. In 1857, however, the librarian Charles Dauban found an old impression in wax on the back of a manuscript leaf. It provided the face of the seal for a replica. Chifflet’s illustrations helped establish the shape of the ring. Both steps lie behind the gilt bronze copy that a visitor sees today.

## What the ground still held {#cemetery}

Archaeologists returned to Saint-Brice between 1983 and 1986. Three pits near the royal grave contained the remains of twenty-one horses. There were other human graves as well. Among the finds from the cemetery were amber beads from four graves and several glass drinking vessels. We have few names for the people who buried their dead here over several generations. Many kings appear by name in the [history of the Merovingian Franks](chapter:reading-merovingian-francia). Saint-Brice also brings us close to people remembered by a string of beads or a drinking glass placed in a grave.
