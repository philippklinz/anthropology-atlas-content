# As the Ice Retreats

Ice sheets melt, sea level rises and coastlines change. The timing and pace varied by region. Three stops show how people and environments left traces in this period. The chapter covers 19,000–11,650 BP, meaning calendar years before 1950.

## Gönnersdorf

Along the Middle Rhine in present-day Germany, people engraved animals and other designs on slate. The site of Gönnersdorf is about 15,800 years old. Researchers studied the engravings by lighting them from different directions. This helps reveal fine lines and where they overlap.

Look at Plaquette 282. The tracing on the left guides you through the lines. The worked surface is on the right. The drawing shows a form interpreted as a fish within a grid. The research group identified fish-and-grid combinations on eight plaquettes and interprets them as depictions of fish caught in nets.

The figure offers a clue to how people might have fished along the river. A net would have been made of other materials and none survives in this evidence. The age of the site alone cannot tell us exactly when a particular line was engraved.

## Tahiti

Off Tahiti in the Pacific, researchers drilled into old reefs. The point on the map marks one of the modern boreholes. The cores tell us something about past coasts. The site provides a geological record of those coasts.

Some species of coral grow within a limited range of water depths. If we know a coral's age and the depth at which it lived, its position today helps us estimate where sea level was at the time.

The study places the start of **Meltwater Pulse 1A** no earlier than 14,650 BP and its end before 14,310 BP. The corals point to 12–22 metres of local rise. The researchers regard 14–18 metres as most likely. This window limits the duration of the rise. Its rate could vary within the window. Changes along other coasts require evidence from those places.

## Shubayqa

At Shubayqa 1 in present-day northeast Jordan, hunter-gatherers built with stone. Plant remains and water birds indicate a wet habitat at times. Structure 1 was partly sunken. Upright stones formed its edge and flagstones covered the floor.

The early phases of use fall around 14,600–14,000 BP. People returned after longer gaps. The aerial photograph shows part of the excavation. To understand when people built and used the structures, researchers follow the sequence of deposits.

People could therefore build and repeatedly use stone structures while making a living by hunting and gathering. Timing alone tells us little about the reasons for their choices during climatic change.

The timeline ends with the geological beginning of the **Holocene**. Researchers defined it using a Greenland ice core and give its rounded age as 11,700 years before 2000. That corresponds to 11,650 years before 1950. When people's ways of life changed remains a question for each site and its surroundings.
