# A royal headdress, a kitchen garden and a K’iche’ book

Between two buildings in what is now El Salvador, an empty space lay concealed in volcanic ash. Excavators poured plaster into cavities like this one. The casts took the shapes of plants that had disappeared long ago. Farther north, stone inscriptions at Palenque and Yaxchilan preserve the names of members of two royal families. The gardeners in the village also knew one another by name. We come to the people of these places by quite different routes. The term Maya embraces many communities with distinct languages and political histories.

## A mother hands on a headdress {#pakal}

K’inich Janaab Pakal was twelve when he became ruler of Palenque in 615. An oval panel in House E of the palace shows him seated opposite his mother. Sak K’uk’ holds out the royal headdress to him. A remarkably precise piece of information accompanies this image of an accession. Palenque’s inscriptions record the day Pakal became king in the Long Count, a calendar that groups elapsed days into successively larger units. Dates for his birth and death survive as well. Two of these records let us calculate how young the new ruler was.

The sculpture raises a question a date alone cannot settle. What position did the woman who placed the emblem of authority in the boy’s hands hold herself? Scholars have considered the possibility that his mother served as regent during the preceding crisis. Her importance to his accession is plain on the tablet. The decisions she took in the years before and after it would require other sorts of evidence. The London cast which can now be viewed online was made in the nineteenth century. The scene belongs at Palenque.

## Between the kitchen and the storehouse {#ceren}

In the same century the Loma Caldera volcano buried the village we know as Joya de Cerén. A published estimate from radiocarbon dates places the eruption between 610 and 671. Beneath the ash are the remains of wattle-and-daub houses with grass-thatched roofs. The first household excavated occupied several buildings. There were cultivated plots beside the kitchen and the storeroom. In the kitchen, which excavators designated Structure 11, there was a fixed hearth and vessels containing maize, beans, squash and chilli peppers.

Maize cobs are recognisable objects. Root crops pose a rather different problem. When the tissues of a manioc plant buried in ash decompose, they can leave a cavity in the surrounding material. The excavators examined these spaces using a fibre-optic instrument and filled them with dental plaster. The positions of casts of stems and tuberous roots in a kitchen garden tied the growing plants to a particular household. Manioc also grew in larger fields south of the village. A count of the pots in the kitchen alone would give a very different impression of the food available to the people who lived there. We have no names for those who grew and cooked it.

## A doorway remembers an earlier occasion {#yaxchilan}

On the Usumacinta River the royal family of Yaxchilan had a woman portrayed in the course of a ritual. On Lintel 24 from Structure 23, K’ab’al Xook kneels while her husband Itzamnaaj Bahlam holds a burning torch. She draws a cord through her tongue. A woven container rests in front of her. Traces of pigment can still be seen on the stone. The image called for a movement that a museum photograph taken head-on scarcely suggests. To see the underside of a lintel as you walked through the doorway, you had to look up.

There are two dates to keep in mind. The inscription dates the ritual to 709. The British Museum dates the carving to between 723 and 726. The family had an earlier action made visible in a place that people continued to enter. Identifying the woman depends on reading the writing. In the Maya script signs can stand for entire words or for syllables. The textile patterns, the posture and the torch help to explain the scene. Reading a name tells us something the clothing alone could hardly disclose.

## A book in facing columns {#chichicastenango}

Around 1700 the Dominican friar Francisco Ximénez transcribed a version of the **Popol Wuj** at Santo Tomás Chichicastenango. The work belongs to the K’iche’ of the Guatemalan highlands. K’iche’ written in the Latin alphabet and Ximénez’s Spanish translation occupy adjoining columns. The surviving manuscript may derive from an earlier text written down in the sixteenth century. Its pages give only partial access to oral performances or to the appearance of earlier writings. Even the date of Ximénez’s own copy is uncertain by a few years.

In one of the creation accounts Xmucane grinds yellow and white maize. From it comes the flesh of the first humans. A reader arriving from the kitchen at Cerén encounters a familiar plant. The excavated kernels cannot tell us what place maize held in a story told there a thousand years earlier. The book can now be read in an edition with guidance in K’iche’, prepared with help from Guatemala’s Academy of Mayan Languages. For another place where people worked closely with Indigenous languages, turn to [the Nahua scholars of Tlatelolco](chapter:life-in-the-mexica-capital#tlatelolco-manuscript).
