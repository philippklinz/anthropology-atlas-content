# Early Goths on the Vistula

A single thread survived on the pin of a brass brooch at Malbork-Wielbark. Contact with the metal altered it so much that its fibre can no longer be identified. The twist is still visible. This scrap once formed part of the clothing or furnishings in a grave on the lower Vistula. The people buried there left no inscription in the cemetery giving their language or the name of their community. The name under which we look for the early Goths comes from books written a long way away.

## What could a Roman know about the Baltic coast?

Writing **around 98 CE**, Tacitus names the Gotones in his *Germania*. The order in which he introduces them matters. Beyond the Lugii come the Gotones, followed on the coast by the Rugii and Lemovii. A reader moves from one neighbour to the next. Tacitus gives no river mouth, no place called Wielbark and no distance measured in days of travel.

In the second century Ptolemy listed a people with a similar name, the Gythones, among the inhabitants of European Sarmatia. The Vistula formed part of its western boundary in his geography. Adam Cieśliński reads the two passages alongside the distribution of sites on the lower river. He draws attention to a peculiarity of ancient geography. The river along which a geographer divided two large regions also connected people living on opposite banks. A lower Vistula location is a historical interpretation. At **100** the shaded area on the map picks out archaeological finds. Present-day Poland gives us our bearings. Some nine centuries separate Tacitus from the meeting of the Piast ruler Bolesław and Emperor Otto III at Gniezno in 1000.

## Malbork-Wielbark

People used a low sandy hill at the edge of the Vistula delta as a cemetery from about the middle of the second century BCE until the early fifth century CE. Some of the earlier graves contained cremated remains in clay vessels. Tests show that many of the vessels would have leaked. Some were probably made specifically for burial. By the third and fourth centuries, burial without cremation predominated. Pollen from rye and ribwort plantain gives us a glimpse of the fields and grazing land nearby.

The thread in the opening paragraph is recorded as a find from **grave 2012/57**. There was a remnant of cloth beneath the arch of the brooch as well as the thread on its pin. Jerzy Maik and his colleagues reported traces of twelve textiles and that one thread from nine graves. Much of this material survived through contact with metal. Counting ornaments in an excavation photograph leaves out the cloth that a brooch once fastened. Too little of it remains to reconstruct an entire outfit.

## Masłomęcz

In the late second century, sites appear in the Hrubieszów Basin in what is now eastern Poland that archaeologists call the **Masłomęcz group**. Masłomęcz and Gródek on the Bug are two of its largest excavated cemeteries. Archaeologists have also found settlements. We know considerably more about how people buried their dead than about the houses in which they lived.

Some graves contained more than one person. Were they members of a family? Researchers examined the remains of three women in grave 20 at Masłomęcz. The women had different maternal lineages. Two of the possible pairs showed signs of more distant biological kinship. What brought the three women together in one grave is still open. Recovering the relationships within even a single village poses questions of its own.

At **220**, sites appear on the map in the eastern Hrubieszów Basin while the old cemetery at Wielbark was still in use. People were still burying their dead here at **275**, by which time another group of finds had emerged in the Southern Buh basin in present-day Ukraine. Establishing whether any particular family moved between these places would take evidence of a different kind. The people who handled gold, pottery and glass far to the southeast during these same centuries appear in [the chapter on the Black Sea](chapter:goths-black-sea).
