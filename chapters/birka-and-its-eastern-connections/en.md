# A ring and a dirham at Birka

When Hjalmar Stolpe opened a grave on the island of Björkö in 1877, he found a ring just below a bronze brooch, next to a pair of scissors. The bones had long since dissolved. He could record where the ring lay, but there was no hand left to tell him whether the buried woman wore it on her finger. Cut into the violet setting are a few Arabic letters. Another find from the same island offers a different way of tracing an object across such a distance. It is a silver coin for which we can name the town where it was struck.

## Who met the monks at the harbour? {#birka-harbour}

Björkö stands in Lake Mälaren in Sweden. When a trading town grew here, these waters were an inlet of the Baltic. Land uplift has since changed the approach to what was once a seaport. Around 830 two Frankish monks, Ansgar and Witmar, reached the harbour at Birka. Raiders had relieved them of nearly forty books on the way. The story comes from Rimbert, a pupil of Ansgar who wrote his teacher’s life after his death. For this part of the journey he could refer his readers to Witmar, who had made it with Ansgar.

King Björn consulted his advisers and gave the monks leave to stay and preach. Some people at Birka were already Christians. The monks’ arrival meant that they could take part in Christian worship again. Rimbert calls them captives. They had arrived with their beliefs, with little of the freedom that had allowed the two visitors to decide whether to carry on with their journey. Hergeir, the town’s reeve and a counsellor of the king, chose baptism and eventually built a church on his own property. Rimbert supplies his name. He tells us much less about the captive Christians, whose place of origin and languages go unrecorded. Over a century later [Harald’s inscription at Jelling](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#jelling) would tell a Danish audience who their king thought deserved the credit for making them Christian.

## A needle case on one side, a ring on the other {#ring}

Around the middle of the ninth century a rectangular wooden coffin was laid in the cemetery north of the fortress at Birka. Fragments of linen and blue wool survived. The clothes and two oval brooches helped archaeologists identify grave Bj 515 as the burial of a woman and place it in time. Glass, rock crystal and carnelian beads lay between the brooches. A needle case hung on a chain on the left. The ring lay on the right. It too might have been suspended from the brooch. The woman might equally have been laid out with her hands resting on her chest.

The rounded violet setting was long taken to be amethyst. Under analysis it proved to be coloured glass. The band is silver with a small proportion of copper. Electron microscopy revealed fine marks where a file had passed over the casting. Their survival suggests that the ring had seen little hard wear. That would fit either a ring kept carefully by one person or a ring passed on quickly by several. Sebastian Wärmländer and his colleagues read the angular Arabic letters as a dedication to Allah. They suggest that, if one assumes a missing stroke, “God willing” is another possible reading. The woman buried at Birka left us no account in her own words of what the ring meant to her.

## Waiting for someone with silver on the Volga {#volga}

In 922 Aḥmad ibn Faḍlān was in the land of the Volga Bulgars on a diplomatic mission from the caliph in Baghdad. On the river he calls the Itil, the Volga, he encountered traders he calls Rūs who had come by boat. They put up large wooden houses on the bank. In his account, groups of ten or twenty might share a house. He names the river but gives us no means of identifying their precise landing. Bolgar marks the general area on the map.

One of his most telling passages is a prayer he attributes to a trader. Addressing a wooden figure, the man lists what he has brought, including sable pelts and enslaved women. He asks for a wealthy buyer with dirhams and dinars who will accept his prices. If no buyer comes, he brings further offerings. The envoy gives the seller’s hopes and disappointments considerable space. We learn nothing of what it was like for one of the women offered for sale to reach this riverbank. There is no record of an agreed price either. Rūs is the name the visitor uses for the group he describes. He does not say from which harbours their ships had come.

## A mint on a frontier {#mint}

One of the later coins from Birka can be traced to al-Maṣṣīṣa, now Misis in southern Turkey. It is a silver dirham issued there by the Hamdanid dynasty. The Swedish catalogue of coin finds places the issue between 963 and 967. The historian of coinage Fedir Androshchuk narrows it to 963/64. The mint town, known in Greek as Mopsuestia, stood on the Ceyhan River at a contested frontier. In the middle of those very years, in 965, Byzantine forces took control of it.

Either dating helps in Sweden. Only after the dirham had been struck could anyone have buried it with the other pieces at Birka. Some of the other coins were generations older. There was a Byzantine coin among them too. Androshchuk has examined how issues from different rulers came to circulate together, including the opportunities for pay and plunder along the Byzantine frontier. There are two places we can put on a map for this particular dirham. One is its mint town, the other the island where it was found. The name of its first owner and the number of times it changed hands between the two have escaped us.

## What lay on the iron dish? {#black-earth}

The finder’s name survives. On 18 September 1872 a workman called Wärner uncovered the iron dish of silver during Stolpe’s excavations. It lay in the former settlement area known as the Black Earth, just below the soil disturbed by the plough. Its precise findspot has been lost. There were arm rings on it as well as hundreds of coins. Of the coins bearing Arabic script, the excavation account counted 89 whole pieces and 360 broken ones. Androshchuk attributes a number of the imitations in the find to mints in Volga Bulgaria. People on the middle Volga could themselves produce silver currency modelled on the issues that circulated there. Putting a single dot on a map for every coin with Arabic writing would erase those moneyers’ work.

Two other discoveries help put the dish in perspective. In 1991 archaeologists found the remains of a container in which somebody had packed about twenty coins, seven weights, pieces of amber and two beads into a small space. Here were the means of weighing out a payment, stored alongside the silver itself. Some coins from the graves had been pierced or fitted with loops so that they could be worn. One coin from [Hedeby](chapter:ribe-and-hedeby#harbour-timbers), recovered from grave Bj 526, shows a ship under sail with two wavy lines beneath the hull. Coins carried pictures of boats across the Baltic. A hole or loop could turn one of them into a piece of jewellery. Such small alterations take us a little closer to the hands the silver passed through after it left the mint.
