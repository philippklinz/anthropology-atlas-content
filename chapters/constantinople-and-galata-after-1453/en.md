# A baker’s oven in Galata. Constantinople after 1453

Marya Maryana owned three houses in Galata. One had a baker’s oven. The oven entered the record because, two years after taking Constantinople, Sultan Mehmed II wanted to know who lived along the Golden Horn, who owned the buildings and what revenue they might yield. From Galata one could look across the water to the city of Hagia Sophia. What conquest meant for any one person depended in part on which shore they stood on that 29 May 1453.

## A cathedral and a family taken apart {#hagia-sophia-1453}

On the southern shore Mehmed’s soldiers entered Constantinople, looted houses and took inhabitants into captivity. The Greek Church lost its patriarchal cathedral when Mehmed had Hagia Sophia converted into a mosque. People who had been baptised here or had attended the liturgy could still pick out the familiar dome from across the water. The congregation that now gathered underneath it turned towards Mecca. The same building appears in [the history of the cathedral](chapter:lives-in-the-byzantine-empire#hagia-sophia-1453) and in [the account of the prayer niche](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#hagia-sophia-1453).

Among the captives was George Sphrantzes, a courtier who had served Emperor Constantine XI in person. In his Greek chronicle he records the emperor’s death that Tuesday. He says little about the emperor’s last moments. Constantine, he explains, had sent him to inspect a different part of the city. He recorded the consequences for his family over several years. Released on payment of a ransom, he raised the money for his wife’s freedom the following year. Their two children remained in the sultan’s hands. Their son John was executed before the end of 1453. Their daughter Thamar died of illness in 1455.

## The keys of Galata {#galata-charter}

Galata, also known as Pera, was a port on the north side of the Golden Horn, governed by Genoa and home to Greeks, Armenians and Jews as well as Genoese. Delegates, including Marchese de Franchi and an interpreter, took the keys of its fortifications to the sultan. On 1 June 1453 Mehmed granted the Genoese of Galata an *ahidname*, a pledge confirmed by oath. A copy issued under his imperial monogram survives in the British Library. Its main text is Greek. At the foot are the date and the signature of the vizier Zağanos in Arabic script. There is also an Italian translation in Genoa and an Ottoman Turkish version in Paris.

The Turkish text lists items by which a port made a living. Residents could keep their ships, provisions, mills and vineyards. Visiting Genoese merchants could travel by land or sea on payment of the customary duties. Non-Muslim subjects who lived in Galata owed an annual poll tax. Its churches could continue in use. Building new ones and ringing bells were forbidden. A church might keep its congregation while the street outside lost a familiar sound. The pledge also preserved householders’ control over the women and men they held in slavery. For such a person, a guarantee protecting the household meant that their own captivity would continue.

## A letter to a brother {#lomellino-letter}

On 23 June Angelo Giovanni Lomellino wrote from Pera to his brother. As *podestà*, he had governed the Genoese settlement until its surrender. He recalled sending men from his own household and mercenaries from Chios to help defend Constantinople. When people in Pera tried to escape by sea, the captains did not wait for everyone who had reached the quay. Ships sailed while his envoys were still negotiating with Mehmed. Some of the people left behind were taken prisoner.

The former governor also had his conduct in office to explain. He wanted his brother to urge the doge of Genoa to send an embassy. The Venetian ship’s surgeon Nicolò Barbaro, meanwhile, suspected Genoese men of spying for the sultan. Rivalries between the two ports shaped how these witnesses judged their neighbours. Near the end of his letter Lomellino returns to a piece of family news no embassy had resolved. His nephew Imperiali had been captured. Once his captors learned who he was, the uncle reported, they refused to let him be ransomed.

## What the clerks recorded beside the oven {#survey}

In December 1455 the sultan’s surveyors had practical questions to answer. Who owed poll tax? Which confiscated houses belonged to the treasury, and what rent did they bring in? In a quarter named for Zani Drapora they listed the widow Marya Maryana and her three houses. An oven made one of them a place where a baker could earn a living. Whether Marya sold bread herself or let someone else use the oven is an open question. Other entries in the same quarter include another woman who owned a house, Marya di Lankaşko, and one house standing empty.

Two documents allow us to recognise a smith. An Ottoman clerk listed Anton di Laştergo as the owner of an empty house and put him in the middle tax bracket. A Genoese notary had drawn up a deed in July 1453 in the workshop of Anton di Lastrego, whose trade it gives. The researcher Nazife Selma Somer matched the names. Marchese de Franchi, the envoy, appears again too, now living with his brother and several merchants in a house whose owner was away. Another house had been confiscated while its owner, Fabyan, was still a captive. A proprietor could have good reason for failing to return. Most children went uncounted in a register like this one. Women come into view especially when their property or position within a household mattered to the assessment.

## From Aksaray to Aksaray {#resettlement}

Across the water, Mehmed needed people to settle his depleted capital. The Ottoman chronicler Aşıkpaşazade says that newcomers were offered ownership of houses, gardens and orchards. Orders then went out to officials to send families, poor and rich alike, by force. Tursun Bey, who held office under the Ottomans, recalls a different aspect of the policy. In his telling many did come in response to the offer. Prosperous people with every reason to stay where they lived had to be summoned by name.

One Istanbul district recalls how far those orders reached. Following campaigns in Karaman, the government had artisans and other households brought to the capital from Konya and Larende. In 1471 İshak Pasha received orders to remove families from the central Anatolian town of Aksaray. Aşıkpaşazade pointed his readers to the people living in the Istanbul quarter they knew as the district of the Aksaray folk. Those, he explained, were the people İshak Pasha had brought there. Their neighbours in Istanbul thus learned the name of a town far inland. How any one family dealt with its tools, its stock or the neighbours it left behind was beyond what the chronicler recorded.

## Two shores on a single sheet {#two-shores}

On a sheet from Cristoforo Buondelmonti’s book of islands the names Pera and Constantinopolis appear on opposite sides of an inlet. The Florentine author compiled his work before the conquest. Watermarks in the paper and details in the drawing date this version, now in Düsseldorf, to about 1485–90. Its draughtsman could take account of the Ottoman city. The ancient Aqueduct of Valens runs above the houses. In a harbour on the Sea of Marmara one can make out five shipsheds and a galley. An iron gate closed off the basins where vessels could be fitted out.

A tall tower rises above the roofs of Pera. Down by the water the houses are packed so tightly that all one can make out is a cluster of little gables. To discover who lived underneath them, one must turn back to the clerk of 1455 whose inquiries in these streets extended to Marya Maryana’s oven.
