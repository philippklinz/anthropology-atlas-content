# Early Homo sapiens in Africa

Where does our story begin? This chapter follows evidence for our own species, Homo sapiens. Its starting window is approximately 350,000 to 200,000 years ago. This is an editorial window for exploration, not a precisely dated beginning of humanity. Our prehistory extends much further back.

## A familiar-looking face

Jebel Irhoud is in present-day Morocco. Researchers found human remains there alongside stone tools. The fossils combine a modern-looking face with a more elongated braincase. Different features of our species evidently did not emerge all at once. The illustrated reconstruction combines multiple fossils; it is not one complete skull recovered in an excavation.

## How old were these people?

The fossil-bearing layer contained flints that had been heated in the past. Thermoluminescence helps estimate when that heating occurred. The result for layer 7 was 315,000 ± 34,000 years, with a statistical uncertainty of 1σ. The hatched timeline bar shows the dating range. It does not mean that an individual event lasted 68,000 years.

The tools also reveal skilled work. In the Levallois technique, a stone core is prepared before a shaped flake is removed. The finds attest the technique at this location. They do not identify its first inventor.

## Volcanic ash above the bones

Farther east, in present-day Ethiopia, the Omo I fossils lie below an ash layer. Researchers chemically correlated this ash with an eruption of Shala volcano. The eruption was dated to 233,000 ± 22,000 years; here the uncertainty is reported at 2σ.

The essential relationship is that the fossils below must be older than the ash. This does not tell us how much older. Omo I therefore has a left-open timeline bar: its beginning at the chart edge is not an age estimate. The studies’ 1σ and 2σ ranges are not directly equivalent uncertainty intervals either.

## A cradle with many connections

Here, cradle is a metaphor for Africa as the region of our species’ origins. Fossils from different regions and genetic models support considering connected populations. The 2023 model by Ragsdale and colleagues investigates recurrent gene flow among ancestral populations. It does not establish their precise locations and routes for this map.

We cannot name a first human or securely pinpoint a single birthplace of our species.

The three points mark fossil sites or the volcanic system. Present-day coastlines and country names provide orientation. Past coastlines, climatic zones and population ranges are not reconstructed here. Point coordinates are deliberately coarse.

BP here means years before 1950. It supplies a fixed comparison scale, so dates do not shift every year; it does not imply radiocarbon dating.
