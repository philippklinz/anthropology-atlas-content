# Queen Thyra and the builders of Jelling

The runes on the smaller stone at Jelling run in upright bands. On its larger neighbour, they run across the face of the stone in horizontal lines. Reading the two monuments calls for a different movement of the eye and body. Their patrons took care to give their names. The carvers left traces of their own in the strokes of the lettering. A few paces away, people worked in oak, earth and turf. Enough survives to tell something of their working lives as well.

## How Gorm remembered Thyra {#thyra}

Around 950 Gorm commissioned a monument for his late wife Thyra. He calls himself king. He calls her his wife, and ends with a phrase that translators still find difficult. In the Jelling museum’s rendering, Thyra is Denmark’s pride. The runologist Lisbeth Imer and her colleagues read the phrase as a tribute to Denmark’s strength or salvation. A family tree giving just the couple’s names would miss what Gorm chose to say about Thyra’s place in the country.

At Læborg, elsewhere in Jutland, a man named Ravnunge-Tue commemorated a woman called Thyra as his lady or queen. He specified that he had cut the runes. Here, then, we know the name of a man who actually handled the tool. A shared name is slender grounds for identifying his lady with Gorm’s wife. The marks of his tool give us a second way of approaching the question. Gorm’s stone has itself been moved. Its position today beside the church locates the memorial at Jelling, though its precise place in the original arrangement is uncertain.

## A little cup in an opened chamber {#north-mound}

In 1820–21 excavators reached a wooden burial chamber inside the North Mound. Someone had opened it centuries before them. Among the objects left behind were fragments of purple silk and a little silver cup, only 4.3 centimetres tall. Its maker had gilded the inside. Two slender animals wind around each other on the outside. Archaeologists later borrowed the name of the site for an entire style of ornament illustrated by this one small piece of metalwork.

Tree rings put wood from the chamber at about 959. The excavators found no human remains inside. A man’s skeleton came to light much later beneath the floor of the present church. These two discoveries suggested that Harald had moved his father Gorm from the mound into a new burial place. No find directly connects the man under the church to the empty chamber. Thyra has also been proposed as the person originally buried in the mound. The [two women buried at Oseberg](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#oseberg) present almost the reverse difficulty. There the people themselves survive and their names elude us. At Jelling we have names and cannot securely match them to the graves.

## The son commissions a stone. Who cut it? {#jelling}

The [great stone commemorating Gorm and Thyra](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#jelling), conventionally dated to about 965, identifies them as the parents of its patron, Harald Bluetooth. Harald went on to claim all Denmark and Norway for himself and to take credit for making the Danes Christian. Two of the stone’s three faces carry pictures. A serpent coils round a beast on one. Christ stands with outstretched arms on another. They belong to a monument explicitly raised in a family’s memory. As for conversion, there had already been Christians in Denmark. A different king, Harald Klak, had been baptised at Mainz in 826.

At close range the lettering records a second person’s part in the monument. Imer, Laila Kitzler Åhfeldt and Henrik Zedig used three-dimensional scans to compare the cross-sections of the grooves and the spacing of the small pits left as the carver advanced. The marks at Jelling resemble the cuts on the signed stone at Læborg. The researchers proposed that Ravnunge-Tue had cut the runes on both stones. Gorm’s smaller stone has weathered too severely for an equally confident comparison. If their attribution holds, the king’s mother was commemorated on her son’s stone by the hand of someone who had called her his queen.

## The timber in the village pond {#palisade}

The church now stands well within the grounds of a timber enclosure covering more than twelve hectares. Carpenters set oak planks on end in a trench over a metre deep and braced the wall on both sides with round posts. At least three large timber buildings inside followed the line of the wall. The holes originally dug for their posts have outlasted the buildings themselves.

Waterlogged ground at the village pond of Smededammen preserved a section of the wall. In 2013 excavators drained the pond and uncovered a small part of its bed. Eleven of the twelve wood samples could be dated. One retained wood very close to the bark. The likeliest felling date is 968, within a possible range of 958–985. Here is a task the runes pass over. Someone chose the trees, prepared the planks and coordinated their installation along an enclosure about 1.4 kilometres in circumference.

## Four posts to carry a span {#ravning}

About ten kilometres south of Jelling a timber bridge crossed the wet valley of the Vejle River. Over roughly 760 metres its builders repeated one arrangement. Four upright posts supported a crossbeam, with a slanting brace on either side. Lengthwise timbers rested on that beam and carried the planks laid crosswise for the roadway. The posts varied in length with the ground they had to bear on. Some were as much as six metres long.

The local museum dates the bridge to about 980 and associates it with Harald. When Kjeld Christensen re-examined the tree-ring evidence, he found reason for caution. One timber retained a ring from 979. Without the bark there was no telling how many younger rings had been hewn away. He considered about 980–1010 a defensible felling range, the interval used on the map. The bridge might have expedited trade across the valley or the movement of troops. The joints in the surviving timbers give a clearer picture of the carpenters’ work than of their patron’s intended traffic.

## Where the last ring survives {#trelleborg}

At Trelleborg on the island of Zealand the final ring just under the bark survived on several oak timbers. Four from a barrier in the inner ditch had formed only a little latewood in 980. The trees were probably cut in June or July. A sample from the bridge over the outer ditch had a broader band of latewood. That tree probably fell between August 980 and May 981. Work on the two ditches can consequently be placed close together in time. The timbers cannot give a moving-in date for each of the sixteen large houses inside the circular rampart.

The four gates and the houses laid out in regular groups reveal an overall plan. The cemetery outside the rampart tells us something different about its surroundings. At least 157 people were buried there, among them men, women and children. Some may have lived in a village only two hundred metres away. The family of a child buried here may have lived outside those rows of longhouses. We can locate the place where they took their dead more closely than the place where they shared their daily lives.
