# At the Danube: refuge, hunger and war

The Danube is a thin line on the map. For the people waiting on its northern bank in 376, it was a broad river. Across the water lay Roman provinces with farms, towns and granaries. Who would be allowed across? Who would feed the newcomers before their first harvest? The answers help explain how a request for admission could lead to years of fighting.

## Lower Danube {#lower-danube}

Hunnic attacks had shaken political alliances north of the Black Sea. Some of the Tervingi asked Emperor Valens to admit them. Others made their own decisions. Roman officials supported the request partly because they hoped to enlist soldiers for the imperial army. Boats and rafts carried people across. The date 376 covers many crossings. The map uses modern Silistra to locate this stretch of the lower Danube. The accounts tell us little about which families embarked from which part of the bank.

In Book 31 of his Roman history, Ammianus Marcellinus describes the breakdown of the food supply. He accuses two Roman commanders of offering starving people dogs for food and taking human beings in exchange as slaves. Even sons of leading families, he says, were enslaved. Ammianus wrote from a Roman perspective and wanted his readers to condemn these officials. We have no account of this exchange from the families themselves. When Roman units left the river, some Greuthungi also took the opportunity to cross. Their request for admission had been turned down.

## Marcianopolis

At modern Devnya in Bulgaria, a Roman commander's dining room stood close to the city gates. Inside, Lupicinus was entertaining the Tervingian leaders Alavivus and Fritigern. Outside, newcomers argued with the guards about access to the market. In Ammianus we can follow the news of violence at the gates as it reaches the banquet. Fritigern managed to rejoin his people. Soon afterwards they defeated a Roman force. The sequence is easier to reconstruct than the month. A recent study places the outbreak of open conflict in the winter of 376–377 and the battle early in 377.

## Hadrianopolis

On 9 August 378, near modern Edirne, Valens and his army faced Fritigern's following and its allies. The emperor ordered the advance before expected reinforcements from the west arrived. The Roman army suffered a severe defeat and Valens died. Even Ammianus knew two different accounts of his last hours. He preserved both in the same book. Exactly how the emperor died remained an open question even for a well-informed Roman writer.

## Peace in Thrace

Another four years passed before the peace of 382 under Emperor Theodosius. Gothic groups were able to remain on Roman soil in Thrace. The surviving texts tell us little about who received which land, whether taxes were due or how soldiers were recruited. In January 383 the orator Themistius praised the return of farming in a speech before the emperor. His address reveals which benefits the court wanted its audience to see in the peace. To find out which field a particular family could actually farm, we would need a different kind of evidence. A little over a decade later a new leader came to prominence in the Balkans.
