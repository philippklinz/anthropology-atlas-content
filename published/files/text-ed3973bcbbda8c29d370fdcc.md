# Inventing with Stone, Wood and Fibres

A sharp stone can already be a tool. A handle makes it easier to hold in a different way. A pointed piece can be part of a larger implement. Yet often only the working end survives in the ground. How do archaeologists tell what it was attached to?

Three sites in Africa and Asia offer different answers. Our map window spans about 70,000 to 15,000 years BP. Here BP means years before 1950. These stops show traces that survive from this window. They do not trace a sequence of first inventions.

## Sibudu

In present-day South Africa, people shaped small stone pieces so that one edge became blunt. Archaeologists call it the back. White dots along the pieces in the figure mark those edges. A flat quartz piece with a blunt back and a sharp edge opposite can serve as an insert in a larger tool.

Traces of organic substances survived on other pieces from Sibudu. Their position and damage to the stones suggest attachment to handles or shafts. Chemical study of two specimens identified substances from conifer resin along with fatty components. The exact kind of tree is hard to determine. Experiments with plant gum and ochre show that those mixtures can also stick. The surviving residue is what can tell us which mixture was on a particular stone.

## Fa-Hien Lena

We move into the rainforest of present-day Sri Lanka. People made pointed implements from animal bones. Shaping marks, unfinished pieces and waste make parts of their work visible. Studies from 2019 and 2020 place early Phase D at about 48,000–34,000 calendar years BP. People returned to the cave during this period.

Look at c and d in the figure. Close-up images show damage at the ends of ground monkey-bone points. Small size, traces of attachment, damage and remains of hunted animals support an arrowhead interpretation for some of the points. A surviving bow, wooden shaft or bowstring is absent from this group of finds. Researchers therefore compare different traces. Arrows and spears can show similar wear.

## Xianrendong

In today's Jiangxi Province in China we encounter fired clay. Thick pottery sherds contain small rock grains. Traces of construction point to techniques using layers and coils of clay.

The 2012 excavation study dates the early sherd contexts to around 20,000–19,000 calendar years BP. Researchers measured bone and charcoal from the deposits. The strength of the association between those samples and sherds excavated earlier remains disputed. The authors support their age estimate with many measurements and thin slices of sediment examined under a microscope. Critics question the association and the treatment of conflicting dates.

Soot and scorch marks are consistent with use over a fire, perhaps for cooking. What people prepared in the vessels remains open. The sherds come from a sequence used by people who hunted and gathered. Firing clay did not require farming.

## Reading the traces

Compare the stops. Chemical residues help at Sibudu. Magnified traces at Fa-Hien Lena show how bones were shaped and stressed. The dating at Xianrendong depends on which samples belong with the pottery-bearing deposits. What can you see directly on each object, and which interpretation needs several lines of evidence?
