# Who opened the city gates after Manzikert?

When the people of Nicaea negotiated the surrender of their city in the summer of 1097, they wanted to know what would become of their families and possessions. The emperor’s promises determined who would open the gates. A year later in Edessa, another promise decided the fate of a city. Its terms were written down, yet the man to whom they were given lost his life. From the Seljuk capture of Ani to a sultan’s pulpit in Konya, changes of government left traces in unexpected places. Among them are the debts owed by townspeople, the language a captive learned and the names carved around a doorway.

## Ani and its creditors {#ani}

Eleventh-century travellers reached Ani on a plateau high above the Akhurian River. The Armenian Bagratid dynasty had made the city its capital. Roads linked its markets to the lands beyond the Caucasus and to trading centres to the west and south. By the time the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan captured Ani in 1064, its last king was long gone. The Byzantine emperor had taken control in 1045. When the inhabitants sought refuge behind the walls during the new siege, they had already lived under imperial governors for nearly twenty years.

The Armenian cleric Aristakes of Lastivert describes people running to the royal palace and others taking shelter in the inner fortress. They were short of food and drinking water. Families who lost people in the capture could not even bury their dead. Aristakes wrote in the 1070s for readers who were to take warning from their fate. His anger falls on prosperous townspeople whose interest charges had made them rich at the expense of the poor. The burning of the palace signified judgement upon such injustice. His lament preserves a distinction that a succession list of kings would never convey. Before the siege, among those living side by side in the Armenian royal city were both debtors and creditors.

## An emperor comes home from captivity {#manzikert}

In August 1071 the emperor Romanos IV Diogenes fell into Alp Arslan’s hands near Manzikert, north of Lake Van. The Byzantine judge Michael Attaleiates had accompanied Romanos on campaign. Before reaching his account of the battle he describes a delegation from the town. Its members would surrender Manzikert to the emperor if they could keep their property. A household appears briefly in the background of a story usually told through the choices of commanders. The ruler who could requisition wagons and feed an army could also decide whether the town’s inhabitants kept what they owned.

Alp Arslan released the captive. Writing in Armenian, Aristakes calls the sultan the king of Persia and recounts that he treated his prisoner like a brother. It was Romanos’s opponents at home who had him blinded. Several decades later the scholar al-Turtushi turned the encounter into a lesson in an Arabic book of counsel for rulers. His victor asks the captive emperor what he would have done had their positions been reversed. The exchange tells us how a writer invited rulers to think about the use of power. What the day at Manzikert settled was the emperor’s fate as a prisoner. Other bargains lay ahead before anyone could claim lordship in Nicaea or Konya.

## Nicaea listens to a charter {#nicaea}

In 1097 Nicaea, modern İznik, was the seat of Sultan Kılıç Arslan I. His family belonged to a different branch of the Seljuk dynasty from Alp Arslan. Their sultanate is known as Rūm, a name for Roman lands. The city stood at the eastern end of a lake. The unnamed Latin writer of the *Gesta Francorum*, himself on the First Crusade, noticed that people were bringing in fodder and timber by water. When the time came to surrender, he says, they asked to leave with their wives, children and possessions. He resented the protection Emperor Alexios gave them against the crusaders and also acknowledged the alms the emperor sent to the poor in his own camp.

Alexios’s daughter Anna Komnene wrote several decades later. In her account the negotiator, Boutoumites, had a document bearing a gold seal read aloud. Among those promised protection and rewards were the sultan’s wife and sister. When the Franks later asked to worship in the city’s churches, they were admitted ten at a time. Two imperial officers, it turns out, could speak Turkish with men who had left Nicaea. One of them, Rhodomerus, had learned the language in captivity. In Anna’s telling, an emperor dealing with his neighbours depended on people whose experience extended well beyond the lands he ruled.

## An oath in the Church of the Apostles {#edessa}

In the spring of 1098 the people of Edessa, present-day Şanlıurfa, faced a different choice. Their ruler, Thoros, bore the Byzantine court title *kouropalates*. He had asked Baldwin of Boulogne, a Frankish count, for help against neighbouring emirs. The Armenian chronicler Matthew of Edessa reckoned that the count arrived with sixty horsemen. The city welcomed him, and Thoros gave him gifts. Within weeks insurgents were plundering the houses of Thoros’s officers. They could hold the outer defences while Thoros remained in the inner citadel, still in a position to negotiate his departure.

Thoros wanted leave to go to Melitene with his wife. Matthew even names the place where their safe conduct was pledged. Baldwin swore on relics of the Cross in the Church of the Apostles, and the oath was put in a letter to Thoros. He surrendered the citadel. People from the city killed him soon afterwards. For Matthew, the weight of the offence lay in the fact that everyone knew what had been sworn. Baldwin took over as lord of Edessa. He still depended on the families who held office there. Sixty newcomers could hardly replace their knowledge or their connections.

## As the preacher climbed the steps {#konya}

A wooden pulpit was finished in Konya in September 1155. The names on its doorway lead back to the family who had ruled Nicaea. Above the entrance stands the name of Masud I, son of Kılıç Arslan I. The frame names Masud’s own son, Kılıç Arslan II, with his titles. Masud died in the year the craftsman finished his work. The second inscription may have been added upon Masud’s death, which would explain why two reigning sultans are honoured on one doorway.

The men whose names appeared above the entrance had other hands to shape the wood. The master signed his work at the upper platform, using a name that associated him with Ahlat on Lake Van. He gave the date as Rajab 550 in the Islamic calendar. The [joints and patterns of the pulpit](chapter:divrigi-and-the-workshops-of-konya#konya-pulpit) preserve more of his workmanship. Its inscriptions show how a change of ruler entered a place of worship. Whenever the preacher passed through the little door, the father’s name was above his head and the son’s on the frame.
