# Mahmud of Kashgar listens to people speak

A boy nudges the boy sitting beside him and calls out *ötüš* twice. His neighbour is supposed to pass the nudge on, and so it travels round the circle. It was the word the boys called out that got their game into an eleventh-century dictionary. Mahmud of Kashgar had to explain it to someone who read Arabic. For his explanation he needed the boys, the circle and the movement from one person to the next. On the same page of the modern English edition are a drinking bowl, a close friend and a word for breadcrumbs.

## A word for a drinking bowl {#kashgar}

Under *idiš*, Mahmud could have stopped after giving the Arabic for a drinking bowl. Instead he adds that the Yaghma, Tukhsi, Yemek, Oghuz and Argu use the word for any kind of pot or vessel. Ask for an *idiš* among people who use it this way and the circumstances will have to tell them what to bring. The list records the distinctions that mattered to the author. It leaves open which languages the people gathered at any particular table might speak.

For the villages around Kashgar, an oasis at the western end of the Tarim Basin, Mahmud names a distinct form of speech, Kanjaki. A word he assigns to it elsewhere, *kändük*, means a large container for flour. The Middle Persian *kandūg* is strikingly similar. Nicholas Sims-Williams points out that names for such containers travel easily between languages. A flour jar bears witness to contact. Its name alone cannot tell us whether those who kept their flour in it spoke Persian, or whose grandparents might have picked up the word from the neighbours. The account belongs to the 1070s. Mahmud gives neither a date for his observation nor the name of a particular village.

## What the moon could teach a reader in Baghdad {#baghdad}

Mahmud began his *Dīwān lughāt al-Turk* in 1072 and went over it several times. The dates in the surviving copy are difficult to reconcile. Some scholars place completion in 1074, while others date the final revision to 1077. The dedication addresses the Abbasid court in Baghdad. A scholar who read Arabic could work through the explanations to learn the Turkic expressions. Mahmud divided his material into eight books, grouping words by their letters and sound patterns. For a verb, the headword was the third person singular of the past tense. To look up an action, a reader had to know the form in which it would appear.

Under *ay* one finds the moon and the month. A verse advises people to get ready for winter while summer is good, since the months run out as surely as day follows night. It leads into a proverb. No one needs a hand to point out the full moon. Mahmud explains that one can say this about a fault already plain to everyone. More than three centuries earlier, [a scribe in the Orkhon valley](chapter:voices-in-the-orkhon-valley#kul-tegin) had set a ruler’s words in stone. Here a Turkic turn of phrase survives because a writer wanted someone reading another language to hear the rebuke in an observation about the moon.

## A city near the centre of the world map {#balasagun}

The manuscript opens on a circle spread across two pages. The fold runs through its centre. East is at the top. Long red strokes stand for mountains and an ocean encloses the inhabited earth. The country around Balasagun takes up a generous share of the drawing. The city stood in the Chüy valley, at the site of Burana in present-day Kyrgyzstan. Kashgar can be found on the map too. Labels reach as far as Egypt and al-Andalus, though distant lands fit into very little space at the rim.

This arrangement had an advantage for readers. In the part of the world from which so many words had come, they could distinguish individual towns and landscapes. The same drawing would be of little use for reckoning a journey to Egypt in days. Even the distribution of words and the geography on the page only partly coincide. Mahmud cites forms used by the Chigil without giving them a label on the map. The coloured circle survives in a thirteenth-century copy, our only witness to the earlier drawing.

## A copyist at work in red ink {#copyist}

At the end of 319 leaves a man named Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr ibn Abi l-Fath signed his work in 1266. Originally from Sawa in Iran, he later lived in Damascus. He may have finished the copy there or in Cairo. He says he had Mahmud’s own manuscript before him. There is no second surviving copy against which to check his work line by line.

The pages still show how he helped readers find their way. Near the beginning he wrote Turkic words in red, then took to drawing a red stroke over them. Headings and the livestock brands of the Oghuz groups also stand out in red. Later hands added signs for vowels. A tiny mark may consequently tell us how somebody read a word long after Mahmud’s death. Who held the pen makes a difference when we try to work out how a word sounded in his lifetime.

## An argument on a title page {#cairo}

An ownership note on the title page names Cairo and gives the year 803 of the Islamic calendar, or 1400–01. The owner was a scholar known as Khatib Darayya. Nearby is a longer note whose writer takes exception to someone calling the entire book Turkmen. He points out that Turkmen and Kipchak speakers could use words in common. Hearing an expression from a Turkmen speaker hardly disqualified it from belonging to another form of speech as well.

Who was making this case? The editors Robert Dankoff and James Kelly thought the longer note was the work of the thirteenth-century copyist. Gudrun Horn compared the letter forms with those of the dated ownership note and judged the Cairo owner the more likely author. The disagreement about words is there on the page. How many generations separate it from the copying depends on how we read the handwriting.

## Putting loose leaves back in order {#istanbul}

In early twentieth-century Istanbul, the collector Ali Emîrî bought the manuscript through a bookseller named Burhan Efendi. In one account he first saw it in the shop. Ali Emîrî himself recalled seeing it in a coffeehouse. The later editor, Kilisli Rifat Bilge, left a much more intimate account of his own work. The binding had come apart, gatherings and leaves were out of order, and there were no page numbers. Rifat would try a sequence, read across the join and move the next leaf if the words failed to follow. After about two months he judged the work complete and numbered the pages.

His edition appeared in three volumes between 1915 and 1917. The manuscript is now Ali Emîrî Arabî 4189 in Istanbul’s Millet Manuscript Library. The few lines on the game of *ötüš* depended on people doing quite different things. One thought a boy’s call worth explaining. Another set it down again in ink. A third had to work out which leaf came before it, and which came after.
