# Mongols – A silversmith in Karakorum, a book painter in Tabriz

The trouble with the silver tree in Möngke Khan’s palace was the trumpet. When the guests wanted a drink, an angel in the branches was supposed to sound it. The first bellows gave too little wind, according to William of Rubruck. The goldsmith found a solution. There was room beneath the tree to hide a man who could blow through a pipe into the trumpet. Servants outside heard the signal and poured the drinks down separate conduits. Much of the ingenuity on display at the Great Khan’s feast depended on people whom his guests could never see. There are other places in the Mongol world where we can still look over a craft worker’s shoulder.

## Bronze dust beneath the anvil {#karakorum}

Chinggis Khan’s son Ögödei developed Karakorum as a capital in the Orkhon valley in what is now Mongolia. The court could stop here on its travels. People who ran a furnace or maintained a paved street still had work to do when the khan moved on. East of a main road, archaeologists uncovered a workshop dating to the first half of the thirteenth century. The working area faced the street. Heavy timber blocks stood side by side. A square socket in the top of each had held an anvil. Dust from working bronze still lay on the wood.

A silver coin from the same workshop floor was struck in 1237 or 1238. Its mint mark names Karakorum. That dates the making of the coin. It gives no measure of how long somebody carried it before it was lost. What brings us closer to a usual day in the workshop are little pieces of bronze, deliberately cut up as stock for the next job. Other kinds of debris reveal other trades in Karakorum. Workers here shaped glass, bone and birch bark. Food had to reach the people making all these things. Archaeologists found a densely settled area and two roads converging outside the east gate. A thirteenth-century visitor remembered that this was where one could buy grain.

## Who knew someone at the palace? {#rubruck}

The visitor was the Franciscan William of Rubruck, who saw Karakorum in 1254 during the reign of Möngke. He had already heard about his namesake, the Parisian goldsmith Guillaume Boucher, at the winter camp. A woman from Metz who had been taken prisoner in Hungary had sought him out there. Paquette offered him food. Her husband, she said, was a skilled house builder. She even thought she knew which Paris bridge the goldsmith’s brother still lived on, and remembered the man’s name. For a stranger in search of an interpreter, she had useful introductions to offer.

In his account of the silver tree, William distinguished four drinks, including wine and clarified mare’s milk. A visitor might have remembered the angel. For those serving the guests it mattered that each drink arrived in the correct bowl while the khan and his company were seated. The device depended on stores of drink, attendants and an agreed signal. William’s account is the evidence for the tree. The anvil blocks and metal scraps from the excavation cannot be assigned to Boucher’s own workshop.

## Where a travelling court spent its summer {#shangdu}

In 1256 Liu Bingzhong designed a residence for Möngke’s brother Khubilai on grassland north of the present-day Great Wall. The city came to be called Shangdu, the Upper Capital. The lines of its banks still distinguish the palace city, imperial city and outer enclosure. Its plan provided for encampments and hunting grounds. A canal helped divert water away from the city. These were lasting investments in a place that the court used seasonally. Dadu, in present-day Beijing, also became a centre of Yuan government.

## Sailing from Happo {#happo}

In 1266 Khubilai sent a letter intended for Japan by way of the Korean kingdom of Goryeo. Eight years later a fleet carrying Yuan and Goryeo forces sailed from Happo on Korea’s southern coast, in what is now the harbour area of Masan. Long before these events, Goryeo had used the bay to store and ship tax deliveries. Now the authorities called up shipwrights and other workers, requisitioning timber and vessels. The same crossing looks different at its two ends. At Happo we can see the work demanded of people before the ships sailed. At [Hakozaki on Hakata Bay](chapter:court-and-coast-in-medieval-japan#hakozaki) it is possible to follow what their arrival meant for people on the other shore.

## Two copies and a box {#tabriz}

Another branch of the Mongol ruling family governed in Iran. At Tabriz the physician and vizier Rashid al-Din worked for the Ilkhans. A commission to recount Ghazan’s reign grew under his successor Öljeytü into a history of many peoples. A page from an early Arabic copy, dated 714 AH or 1314–15 CE, depicts an episode from the Buddha’s life. Shakyamuni holds out a piece of fruit. The folio can be seen in the Khalili Collections. Another part of the same manuscript is in Edinburgh.

Rashid al-Din endowed a foundation on the outskirts of Tabriz. Six of his works were to be copied every year in Arabic and Persian. He specified good paper, collation against the library’s original, a leather binding and a protective box. A reader at the far end of the journey needed to be able to trust that the scribe had not skipped a line. The vizier’s name occupies a prominent place in the record. Whether a reader ever received a complete world history also depended on those who checked the paper, compared the pages and packed the box.
