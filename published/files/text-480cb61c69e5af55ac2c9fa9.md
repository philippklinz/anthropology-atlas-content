# Who could cross the Danube? A journey to Attila

On his way to see Attila, the historian Priscus owed a dry set of clothes to the inhabitants of a small village. A storm had overtaken his party in the dark. People came out in boats to collect the travellers and built reed fires in their huts. The woman who had authority in the village sent provisions. Priscus tells us her position and the name of her late husband. Her own name has disappeared. Encounters like this one suggest a different way of approaching the Huns. To reach their king, a Roman envoy depended on people willing to row a boat, interpret a message, negotiate or make room for a stranger for one night.

## Gold for the kings and a market for traders {#margus}

The Great Morava joins the Danube at Margus. A boat here could turn off the main river into a valley leading south. Near the Roman frontier town, representatives of Emperor Theodosius II met envoys representing the Hunnic rulers Attila and Bleda. Their agreement is often dated to 434 or 435. Yet one of the envoys, Epigenes, only took up the office he holds in the account in 438. On that evidence the historian Constantin Zuckerman places the meeting in the winter of 439/440. The map places the ancient town near modern Dubravica in Serbia. The exact spot at which the negotiators drew up is unknown.

Constantinople was now to set aside twice as much gold each year for the kings, seven hundred Roman pounds. Someone hoping to sell cattle or grain might have cared more about another clause. Traders on the two sides were to do business on equal terms. Someone seeking refuge would have read the agreement differently again. The emperor undertook to hand back fugitives from the Hunnic rulers’ territory. The border could open to trade while closing to a person on the run. Elsewhere on this same river, [Gothic families had asked to cross in 376](chapter:danube-crossing-and-adrianople#lower-danube).

## Five people on an envoy’s list {#naissus}

In the summer of 449 Priscus accompanied the eastern Roman envoy Maximinus through Naissus, modern Niš. The churches of the devastated town sheltered a few sick people. Human bones lay on the riverbank. The following day a Roman commander outside the town handed the embassy five of the seventeen deserters whom Attila was demanding from the emperor. Priscus knew their number and the purpose of the handover. He had nothing to say about where the five themselves wished to go.

The woman who later fed the party after the storm was one of Bleda’s widows. The following morning the travellers thanked her with gifts including silver vessels and pepper. Further along the road a man greeted Priscus in Greek. He had come as a trader to Viminacium on the Danube, been captured when the town fell and then assigned to the household of Onegesius, a powerful man among the Huns. By his own account he had won booty in war and surrendered it to his master to earn his freedom. Priscus turned their conversation into an argument about the merits of life among the Huns and life under Roman rule. We can pick out a former captive’s biography in it, as well as a Greek author’s choices about how to tell the story. The site of Attila’s timber residence remains unknown.

## What survived the sack of Aquileia {#aquileia}

In 452 Attila’s army took Aquileia at the head of the Adriatic. The city had a river port with long stone quays. Goods arriving by sea from the Mediterranean met the roads into central Europe here. The loss of houses, stores and inhabitants had consequences even for people whose next payment depended on a delivery. The sack passed into memory as the destruction of the city. It is harder to assign the end of any individual excavated building to that single year. Life continued around the bishop’s seat. Some of the inhabitants briefly come into view again because a letter survived.

## When a husband believed dead came home {#nicetas}

On 21 March 458 Leo, bishop of Rome, answered a question from Nicetas, bishop of Aquileia. Women had married again after their husbands were taken captive. Years later some of the men who had been given up for dead came home. Leo upheld the first marriage if the returning husband wished to resume it. A woman who chose to remain with her second partner faced exclusion from communion. How a particular couple acted is beyond recovery. The weight the bishop gave to the husband’s wishes is a matter of record. Some fifty years later, [Frankish bishops would be asked to vouch for captives](chapter:clovis-and-his-bishops#vouille) in another aftermath of war.

Who belonged to the Huns whose invasion lay behind these events? A study of ancient DNA has traced lines of descent between a few people buried in the Carpathian Basin and members of the earlier Xiongnu elite on the Mongolian steppe. Family histories within the graves studied were remarkably varied. Who chose to follow Attila, who sought his protection and who entered his service as a captive are different questions. The Greek-speaking trader who greeted Priscus at court might have answered them differently at several moments in his life.
