# A horse for a book

A man in Wales once gave his best horse for a Gospel book. We know what it cost him because somebody wrote the transaction into the book itself. Its pages carried the words of the evangelists, but there was room to record the names of people who had seen land or a manuscript change hands. Entries like these survive from Wales to north-eastern Scotland. On the way, both the language in which a claim was recorded and the audience expected to honour it change.

## An island looking towards Ireland {#iona}

In 563, according to the traditional date, Columba left Ireland for the Hebridean island of Iona. Twelve companions are said to have come with him. The abbey church a visitor enters today dates to the thirteenth century. Evidence of timber buildings belonging to the early monastery came to light close by during an extension of the burial ground. The great carved crosses around the abbey were additions of the eighth and ninth centuries. The objects and buildings on that small island each have their own time.

How did the memory of the founder become something a person could carry across the water? About a century after Columba died, a later abbot, Adomnán, wrote a Latin account of his life. An early copy ends with a request for a prayer. In making that request its scribe supplied his own name, Dorbbéne. He wrote in two columns on parchment. The manuscript, probably made before 713, is now in Schaffhausen in Switzerland. An abbot on an island with close ties to Gaelic-speaking Ireland could write for a reader of Latin on the continent. A partly erased ownership note at the foot of the first page even places it in one particular library, at Reichenau on Lake Constance.

## What Gelhi paid {#llandeilo}

Wales takes us into a different branch of the Celtic language family. Welsh belongs with Cornish and Breton to the Brittonic branch. Irish and Scottish Gaelic belong to the Goidelic branch. The relationship is one that historical linguists can trace. People in the medieval records we are about to read had immediate reasons to identify with a kingdom, a particular church or the group that recognised their rights.

Gelhi bought the Gospel book from a man called Cingal and gave it as a gift at the altar of St Teilo. The entry dates to the ninth century. The church was probably at **Llandeilo Fawr**. Other entries deal with lands nearby, a clue that matters because more than one church was associated with Teilo. The Gospel text was already there. It had been copied in the eighth century. By the time the volume reached Lichfield in England, its users had made it into something of a local archive.

The story about the horse was recorded in Latin. Another entry tells in Old Welsh prose how a dispute over land was settled. Elsewhere in the same book we meet a man named Ris making a grant. There is a practical reason to look closely at its rather dry details. Someone receiving land needed to know where it ended and what dues came with it. At [Bodmin in Cornwall](chapter:kings-and-households-in-england#bodmin), people also used spare space in a Gospel book to record rights before witnesses. For the people named there, what was at stake was their freedom.

## A town by two waters {#dublin}

Two place names on the east coast of Ireland preserve different ways of seeing the place we now call Dublin. Duiblin referred to a dark pool on the River Poddle. Áth Cliath referred to a crossing of the Liffey. In the waterlogged deposits of the medieval town, shoes and leather bags survived. Spindles, loom weights and yarn tell us about the work that went into a garment before anyone could put it on. They tell us more about what happened in a neighbourhood than about the language spoken by whoever last handled a particular tool.

Another material takes the story out of town. In May or June 1042 an oak was felled in Ireland. A shipbuilder made its timber into the keelson of a longship, the beam supporting the mast inside the hull. The outermost growth ring was still incomplete when the tree came down. The evidence points to construction in eastern Ireland, probably around Dublin. The yard is unknown. To see where the vessel turned up after decades of use, open [the same episode as read from the Danish finds](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#dublin).

The [annal on the captives brought to Dublin in 871](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#captives) tells another part of the waterfront’s history.

## What Éte could give {#deer}

In Buchan, in north-eastern Scotland, an archive fits into the palm of a hand. The **Book of Deer** measures about fifteen by eleven centimetres. Its Latin Gospel texts were probably copied in the first half of the tenth century. Later hands used available pages to record, in Gaelic, rights belonging to the local monastery. One addition tells how Columba and Drostan founded the community. It reveals whose names the monastery could invoke in the twelfth century.

One grant can be dated to between April 1131 and April 1132. It names two donors, Gartnait son of Cainnech and Éte daughter of Gille-Míchéil. They gave a pledge to Cormac, bishop of Dunkeld, guaranteeing that the terms would be kept. We learn little else about Éte's life. What this small Gospel book does preserve is a transaction in which her word counted when land changed hands.
