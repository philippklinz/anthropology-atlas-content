# Caring for the sick on the Panama isthmus

In August 1919 a family planning a visit to Ancon Hospital could look up the hours in the canal administration’s weekly paper. Some patients could receive visitors on Sunday morning. Others had only an hour and a half that afternoon. For anyone who urgently needed to get to a bedside outside those hours, one person had the discretion to issue a pass. It was the nurse in charge of the section. A generation earlier, Catholic sisters had looked after the patients on this hillside.

## The sisters on Ancón Hill {#ancon-hospital}

Huerta de Gallo was the name of a tract of land just outside Panama City. The French canal company built wooden hospital pavilions on the slope, where patients could catch a breeze well away from the city’s wastewater. Dr Companyo, who had worked with Ferdinand de Lesseps at Suez, arrived with medical staff in January 1881. While construction went ahead, the company sent some of its sick to the Hospital de Extranjeros. A local charitable committee supported this hospital for foreigners, which had been caring for people on the isthmus since before the canal project.

The new central hospital opened in September 1882. On the seventeenth, Telésforo Paúl, the bishop of Panama, celebrated a thanksgiving Mass. The Sisters of St Vincent de Paul received patients from the Hospital de Extranjeros and from dispensaries along the excavations. Black women and men did much of the physical work on the wards and helped nurse the patients under the sisters’ supervision. On the Atlantic side the company also had a hospital in Colón. Convalescents could later stay in a former hotel on Taboga, an island off the Pacific coast. After the French company [went into liquidation](chapter:the-french-panama-canal-project#liquidation), the sisters had so few patients at Ancón that they found room in the buildings for an orphanage.

## A kitchen and a linen room {#nursing}

The US Isthmian Canal Commission dated Mary Eugene Hibbard’s service as chief nurse from 7 June 1904. She came to Ancón with sanitary officer William Gorgas and two other nurses. Hibbard remembered about seventy-five buildings scattered over the grounds. She picked out five to accommodate wards, washing facilities, a kitchen for preparing special diets and space for bedding. The old hospital still had patients. In a report published that February, US physician Claude Pierce counted twenty-four people in the foreign section and twenty in the canal company’s section, with the sisters nursing them.

One surviving service card records the hiring of Anna L. Doolittle. She was twenty-three when she came from West Virginia to nurse in Ancón in 1907. Historian Vilma Chiriboga Castillo cites figures for the following year. Of 205 US women working in the Canal Zone, 117 were serving as nurses. The women caring for patients in Panama City and the relatives who nursed someone through a fever at home lay outside that count.

## Four patients in January {#fever-reports}

On 20 January 1905 John Barrett, the US minister in Panama City, wrote to the foreign representatives posted there. Rumours of yellow fever were reaching his legation. Since 1 July 1904 Gorgas had officially reported eighteen cases in Panama City, Colón and the intervening Canal Zone. Three people had died. Four of the eighteen were still in hospital that January day. All four worked for the sanitary department. Barrett thought they had contracted the illness while preparing houses and yards for disinfection. He visited them. A note he added on 25 January said all four had recovered and been discharged.

Barrett wanted ships to continue leaving port with clean bills of health. Before his government could act in the streets of the capital, it also had to deal with the Panamanian authorities. On 21 January minister Santiago de la Guardia approved the sanitary regulations after amendments suggested by his office. The newspaper *La Estrella de Panamá* offered a different view from the wards. On 9 February it reported that Ancón was crowded with malaria patients and complained of the smell from the latrines.

## Water for the city {#city-water}

Panama City inaugurated its hydrant system in Cathedral Square on 4 July 1905. Engineers had chosen an old French dam on the upper Río Grande to supply the city. Local newspapers had been calling for water mains since the 1880s. For people whose houses might catch fire, it mattered how quickly the fire brigade could find water.

Many households collected rain from their roofs in barrels or cisterns. In the dry season water sellers brought fresh supplies at a price. Inside the house people kept water in large earthenware jars called *tinajas*. A dipper could only reach so far down a jar, so families often topped it up before it was empty. When inspectors looking for mosquito larvae poured out the contents, they might be tipping away water somebody had just paid for. Some residents hid their jars at inspection time. Even the fire brigade still had occasion to use the old supplies after the hydrants were installed. At a fire in Malambo in January 1906, the firefighters connected their pumps to cisterns.

When [the 2023 rainy season brought too little water to Gatún Lake](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#rainfall), household supplies again came under pressure. By then households shared that reservoir with the canal locks.

## Visiting hours in Ward Nine {#ward-nine}

An August 1919 photograph shows two rows of metal beds under the windows. Papers lie on a desk in the foreground. Farther down the room two nurses in caps stand among the beds. At the foot of the print the word “Spanish” follows the ward number in parentheses. A bed list published that month allotted Ward Nine in Section C thirty-nine places for men the authorities classed as white and foreign. The individual patients’ nationalities and first languages remain unknown.

The other five numbered wards in the section carried a separate collective label for Black patients. Throughout Section C visiting time was half past one to three on Wednesdays, Sundays and holidays. Section A, for white US men, offered several additional visiting periods. The canal administration’s distinctions extended from the [payroll on which a worker’s name appeared](chapter:caribbean-workers-in-the-canal-zone#payroll) to the afternoon a family could spend at his bedside.

## Hospital Santo Tomás in Calidonia {#santo-tomas}

In Panama City, Hospital Santo Tomás had trained nurses well before it moved to Calidonia. A call for applicants in March 1909 specified single women aged sixteen to twenty-five. Four Panamanian women graduated in 1912, among them Ángela Lasso and Débora Jaén de Patiño. More patients kept coming, and in 1916 the minister responsible asked the National Assembly for a bigger hospital.

Separate buildings with broad eaves went up on the former national exhibition grounds, with paths and gardens between them. Proceeds from Panama’s public welfare lottery paid for construction. President Belisario Porras opened the new Hospital Santo Tomás on 1 September 1924. His decree the following day declared that the nation had built it and placed it in public service. In March Porras had already told the organising committee whom to include in the festivities. The hospital’s convalescent patients were to have a celebration too.
