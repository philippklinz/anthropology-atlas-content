# Wulfila and the Gothic Silver Bible

Four small arches stand at the foot of this page from the Gospel of John. Above them the lines of writing stretch across purple parchment. Some letters still catch the light more readily than others. The arches helped a reader look things up. Someone reading a story in John could use the references to find a corresponding passage in another Gospel. The page was designed for a person who might want to read two accounts side by side. How did anyone come to make an edition of the Gospels like this in Gothic?

## Across the Danube

One early witness was a man called Auxentius. Wulfila had taught him as a child. In a letter written in Latin, Auxentius remembers his teacher taking him in, explaining the Scriptures to him and bringing him up like a son. He lists three languages in which Wulfila preached and wrote. A person who heard him speak Gothic might encounter the same man addressing a different audience in Greek or Latin. The pupil was especially keen to record what his teacher believed about God. It is to the later church historians that we owe the account of Wulfila translating the Bible.

Wulfila served as a bishop among Christians north of the lower Danube. When persecution broke out, he led a group onto Roman territory. Emperor Constantius II received them. The crossing is often dated to **347 or 348**. That calculation depends on the date assigned to Wulfila’s consecration as bishop. The point at **Nicopolis ad Istrum**, in modern Bulgaria, locates the district where the group settled near the foot of the Balkan Mountains. The sources leave the actual crossing place open. Roughly a generation later, other refugees stood at the frontier of Emperor Valens. The better-known [Danube crossing of 376](chapter:danube-crossing-and-adrianople) involved different people and had a history of its own.

## What did it take to translate a Gospel?

Working from a Greek text meant choosing Gothic words and finding a way to represent their sounds on the page. Many Gothic letters resemble Greek ones. Their order and their use as numerals also show the influence of the Greek alphabet. The church historian Philostorgius credits Wulfila with both the script and the translation. The surviving copies leave little basis for deciding which verses he phrased himself or who else worked alongside him. Another part of his work can be dated more closely. In **360** at Constantinople, he endorsed a creed that described the Son as like the Father. For a bishop, a great deal could rest on a choice of words.

You can read two words for yourself. On the back of the fourth surviving leaf, in Matthew 6.9, the prayer begins **atta unsar**. The first word addresses someone as father. The second means “our”. A few words later comes **in himinam**. The ending tells us that the Gothic word for heaven is plural here. In the digital edition you can select each word in turn. It even records where the writing moves from one side of a leaf to the next. The opening of the prayer is on folio 4v and the next petition faces it on 5r. The word for “our” turns up again on the page pictured here. In John 12.38 it appears as **unsarai**. It goes with “report” here and takes a different ending from the one it has with “father”.

## A book in separate pieces

The **Codex Argenteus** was copied in Italy in the early sixth century, probably in [Theoderic’s Ravenna](chapter:theoderic-and-ravenna). Silver and gold inks, dyed parchment and carefully drawn arches made it an expensive book. More than a century separated its production from Wulfila’s work. Portions of all four Gospels survive. In this book John followed immediately after Matthew, with Luke and then Mark closing the sequence.

Of at least 336 original leaves, 187 are in Uppsala. One more turned up in Speyer Cathedral in 1970 with a group of relics. It carries the end of Mark. When you reach that last passage in an edition today, you have read a text whose surviving leaves are kept in two different places.
