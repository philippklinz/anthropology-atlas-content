# The Panama Canal

In May 1913 two steam shovels met on the floor of the future Panama Canal at Culebra, twelve metres above sea level. They had dug deep enough. Ships would cross the hills on a lake fed by the Chagres River. For generations the river had carried people partway from one ocean to the other. One village where travellers went ashore now lies beneath the dam that holds back the lake.

## Where the muleteers took over {#cruces}

From the Spanish Caribbean port of Nombre de Dios there were good reasons to take the long way round to the Pacific. Rain could turn the direct mule road across the hills to mud. Boats followed the coast to the Chagres and went upriver to Venta de Cruces. There the crossing continued overland. In 1536 the town of Panamá built a [warehouse at Cruces](chapter:roads-rivers-and-the-panama-railroad#cruces) with forty-seven compartments where bales could wait for the muleteers. Enslaved Black boatmen lived in twenty of the village’s seventy or so houses. In shallow water they used poles to push the laden boats off the riverbed.

## The price of a crossing {#railway}

On her way from Jamaica to see her brother in Cruces in 1851, Mary Seacole could already take a train inland. At Gatún the tracks ended. Ten pounds secured a riverboat for her party, with a steersman and four crewmen. They stopped for the night when the rising Chagres sent tree trunks careering downstream. Four years later a traveller could buy one ticket for the whole crossing. The [first coast-to-coast train](chapter:roads-rivers-and-the-panama-railroad#railway) ran on 28 January 1855. At the summit, passengers had once had to change to mules. One traveller recalled that the train now waited just long enough for someone to buy oranges.

## Two ways through the hills {#french}

How much rock would have to come out for a ship to cross those hills? When engineers gathered [in Paris in May 1879](chapter:the-french-panama-canal-project#congress), Panama belonged to Colombia, whose government had granted a private concession to build a canal. Ferdinand de Lesseps wanted to keep ships at sea level, as at Suez. Godin de Lépinay proposed damming the rivers and lifting ships to a lake. A majority, including the Panamanian engineer Pedro Sosa, favoured a deep cut. At Culebra the problem was where to put the earth. Digging had to stop whenever the excavators ran out of empty railway cars to carry it away.

## A hospital above Panama City {#hospital}

Clinics along the railway sent sick workers to the [hospital on Ancón Hill](chapter:health-and-hospitals-on-the-isthmus#ancon-hospital), opened by the French company in 1882. The builders chose a site exposed to the breeze, away from the city’s sewage. The Sisters of St Vincent de Paul cared for patients here and at Colón, the Caribbean end of the line. Convalescents could recover on the island of Taboga. The hospital outlived the company, which went into liquidation in 1889. The US canal administration took over the site in 1904. Panama’s Ministry of Health now has its offices there.

## News from Panama reaches Barbados {#workers}

In 1903, soon after independence from Colombia, Panama granted the United States sweeping powers in a strip of land along the canal route. The US commission sent William Karner to [recruit workers in Barbados](chapter:caribbean-workers-in-the-canal-zone#barbados) in 1905. His newspaper appeal attracted few takers at first. Islanders remembered men stranded when the French company failed. Returning workers who could bring their wages home and say what a job was worth did much to persuade others to go. [Edgar Llewellyn Simmons](chapter:caribbean-workers-in-the-canal-zone#pay-car) made the journey in 1908. On his first day at San Pablo he followed a fellow West Indian to a freight car, hoping for a good job. He spent the day unloading coal. Later he worked the railway switches at Gatún.

## The village under the dam {#gatun}

Bananas came into [old Gatún](chapter:gatun-lake-and-the-displaced-towns#gatun) by boat from riverside farms. Growers sold some locally and sent some away by train. Where the dam was to stand there were a school, a church, a dozen or so shops and at least ninety homes. By 1908 the residents had left. Many had taken their houses apart and built them again a little farther east. Rock and earth tipped for the dam covered the old village. Nuevo Gatún stood on dry ground, yet in 1914 its residents had to move again. The US president had claimed land throughout the Canal Zone for the waterway and its associated works.

## Two funnels at Pedro Miguel {#passage}

On 15 August 1914 a photographer on Cerro Luisa watched the [steamer *Ancon* make the official opening passage](chapter:locks-and-the-first-ocean-passage#ancon). Its two funnels line up in the picture. Spectators stand on the lock walls as the pilot, John A. Constantine, takes the ship into the eastern chamber at Pedro Miguel. It had crossed the divide on the lake. Here it began its descent to the Pacific. A crane boat had already worked its way across the isthmus in stages that January. On 3 August the steamer *Cristobal* had made a coast-to-coast trial run. After the opening came the ships waiting for an ordinary passage. Six went through the next day, three in each direction.

## A flag carried to Balboa {#handover}

On 9 January 1964 students from Panama’s Instituto Nacional carried their flag to Balboa High School in the US-administered Canal Zone. They wanted to fly it at the school. They stopped singing as they passed the hospital, student reporter Guillermo Guevara Paz recalled. His schoolmates returned from the flagpole with the [flag torn](chapter:who-governed-the-panama-canal#flag). People died in the days that followed. Panama broke off diplomatic relations with the United States. When relations resumed, both governments agreed to reopen negotiations. In 1977 Omar Torrijos and Jimmy Carter signed two treaties. One set a timetable for the transfer. The other guaranteed ships of all nations peaceful passage on equal terms in perpetuity. The Canal Zone ended in 1979. At [noon on 31 December 1999](chapter:who-governed-the-panama-canal#handover) Panama assumed full responsibility for running the canal.

## Water for ships and kitchens {#water}

Panamanian voters approved a wider canal in a [2006 referendum](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#referendum). In 2016 the container ship *Cosco Shipping Panama* passed through the [new locks at Agua Clara and Cocolí](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#new-locks). Basins beside the chambers hold water for reuse. How much there is to share depends on [rainfall across the watershed](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#rainfall). In fiscal year 2024 the Panama Canal Authority recorded 2,118 millimetres against a long-term average of 2,660. It limited the number of ships going through. The same year it recorded 542 million cubic metres used for drinking water. People cooking a meal in Colón or Panama City draw on the same reservoirs.
