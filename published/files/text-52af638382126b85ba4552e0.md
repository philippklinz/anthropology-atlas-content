# On the road in the Sultanate of Rum

A scroll more than ten metres long sets out the arrangements for running an inn east of Kayseri. Among the purchases its founder allowed for were shoe leather and horseshoe nails. The shoemaker had a salary both to make shoes and to mend worn pairs. In other Anatolian caravanserais, the stonework still shows where people and animals spent the night. The cost of bringing goods into the sultanate came up for negotiation on the Bosporus.

## What duty on a cargo of grain? {#merchant-treaty}

In March 1220 Jacopo Tiepolo, the Venetian governor in Constantinople, received an envoy from Sultan Kayqubad I. The envoy brought a document sealed in gold. Tiepolo answered with a gold-sealed instrument of his own. His reply survives in Latin in Venetian register copies. Tiepolo repeated the sultan’s undertakings before adding his own. The agreement was to last two years.

Venetian merchants could calculate their duty in advance. On most goods traded in the sultan’s territory, they owed two per cent of their value. Grain was exempt, as were pearls, precious stones, gold and silver. If a Venetian ship was wrecked on the sultan’s coast, its people were to receive help and salvaged goods were to be returned to their owners. If a foreign ship was seized, Venetians aboard it and their possessions were protected too. Tiepolo in turn undertook to safeguard the property of the sultan’s subjects who died under Venetian jurisdiction until those entitled to it made a claim. The price of a mule or lodging for its load inland remained the merchant’s concern.

## A mosque above the courtyard {#sultan-han}

In 1229 the same sultan commissioned a *han*, or roadside inn, on the road between Konya and Aksaray. A traveller entering the Sultan Han faced two portals in succession. The first led from the road into an open courtyard, the second into a covered hall. In between stands a small mosque raised on four piers joined by arches. Two flights of stairs lead up to the prayer room. The arches left a way through at ground level.

In winter people and animals could shelter under one roof in the hall behind the second portal. Raised stone platforms kept the sleeping places clear of the stable floor. Only a few narrow openings let daylight through the outside walls. Further in, a raised lantern brought light into the depths of the vault. The inn also had a bath and rows of water channels. A conduit brought water from the village of Bağluca. Travellers behind the walls depended on someone maintaining a stretch of pipe in the open country.

## What the sultan proclaimed on the Alara River {#alara}

Near the Mediterranean coast, the road from Alanya towards Konya passed the Alara River. Kayqubad's inn on its bank bears the year 629 of the Islamic calendar, equivalent to 1231/32. Just inside the gate are a vaulted recess for a fountain and a prayer room. The guest quarters occupy the centre of the building, along a narrow passage. Two ranges of vaulted spaces run around them. The outer range probably accommodated the animals. The one just inside it may have housed goods and caravan attendants. The surviving walls reveal more about routes through the inn than about who slept where.

The inscription over the entrance includes Armenians and Franks among the peoples Kayqubad claimed mastery over. The Armenian kingdom of Cilicia was close by, and kings of the Lusignan house ruled Cyprus. These were names that travellers would recognise along this coast. Kayqubad had dealt with the Venetian governor as a partner in a commercial agreement. Here he had his own claims to precedence cut into stone. The inscription gives no record of Armenian or Cypriot guests staying at the inn.

## Two sultans at the gates of the Karatay Han {#karatay-portal}

East of Kayseri, on the road to Elbistan, two rulers have left their names at what is now the Karatay Han. The portal of the covered hall names Kayqubad I but supplies no year. The outer courtyard gate names his son and successor, Kaykhusraw II, and gives the year 638, or 1240/41. Inside the hall there are feeding troughs carved out of stone. Holes in the troughs and the piers provided places to tether the animals.

Neither inscription mentions Jalal al-Din Karatay, whose name the inn bears today. His part in the building work is disputed. His arrangements for keeping it running can be followed down to individual items of expenditure. In the intervening years, power in the sultanate had shifted. The Mongols defeated the Seljuk army at Köse Dağ in 1243. High officials gained influence. Karatay could take over the provision for an inn built on a royal scale while the sultans' names remained above its doors.

## Who paid for the shoes {#karatay-deed}

An endowment, or *waqf*, devoted income from villages, fields and houses to the upkeep of the han. Fifteen shops in its own village belonged to the foundation. The rent from a shop could thus help pay for a stranger's meal on the road. The foundation deed of 1247 provides for a cook, a veterinarian and a man to receive the guests as well as the shoemaker. The kitchen was to have copper pots in several sizes and a hundred large wooden bowls. Animals were to get barley and straw. The cook was allowed two hundred silver dirhams a year, the veterinarian and the shoemaker a hundred each. All three were entitled to the same grain allowance.

The deed is remarkably explicit about distributing meals. Bread, cooked meat and another dish were intended for people who stopped at the inn and those who were only passing. It expressly includes men and women, Muslims and people of other faiths, free people and enslaved people. All were promised a meal. Shoes carried a narrower condition. They were reserved for poor, barefoot travellers judged to be of good character. Poor guests who fell ill at the han were to receive medicine. These were the foundation's obligations. How many bowls the cook handed out on a particular night goes unrecorded. Karatay made separate provision for [his school in Konya](chapter:divrigi-and-the-workshops-of-konya#foundation-deed). Three centuries later, the [portions assigned in a kitchen in Jerusalem](chapter:hurrem-and-the-kitchens-of-jerusalem#portions) show how differently a founder could choose to distribute food.

## A second date at the Sultan Han {#sultan-han-repairs}

Back on the road to Aksaray, a short line below the lintel at the entrance to the Sultan Han commemorates a repair. Fire had damaged the gate. Siraj al-Din Ahmad, the administrator of the endowment, commissioned the work. There are two readings of the date. Some earlier scholars read 667, equivalent to 1268/69. More recent studies give 677, or 1278/79. Both place the repair several decades after the building went up under Kayqubad.

Three names at the entrance thus belong to three different jobs. A dedication honours the sultan as patron, a medallion names the master builder, and the single line below the lintel records the administrator. Long after Kayqubad’s death someone still had to find the money and the craftsmen to let loaded caravans through the gate again.
