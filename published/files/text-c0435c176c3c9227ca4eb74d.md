# Aztecs – Who supplied the island city of Tenochtitlan?

On a page recording tribute from Xoconochco, cacao beans, bundles of bright feathers and spotted jaguar skins sit in neat rows. There are even vessels for drinking the cacao. This page of the **Codex Mendoza** was painted in the early colonial period. It preserves an account of what the rulers of Tenochtitlan expected from a distant province. How many hands did a cacao bean pass through on its way to the lake? The page counts deliveries. Finding the people who grew, harvested and carried the beans takes rather more work.

## What a town on the lake owed {#huitzilopochco}

A much shorter journey leads to Huitzilopochco, on the southwestern shore in what is now Churubusco. Its people drew on both fresh water from the Xochimilco lakes and brackish water from Lake Texcoco. They made raised plots called chinampas on the wet ground. They hunted and farmed and maintained an alliance of their own with Iztapalapa, Mexicaltzingo and Culhuacán. From the island capital the shore might look like a hinterland. Its inhabitants knew a landscape of neighbours, political obligations and access to water.

In 1428 Huitzilopochco fell under the rule of Tenochtitlan. Goods and labour were now due in tribute. For a household there was a practical difference between delivering a share of a harvest and losing a pair of hands when its crops needed attention. The local evidence does not say what any particular family owed. The cacao page in the Codex Mendoza concerns an entirely different province. It gives some sense of how precisely the demands made of different landscapes could be recorded.

The Mexica of Tenochtitlan had themselves lived in the shadow of the city of Azcapotzalco. Allied to the rulers of Texcoco and Tlacopan, they gained the upper hand in the late 1420s. This Triple Alliance grew into the empire usually called Aztec. Many subject cities kept rulers of their own. Other communities, among them Tlaxcala, retained their independence. A single name on a map can make it remarkably hard to tell who demanded tribute and who had to deliver it.

## Two rulers on the same stone {#temple-dedication}

Signs of work survive at the centre of Tenochtitlan too. Tízoc began an enlargement of the Great Temple and his successor Ahuítzotl saw it through. At the bottom of a commemorative stone is the sign for the year **Eight Reed**, corresponding here to 1487. Above it both rulers offer their own blood. Between them is a bundle for the implements used in the rite. The carver brought two men who ruled in succession together in a single action.

A family living beside the lake might have seen this building project differently. Someone had to quarry, move and dress the stone. The museum can identify the rulers portrayed on the plaque. Its catalogue gives no name for the person who carved them. The plaque itself probably stood in the ceremonial precinct. Its date marks the completion of one enlargement of a temple that had undergone a number of earlier changes.

## Who spoke at the welcome? {#causeway-1519}

In 1519 Moteuczoma II went out to meet Hernán Cortés on the southern approach to the island city. In the account later written down in Nahuatl, people arrange flowers in gourd vessels and bind them into garlands. The ruler places flowers and necklaces on his guest. We also learn the names of some of those who came with him. The rulers of Texcoco and Tlacopan were remembered as being there. For a moment the names of the three allied cities resolve into a group of individuals.

When Moteuczoma finishes his speech the words must pass through someone else. Malintzin, called Marina in Spanish, interprets. The guest's answer also reaches his host through her. The speeches as written have at least two histories. One belongs to the people who had to understand one another that day. The other belongs to the Nahua people who later recalled and wrote down the gestures and words that mattered to them. Their account names Xoloco and Huitzillan. The map locates the general area of the reception. The [letter Cortés sent to Charles](chapter:kingdoms-and-crossings-of-iberia#causeway-1519) recalls that day for a different audience.

## Who wrote at Tlatelolco? {#tlatelolco-manuscript}

The manuscript preserving this Nahuatl account was completed at Tlatelolco in 1577. Nahua scholars, elders and painters, together with the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún, worked on its twelve books at the College of Santa Cruz. Some of the collaborators have names we can recover. Martín Jacobita of Tlatelolco served as rector of the college. Antonio Valeriano came from Azcapotzalco, the earlier power on the lake. Both were grammarians who knew Nahuatl, Spanish and Latin.

Nahuatl and Spanish occupy adjoining columns on the page, accompanied by paintings. To read about the encounter of 1519 here is also to look at the work of scholars in a city that had since been conquered. They made room for the names of the princes who came to the meeting. They also made room for the gourd vessels in which someone arranged the flowers.
