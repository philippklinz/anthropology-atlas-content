# Connected Across Long Distances

A tiny bead can raise a large question: who knew whom? People worked ostrich eggshell into small, perforated discs. These ornaments survive at widely separated sites. Their shapes, materials and manufacturing traces provide different kinds of evidence about relationships between people.

This chapter explores eastern and southern Africa between approximately 50,000 and 14,000 BP. BP means years before 1950. The map marks four findsites. The routes that people or beads took between them remain open.

## Comparing beads

Begin at Enkapune ya Muto in present-day Kenya and Border Cave in present-day South Africa. The selected layers with beads are placed around 43,400 and 42,500 BP respectively. These are rounded estimates for the layers in which the pieces were recovered.

Open the figure and notice its 5 mm scales. Compare the outer diameters of the beads with their perforations. Jennifer Miller and Yiming Wang examined measurements like these in 1,516 beads from 31 sites. For the phase 50,000–33,000 BP they found similarities between eastern and southern Africa. They interpret these as evidence of shared traditions and social contact across regions.

How strong is the comparison? Twelve of the fourteen southern beads in that early phase come from Border Cave alone. A large overall total can conceal an uneven sample. About 3,000 kilometres describes the span of the regional comparison. The measurements leave it open whether any person or object travelled the whole distance. Several groups could have carried objects or knowledge onward.

## Sehonghong

Now move to the highlands of present-day Lesotho. Here was found bead I13-170, from a context dated between 33,700 and 31,900 BP.

Strontium enters eggshell through food and water. One of its isotope ratios can be compared with values from different landscapes. Under the geological and ecological assumptions of one study, this bead's shell came from several hundred kilometres away. The exact place where the egg was laid, who carried the bead and how many times it changed hands remain unresolved.

## Ga-Mohana

The third stop lies in the southern Kalahari. A sediment layer at Ga-Mohana Hill North contained 19 beads, nine unfinished preforms and 21 other eggshell fragments. The layer is about 14,800 ± 800 years old.

Look at the figure: which pieces are already round, and which are unfinished? Piece 4082 still has a depression where its hole would be. Different stages of work support bead manufacture here. Where completed pieces subsequently travelled remains beyond what this layer can tell us.

Ga-Mohana and the comparison with other findsites document beads from the glacial period in southern Africa. Form, material and manufacturing stages each answer a different question. Together they show the kinds of traces social relationships can leave, and how much remains open between the points on the map.
