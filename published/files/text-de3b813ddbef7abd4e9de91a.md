# How Cities Grow

Look across Tell Brak today and you see a mound in a broad plain. How do we know that many people once lived close together there? The first stop uses potsherds, the second uses walls and the third uses impressions left by sealings. At each stop, open the evidence on the map and look at its date on the timeline.

## Tell Brak

A trench shows exactly what lies above and below a small patch of ground. To learn how far the settlement extended, archaeologists at Tell Brak systematically walked the fields around the main mound. They gathered sherds from set areas, identified their shapes and mapped their distribution. Characteristic vessel rims and bases can be assigned to approximate periods.

Finds from about **4100–3800 BCE** trace the occupied mound and six separate clusters around it. The estimated settled area is about **55 hectares**. The **3800–3300 BCE** finds show the clusters extending farther into the spaces between them. Their overall extent is now **at least about 130 hectares**. The two atlas markers represent these estimates. The areas are useful for comparison. How tightly houses stood and how many people lived in them are harder questions.

## Uruk

Uruk lies farther south, in modern Iraq. In its Eanna precinct, archaeologists excavated parts of a building whose construction dates to about **3500–3400 BCE**. Coloured stone cones decorated the walls around a courtyard. Their exposed round ends formed patterns. Look at the photograph of a mosaic from Uruk now in the Iraq Museum. You can still pick out the end of each cone.

Only parts of the Stone Cone Building's walls and floor survived. They show the care that went into construction. How people used the courtyard on an ordinary day remains unclear. The city grew and changed over many centuries. The map point locates the site as a whole. The timeline shows the approximate date of construction of this one building.

Open **Uruk · Stone Cone Building in 3D** to turn the structure around and explore how the courtyard, enclosure wall and earthen terrace fit together around 3500 BCE. Brown areas show the excavated remains. Light-coloured parts fill out one possible reconstruction. A modern panel in the model shows a mosaic detail from the Pergamon Museum for comparison.

## Arslantepe

In what is now eastern Türkiye, excavators uncovered a mudbrick complex dating to about **3400–3100 BCE**. It had courtyards, painted walls and rooms with large storage vessels. Archaeologists found many small lumps of clay bearing seal impressions. Such pieces were left when sealed containers or rooms were opened. The photograph from the Malatya Museum shows surviving wall painting from the complex.

The distribution of finds offers clues to activities inside the building. Where vessels, clay sealings and many similar bowls occurred together, the excavators infer regular food distribution. The idea that portions were served as meals for workers is an interpretation. The evidence shows that organised storage was possible at a smaller settlement mound as well.
