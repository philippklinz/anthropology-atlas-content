# Who could charge a fee on the bridge at Regensburg?

The earliest bridge master of Regensburg whose name we know was a man called Herbord. His bridge had been in use for decades by the time a charter named him in 1182. The charter brings us to a crossing where the city’s traffic and the emperor’s rights came into contact. How much authority did an emperor have over a bridge? Who spoke for a community? Which languages should young princes learn in order to negotiate with their neighbours?

## A bishop invites Jewish families to Speyer {#speyer}

In 1084 Bishop Rüdiger of Speyer set out terms for Jewish families settling there. He gave his own reason for doing so. He wanted to make a more considerable city of the place. Members of the community could buy and sell goods and exchange gold and silver throughout the town. The charter took particular care to mention the road down to the wharf and the wharf itself. Jewish visitors coming from elsewhere to stay with the community could claim an exemption from toll. Someone arriving for the first time might feel the effect of a local rule on their very first day.

For the residential land the bishop set an annual payment of three and a half pounds in the currency of Speyer. He granted the community land for burials. Its own head was to hear disputes in the first instance. If he could reach no decision, the bishop or his chamberlain could hear the case. Rüdiger explained that he had walled the residential area to protect its inhabitants from attack. The danger became painfully clear in 1096, when crusaders on their way east killed Jews in Speyer. Rüdiger's successor, Bishop Johann, took people in danger into his castle. A written grant makes it possible to recover what the bishop promised. The protection it offered also depended on who would act when the need arose.

## Crossing the Danube with twelve feet to spare {#regensburg}

In Regensburg, townspeople had practical business to put before Frederick Barbarossa. A stone bridge built in the twelfth century connected the two banks of the Danube. Through traffic brought customers and made the approaches to the crossing attractive places to build. On 26 September 1182 the emperor issued a charter at the request of the citizens and of Herbord, master of the bridge. Travellers were to cross free of toll. Anyone wishing to contribute towards upkeep or repairs could make a voluntary gift.

A space of twelve feet at each end was to be kept clear. Anyone infringing the terms risked a fine, half of which went to the imperial treasury and half to the bridge administration. Both the bishop and the duke present in the city assented. A small piece of roadway thus brought several kinds of authority together. The emperor could issue a binding guarantee, the bridge master had to see to the fabric of the crossing, and the citizens had taken the initiative in bringing the matter to court.

## Two women in the west choir of Naumburg {#naumburg}

Around the middle of the thirteenth century, sculptors made twelve life-size figures of donors for the west choir of Naumburg Cathedral. Uta of Ballenstedt draws up the collar of her cloak. Beside her stands her husband, Margrave Ekkehard II. Across from them are his brother Hermann and his wife Reglindis, daughter of the Polish king Bolesław. Her father's court was part of the family connections that these patrons brought to Naumburg. Her sculpted smile is striking. Whatever she felt on the day of a gift lies beyond the reach of her expression in stone. The workshop carved these figures some two centuries after the donors had lived.

The sculptors also left us a little reckoning in stone. An inscription on the shield of Thimo of Kistritz credits him with giving seven villages to the church. The people who worked their fields and paid dues have no figures of their own in the choir. The cathedral chapter kept alive the memory of those who had given the land. Even the name of the artist who directed the work has slipped away. His conventional title, the Naumburg Master, comes from the surviving work of his workshop here.

## What a gold seal guaranteed at Nuremberg {#golden-bull}

On 10 January 1356 Emperor Charles IV issued the first part of a law governing the election of the King of the Romans. Seven electors were to cast their votes at Frankfurt. Three were archbishops and one was the king of Bohemia. The successful candidate in turn had to confirm the electors in their rights. The older claim to the Roman imperial title can be traced through [Charlemagne and his Frankish court](chapter:from-the-merovingians-to-aachen).

The copy made for Bohemia still shows what gave the Golden Bull its familiar name. The Nuremberg part of the parchment book carries a gold seal nearly seven centimetres across. The second part of the book took shape later that year in another city.

## Which languages would a future elector need? {#metz}

The final chapters were issued at Metz on 25 December. The last of them turns a law of succession into something very like a school timetable. The authors expected the heirs of the four lay electors to have acquired German in early childhood. From the age of seven they were also to learn Latin, Italian and the language termed Slavic, understood in the Bohemian context as Czech. A boy might be sent away to another court or taught at home. How proficient each of these pupils became is another matter. A law book about choosing a king nevertheless ends with an invitation to imagine a child learning to address the people he might one day govern.
