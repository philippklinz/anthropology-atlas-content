# From the gaming tables of Seville to a letter for Charles

On the pages of a book made in Seville, the players sit facing one another. Between them the board has been tilted towards the viewer. Open the book and you can see the position of the pieces and work out what you might do next. A king commissioned the book. Medieval Iberia had several kings, several court languages and many reasons for putting something into writing. A vineyard in León, the rim of a fountain in Granada and the property of a Jewish community in Girona lead us to rather different readers.

## Who could seize a plough ox? {#leon-1188}

In 1188 Alfonso IX held a gathering at León. In the **Decreta**, the decisions associated with it, he calls himself king of León and Galicia. The list of those present includes the archbishop of Compostela, other bishops and magnates of the realm. Another group follows. Chosen citizens had come from the towns. The introduction takes care to name them. Its wording leaves open who did the choosing and which parts of the population they spoke for.

The decisions reward a reader with no particular interest in assemblies. Imagine someone who owes a neighbour money and is out ploughing. May the creditor simply take the ox out of the field? Article VIII reserves seizures to the proper officers and protects animals used for tillage. Another provision forbids reprisals such as pulling down a house or laying waste to someone’s vineyard and trees. The injured party should be able to bring a complaint. The distance an individual might have to travel to be heard scarcely registers in the royal promise. The modern Spanish edition compares later copies of the Latin text.

## A book for the next move {#seville-games}

Almost a century later, Alfonso X of Castile and León gave readers a view over the shoulders of players in Seville. A note at the end of his **Book of Games** says that work on the manuscript began and ended in Seville in 1283. The language is Castilian. The games it describes reached its compilers partly through Arabic sources. Men and women appear at the boards. In some pictures the king himself plays.

The book arranges the games around a question familiar to anyone who has played one. How much of the outcome can a player control? Chess calls for deliberation. The roll of a die is a matter of chance. In the family of board games known as tables, players need both judgement and luck. The volume is kept in the library of El Escorial today. Seville is its place on this map because the people who made it left their working location in the book.

## A poem beside running water {#granada-lions}

In the late 1370s or early 1380s the Nasrid sultan Muhammad V ordered work to begin on a new palace at the Alhambra in Granada. In its court twelve stone lions support a white marble basin. Look from one face or tail to the next and differences in the carving emerge. The water level is also a clue to the engineering. A device at the centre of the basin admitted and drained water, maintaining a shallow sheet across the surface.

The court poet Ibn Zamrak composed verses inscribed on the basin. They praise the sultan, his garden and the fountain at which the reader is standing. The Arabic words and the sound of the water both belong to the experience of the courtyard. To read the inscription one has to move around the basin.

## Eleven parchments in the Jewish quarter {#girona-sale}

In July 1492 the leaders of the Jewish community of Girona sold its synagogue premises to the canons of the city cathedral. The museum gives the price as 300 pounds. The community had to dispose of its common property because Ferdinand had ordered the expulsion of the Jews from Catalonia. Girona belonged to the Crown of Aragon. After Ferdinand and Isabella had brought the Aragonese and Castilian crowns together by marriage, the distinct territories retained their own institutions.

Girona’s last medieval synagogue had been established only in 1435. The Museum of Jewish History occupies the site today. In the municipal archive, eleven documents concerning the sale of the buildings and courtyards are sewn together in a roll 3.6 metres long. We can establish the price paid for the property. Tracing the subsequent lives of individual members of the congregation would require other records. [Columbus sailed from Palos](chapter:columbus-first-voyage) that same year. In Girona we can trace the meaning of 1492 for some of its families in a very different document.

## One ruler reads about another {#causeway-1519}

When Hernán Cortés wrote to Charles about meeting Moteuczoma II in 1519, he described even the removable beams of a bridge on the approach to Tenochtitlan. In his second letter he explained how the opening let water through and helped secure the city. He also described the moment he approached Moteuczoma. Two attendants stopped him when he reached out to touch their ruler. Cortés attributed far-reaching claims to his host’s later words. Charles could read the speech in the letter as an acknowledgement of his own sovereignty.

Cortés dated the letter October 1520. A printed edition appeared in Seville in 1522. Decades later Nahua scribes set down the flowers, the people and the interpreted words remembered in the city. The [account from Tenochtitlan](chapter:life-in-the-mexica-capital#causeway-1519) takes us to the same welcome on the southern approach. Cortés’s letter tells us what he wanted the crown to understand. Beside it we can read what mattered to some of the people who received his party.
