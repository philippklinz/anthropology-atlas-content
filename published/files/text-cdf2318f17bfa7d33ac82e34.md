# Crossing the Isthmus of Panama before the canal

At Gatún station, Mary Seacole got off the train in the rain. Porters seized her luggage. She climbed a bank on slippery steps cut into red clay. By the time she had scrambled down the other side to the river, her light blue dress was as red as the ground. Seacole later entertained readers in Britain with this mishap on her journey from Jamaica to visit her brother. It also fixes a moment in the history of the crossing. In 1851 the rails ended here. To go further, a traveller had to strike a bargain with people who knew the river.

## Beneath the main square of Panamá Viejo {#plaza}

When Pedro Arias Dávila founded the Spanish city of Panamá on 15 August 1519, people had lived along this Pacific shore for centuries. The earliest human remains recovered at the site date to the sixth century CE. Archaeologists have also found an Indigenous settlement farther west, in what is now Morelos Park. People caught fish in the shallows and collected shellfish along a coast fringed with mangroves. The Spanish Crown wanted a port from which ships could carry people and supplies farther along the Pacific coast.

When the newcomers built their houses and marked out a square, some familiar work continued. Archaeologists Francisco Javier Aceituno and Juan Guillermo Martín examined four handstones and a larger grinding slab from the main square and the buildings around it. Starch grains survived on the working surfaces. Thirteen could be identified as maize. Three of the tools came from securely colonial deposits, two on the site of the town hall. The finds place an Indigenous crop and a way of preparing it inside the Spanish town. Individual cooks and the people who raised their corn remain anonymous.

## Where cargo changed hands at Cruces {#cruces}

The Camino Real crossed the hills between Panamá and the Caribbean port of Nombre de Dios. Muleteers could take this direct route in the dry season. Heavy rain made the going worse for pack animals and favoured a longer crossing by water. From Nombre de Dios, boats went along the coast to the mouth of the Chagres and then up the river to Venta de Cruces. There passengers could go ashore and have their baggage put on mules for the shorter land journey to the Pacific.

In 1536 the municipality of Panamá built a warehouse at Cruces. Historian Jesús Sanjurjo Ramos describes a building with forty-seven compartments under the supervision of the mayor of Panamá. Bales could wait under cover between the arrival of a boat and the departure of a mule train. Sanjurjo also gives an account of the village that grew up around this work. Of some seventy houses, twenty were occupied by enslaved Black people who transported freight on the river. They worked wooden cargo boats called *bongos* or *chatas*, using poles to push off the riverbed in shallow water. Merchants could choose how their goods travelled. The enslaved crews had little say in whose cargo they carried.

## The terms of freedom near Nombre de Dios {#maroons}

People who escaped enslavement also made homes in the forests and hills. Spanish writers called them *cimarrones*, known in English as Maroons. One community near Portobelo was led by Luis de Mozambique. In 1579 its representatives secured formal recognition of their freedom from the colonial authorities. They rejected the marshy ground at Chilibre proposed for their new town and settled near the bay of Nombre de Dios. Historian Marta Hidalgo Pérez compared references in letters with the topography. Excavators found sixteenth-century pottery on a hill called Lomita de Piedra. Its identification with the new town, Santiago del Príncipe, remains tentative.

Luis became governor for life. The townspeople farmed and sold produce in the neighbouring port. The Crown required them to help defend the coast and capture other fugitives. A family in Santiago could call on the authorities to recognise a freedom it had already won. Someone fleeing enslavement along this same stretch of coast could still be taken back into captivity.

## When the fleet came to Portobelo {#portobelo}

On 20 March 1597 the Spanish founded San Felipe de Portobelo to succeed Nombre de Dios as their Caribbean port. Traders from Spain met merchants from the viceroyalty of Peru there. Andean silver went by sea to Panamá, by pack mule across the isthmus and back aboard ship on the Caribbean. Bolts of cloth, glass, hats and soap travelled in the other direction. A merchant concluding a sale at the fair might still have to arrange a journey by road and another sea passage for the goods.

Fleet arrivals put rooms, food and dry storage space at a premium. Provisions came from as far away as Cartagena and Nicaragua. Some fairs were over in two or three weeks. Others lasted well over a month. The same visitors who made it worthwhile for a householder to rent a room could drive up the price of supper. The far end of the route moved too. Following a fire in 1671, Panamá was founded anew farther west in 1673.

## Mary Seacole hires a boat {#boatmen}

When Seacole left Kingston for Cruces in 1851, people bound for the California goldfields meant business for innkeepers and boat owners on the isthmus. She took the finished stretch of railway inland from the Caribbean coast to Gatún. In her memoir, published in 1857, she explained why she wanted a boat for her party alone. There was all that luggage. She had also experienced too much contempt for her complexion among white travellers from the United States to welcome their company at close quarters.

She paid ten pounds. The boat had a flat bottom and a hammock slung beneath an awning. Its master steered while the four crewmen paddled or poled according to the depth. When the river rose and swept tree trunks downstream, the boats stopped for the night. At Gorgona next morning Seacole found a butcher selling meat by length, cut in long strips. She reached Cruces that afternoon. The site of [old Gatún](chapter:gatun-lake-and-the-displaced-towns#gatun), where she had hired the boat, now lies beneath the dam.

## A train from the Caribbean to the Pacific {#railway}

On 28 January 1855 a train made the first full crossing from the Caribbean to Panamá. The crews had laid the last rail at the summit the day before. Workers from the Caribbean and China were among those who built the line, begun in 1850. Forty-seven and a half miles of track now connected the two coasts of New Granada. The railway company called its Caribbean terminus on Manzanillo Island *Aspinwall*. The authorities called it *Colón*.

Thomas Harrison’s 1857 map shows what passengers could buy their way out of. The Chagres swings through great loops alongside a railway that takes a course of its own. A profile along the margin shows how high the tracks climbed. A first-class ticket cost twenty-five dollars, a second-class ticket ten. One traveller recalled the summit where passengers had once transferred to mules. The train now stopped just long enough to buy a few oranges. Decades later the French company planning [a canal across the isthmus](chapter:the-french-panama-canal-project#concession) bought almost all the railway shares.
