# Cultivation in Three Landscapes

An early garden rarely survives as a planting bed. Sometimes researchers find only traces on a grinding stone. Elsewhere they recover a seed that can be dated, or soil people moved when making planting mounds. Each of these kinds of evidence tells us something different about cultivation.

Cultivation means deliberately tending, transplanting or sowing plants. Over many generations human selection can change how plants grow and reproduce. These inherited changes are called domestication. The three stops show how evidence for plant use, cultivation and domestication differs.

## Xihuatoxtla

A rock overhang in the Balsas watershed provided shelter. There, in present-day Guerrero, Mexico, people left milling stones and tools made from stone flakes. Starch grains survived in tiny crevices on the tools. Their shapes and sizes matched maize in the research team's comparison. The deposits also preserved phytoliths. These mineral particles sometimes retain the shapes of plant tissues. They include traces of maize cobs and squash, whose rind phytoliths suggest a domesticated form.

The date comes from charcoal in layer D. Its calibrated age is **8,990–8,610 calendar years BP**. Plant residues occurred at that level and in deeper deposits. The study did not establish a direct date for the deeper layer. The finds show that people were processing maize by this time. The precise places where the plants grew and the first moment of deliberate selection remain unknown. The photograph shows part of the modern river landscape.

## Ñanchoc

Farther south, on the western slopes of the Andes in present-day northern Peru, researchers found a squash seed in a house floor. Here they dated the seed itself. Sample Beta 179512 from site **CA-09-77** gave **10,403–10,163 calendar years BP**. The team attributed the valley's squash seed finds to *Cucurbita moschata* based on their traits. Another dated squash seed from **CA-09-27** is younger. The site codes keep the discoveries distinct.

House floors, hearths and later water channels provide further clues to land use in the valley. The oldest seed determination dates that one plant remain. Cultivation could supplement gathering and hunting. Squash may already have been cultivated elsewhere, and the excavators found no evidence that its initial domestication happened in the Ñanchoc Valley itself.

## Kuk Swamp

A wetland in the New Guinea highlands preserves a different kind of trace. Pits, stake holes and small channels remained along the edge of a former watercourse. The watercourse dates to **10,220–9,910 calendar years BP**. The traces are consistent with a site people modified. Researchers debate how strongly they demonstrate cultivation already taking place.

The evidence from **6,950–6,440 calendar years BP** is clearer. Regularly spaced circular and subcircular bases are interpreted as planting mounds. Such mounds could provide more air to roots in wet soil. Abundant banana phytoliths in what was then an open grassland support the interpretation that people grew bananas there. The ditch networks belong to a later phase of use.
