# The people who worked on the Panama Canal

Marian Cunningson and Caroline Lowe sold hot coffee and bread and butter at a canal worksite. Breakfast cost ten US cents, a Panamanian named Amos E. Clarke recalled decades later. He remembered the trays the two Jamaican women balanced on their heads, and he took care to write down their names. A canal payroll would be an unlikely place to find that breakfast. Following the people who made a living around it leads to the sugar-growing villages of Barbados, a railway carriage in Culebra and a little shop where families bought fish on credit.

## Leaving Bridgetown {#barbados}

On 23 January 1905 William J. Karner wrote to the *Barbados Advocate* looking for workers. The US Isthmian Canal Commission had sent him to the island. Many families depended on poorly paid work on the sugar estates. Even so, his offer initially attracted little interest. People still remembered the collapse of the French canal company. Caribbean labourers had been stranded in Panama once already. From Bridgetown it was hard to judge how much an American contract was worth.

The first recruits to return home brought money and accounts of what it took to earn it. By 1908 the recruiting office had more applicants than places. Families walked down to the Bridgetown wharf on sailing days. Small boats ferried the men out to steamers waiting in Carlisle Bay. The Panama Canal Authority records 19,900 workers recruited in Barbados. Other people, including women and children, paid their own fares. Jamaica's authorities refused to allow the Commission to recruit there. Jamaican tradesmen who could afford the passage still made their way to Panama. Whole families who travelled under their own arrangements are missing from the recruitment figures.

## A letter about the payroll {#payroll}

On 30 November 1906 chief engineer John F. Stevens wrote to William Gorgas, head of the sanitary department. Black employees were to be transferred to the silver payroll, with an exception for certain men on contracts from the United States. For those affected, Stevens specified the silver equivalent of their existing pay. Looking at a single payday, the change might seem a matter of bookkeeping. Yet the roll on which a person's name appeared increasingly determined their access to housing, shops and other facilities in the Canal Zone.

Occupation, citizenship, hiring contract and the colour line all mattered to the officials who drew up the lists. The gold roll consisted mostly of white Americans and Europeans. The silver roll included most Black employees as well as European labourers. A letter from the commissary manager shows how little the distinction said about skill. In February 1907 he pointed out that every salesperson in the retail stores was Black. Black department heads had posted bonds of between $2,000 and $5,000. They were personally answerable for goods entrusted to them. The manager cited that responsibility when he objected to dividing his staff by colour.

## The pay car and the first day's work {#pay-car}

On 12 January 1908 a photographer recorded the pay car at Culebra. Men wait alongside its windows, others stand at an open door. The carriage had counters inside. Employees entered on one side and left on the other. The photograph leaves out what each man received and how much he might have sent home.

That same year Edgar Llewellyn Simmons arrived from Barbados. He took [the railway](chapter:roads-rivers-and-the-panama-railroad#railway) inland from Colón to San Pablo. Next morning he watched a supervisor give a pick and shovel to every other man in line. When the supervisor passed him by, Simmons hoped he might get a better job. Then a fellow West Indian took him to a freight car, handed him a shovel and told him to unload the coal. On the second day he laid railway sleepers. Friends introduced him to another foreman and his father found him a job carrying water. In January 1910 he went to work at the Gatún spillway, close to the [site of the old riverside village](chapter:gatun-lake-and-the-displaced-towns#gatun). He worked as a brakeman, then tended switches, keeping an eye on both the trains and the crane lifting buckets of concrete.

## Making a home in La Boca {#la-boca}

Emily Amelia Lewis spent five years in Barbados with a child while her partner William Edward Griffith worked as a blacksmith in Panama. In Barbados he had shod horses. In Panama he worked at the Balboa shops. She joined him in 1910. They married and lived in La Boca, a settlement for Black employees at the Pacific end of the canal. Women found work in kitchens, laundries and private homes. Selling food independently brought customers who found little to their taste in the workers' messes. Women who stayed in the islands raised children and decided how to spend the money relatives sent from Panama.

Emily Amelia's daughter Adica, born in 1924, recalled four families sharing an outdoor washroom. She bought groceries at the commissary with a purchase book. Manuel's, a Chinese-owned shop, sold fresh vegetables and fish on credit between paydays. Adica became a saleswoman and cashier herself. For twenty years she managed the snack bar at [Gorgas Hospital, formerly Ancon Hospital](chapter:health-and-hospitals-on-the-isthmus#ancon-hospital), where her older sister had died. Adica Moore told interviewer Frances Williams-Yearwood about these places in July 2020. Historian Joan Flores-Villalobos later read her account alongside census records and a newspaper photograph of Adica at work.

## A walkout on 24 February {#strike}

Ships had been passing through the canal for years when silver employees walked off the job on 24 February 1920. Support was particularly strong in Colón on the Atlantic coast. The workers wanted a seven-cent hourly raise, an eight-hour day and the freedom to join a union. They wanted dismissals investigated if challenged. Governor Chester Harding refused to negotiate an agreement on pay and working conditions with an employee committee.

His annual report put the number on strike at 15,000 to 17,000. Ships kept moving, though the commissaries felt the loss of their staff and gold employees volunteered to take their places. Many strikers returned on 2 March. Most were taken on as new hires, losing entitlements that depended on unbroken service. For somebody who had worked on the canal for years, a fresh start date on paper erased part of a working life.

## What Clarke wrote down in Colón {#testimony}

In 1963 the Isthmian Historical Society invited people without US citizenship to write about their time on the isthmus before 1915. Newspapers on several islands received the announcement. Notices went into food packages for former employees on disability relief. Entries had to be posted by 1 November. The occasion was the fiftieth anniversary of the opening, due the following year. Among the 112 submissions was one from Amos E. Clarke in Colón. He counted eighteen years as an artisan and thirty as a deckhand. He even supplied a figure for his passages through the canal, 5,330 in all. He added that the women who had carried breakfast to the workers in Bajo Matachín had long since died. Marian Cunningson and Caroline Lowe could no longer enter a contest to tell their own stories. Clarke preserved their names.
