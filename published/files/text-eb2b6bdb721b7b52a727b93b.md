# Growing Up, Caring and Saying Goodbye

How did children grow up in the Palaeolithic? How did adults live with physical impairments? Three sites offer glimpses of childhood, possible support and how people treated the dead. The stops follow these questions. The third find is older than the first two.

## El Sidrón

In a cave in Asturias, researchers found parts of a Neanderthal child's skeleton. They call the find J1. As teeth grow, they retain daily growth marks. From two sections of a molar, the team estimated an age at death of 7.69 years, with a range of 7.61 to 7.78 years.

Most of the developmental features studied fall within the range of children today. Some vertebrae, including the first one in the neck, were less mature. Different parts of the same body grow at different rates. More comparable finds would be needed to draw conclusions about childhood across Neanderthal populations.

Placing J1 on the longer timeline is much harder than comparing the child with others of the same age. Two published calibrations for another bone from the site give a broad context of about 63,300–43,400 years BP. That range places the archaeological context in time. J1's age at death comes from the study of the tooth.

## Shanidar

Shanidar 1 lived around 50,000 years ago in what is now Iraqi Kurdistan. The adult Neanderthal survived with several physical impairments. Part of his right arm was missing. Bony growths narrowed both ear canals, with particularly severe blockage at the right opening. This suggests reduced hearing.

Trinkaus and Villotte interpret survival with several such impairments as evidence of possible support from others. The bones tell us about the impairments. Whether, when and how particular people helped remains a matter of interpretation. The photograph shows the present-day view from the cave into the valley.

## Panga ya Saidi

In what is now Kenya, archaeologists found the remains of a Homo sapiens child aged about two and a half to three years. The team calls the find Mtoto, Swahili for “child”. The name used during the child's lifetime is unknown.

The pit and its fill differed from the surrounding soil. Parts of the skeleton retained the relationships they had within the body. This suggests that people deliberately placed the child in the pit and that the body decomposed there within the fill. An analysis of the pit fill yielded 78,300 ± 4,100 years. The plus-or-minus expresses the uncertainty in the estimated date of the burial.

The arrangement of bones and soil supports an interpretation of deliberate burial. What the people felt and what they believed about death remain open questions.
