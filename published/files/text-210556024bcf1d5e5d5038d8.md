# Britons – A song, a manumission and 60,000 herrings

Halfway through an English survey of land and revenue, someone entered a gift of 60,000 herrings. In Cornwall a scribe took care to record that a woman and her son had left church as free people. Both entries have something to say about living in England that a succession of kings can easily leave out. Even the game's name for the people of England, the Britons, deserves a second look. The monk Bede, one of the earliest writers we shall meet, had different neighbours in mind when he wrote about Britons and when he wrote the history of the English.

## How a song reached a monastic library {#jarrow}

In 731 Bede finished his ecclesiastical history at the twin monastery of Wearmouth and Jarrow in Northumbria. Near the beginning he took stock of the languages of Britain. He counted English, British, Irish and Pictish alongside Latin. British here belonged to the tradition from which Welsh and Cornish developed. Latin allowed him to reach clergy across such divisions. A reader trying to mark off five separate territories on a map would already run up against people using more than one of the languages inside Bede's monastery.

For a sense of what this sounded like, Bede told a story about a man at Whitby. Cædmon looked after animals on the monastery's land. In Bede's account he slipped away from a feast when the harp went round because he had nothing to sing. As he slept, words came to him for a song about the creation of the world. He found that he could still sing them when he woke. Bede recounted this in Latin and rendered the sense of the English verse in Latin prose. An early copyist gave readers another way to encounter the song. Turn to the back of the last leaf of the manuscript now in Cambridge and there, in three lines, is a version in Northumbrian Old English. For the actual words of the hymn we owe as much to this scribe as to the celebrated author.

## Two people at the church door in Bodmin {#bodmin}

In Cornwall a Gospel book acquired an additional use. Clergy wrote records of people gaining their freedom in the spaces the Gospel text left bare. One Old English entry begins at the door of the church at Bodmin. Æilsige bought a woman named Ongynethel and her son Gythiccael from Thurkil. He paid half a pound for the pair and four pence in toll to two local officials. Then he took mother and son to the altar of St Petroc and freed them. The entry names those who witnessed what happened, among them the priests Isaac and Wulfger. If anyone challenged the family's freedom, the book could be opened and their names read out.

The purchase price, fee and witnesses take up more room on the parchment than the people whose lives had just changed. Whatever Ongynethel told her son that day has slipped beyond our hearing. Historians have compared names in several entries to try to date the transaction. In particular, differing identifications of the official Maccos allow dates around the opening of the eleventh century or much later within it. At [Llandeilo, a horse provides another reason for making an entry in a Gospel book](chapter:iona-and-the-gaelic-seas#llandeilo).

## What the manor of Dunwich brought in {#domesday}

In 1086 William the Conqueror commissioned an inquiry into land and its revenues. The men recording an estate at Dunwich on the Suffolk coast could name two successive landholders. Edric of Laxfield held the estate in King Edward's time. Robert Malet held it at the time of the inquiry. The Latin keeps switching between then and now. Of two ploughlands, the sea had taken one. Meanwhile the number of recorded burgesses rose from 120 to 236. In these few lines the settlement could grow even as its land washed away.

The scribes also counted 24 men identified as French who held 40 acres between them. Beside a valuation of fifty pounds they recorded a gift of 60,000 herrings. An accountant had little reason to enter the names of those who caught, salted or counted the fish. Even the number of burgesses is a poor basis for calculating how many people lived in the town. What mattered to the inquiry was what the estate owed. The point on today's coast locates the settlement approximately. Much of medieval Dunwich now lies offshore.

## A royal letter to be read aloud {#london}

On 18 October 1258 Henry III issued a proclamation in London undertaking to uphold what his councillors decided. The version addressed to Huntingdonshire expressly included both clergy and laity. The king sent an open letter under his seal to be kept locally. An English copy for Oxfordshire survived in Oxford's city archives, while the text for Huntingdonshire was entered on a royal roll. A French text survives too. A chronicler also mentions a Latin version.

What did the choice of language mean to someone who heard the letter read? At a time when king and barons were at odds over how England should be governed, listeners needed to understand what they were being asked to swear to. The accounts even preserve a clerk's payment. In November Robert of Fulham received fifty shillings for the English and French letters sent out to the shires. For an instant we can see behind the king's announcement to the man paid to put those words on parchment.
