# From the Orkhon inscriptions to the Ottoman city

One man recorded his working days on a stone. A woman had her name put up over the door of a hospital. Another turns up in the records with three houses and a bread oven because the sultan wanted taxes assessed. They lived more than seven centuries apart. In the Orkhon inscriptions *Türk* names the people a qaghan addressed as his own, while centuries later Mahmud distinguished words by the usage of different Turkic-speaking communities. The Ottoman subjects of Galata spoke several languages, and what counted in the tax survey was who owned a house and who owed dues.

## Twenty days at work in the Orkhon valley {#orkhon}

In 732 one face of a monument to Prince Kül Tegin, in what is now Mongolia, carried his brother’s words. Bilge Qaghan addressed his relatives and the people he ruled. He warned them of the attractions of Chinese silk. On the west face the Tang emperor wrote in Chinese, calling Bilge his adopted son and explaining his share in the family's grief. On a narrow surface [Yollug Tegin left his own account of working on the monument](chapter:voices-in-the-orkhon-valley#kul-tegin). Stone and wall, he wrote, had kept him busy for twenty days. Beside the two sovereigns we can just make out the man who reckoned how much of his own time it had taken to commemorate the prince.

## News reaches a camp {#manzikert}

In August 1071 the armies of the Byzantine emperor Romanos IV and the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan fought at Manzikert, north of Lake Van. Even in the imperial camp the reports on what had happened to Romanos contradicted one another. Michael Attaleiates recounts news that the emperor had held fast and put his opponents to flight. Others reported him dead or captured. A fugitive had only rumour to go on. Just before the fighting [envoys from Manzikert had approached Romanos](chapter:manzikert-and-the-cities-of-rum#manzikert). They wanted to keep their property if they handed over the town. Romanos was taken prisoner, then released by the sultan. His rivals in Constantinople were already making their own bid for power.

## A drinking bowl for readers in Baghdad {#baghdad}

The year after the battle Mahmud of Kashgar began his dictionary for people who read Arabic. Someone looking up *idiš* at the Abbasid court in Baghdad would first come across the gloss for a drinking bowl. They would then discover that several groups used the same word for all sorts of vessels. An easy distinction to pass over after a thousand years, it would have mattered to anyone asking for a bowl. Under [Mahmud’s word for the moon and the month](chapter:mahmud-and-the-turkic-languages#baghdad), there is even a suggestion for when to put somebody in their place by remarking on the full moon. Translating involved explaining what one person might do to another with a few words.

## At the mosque and hospital doors in Divriği {#divrigi}

Visitors to Divriği could read the name of the patron Ahmed Shah at the north portal of the Great Mosque in 1228/29. To find the entrance to the hospital adjoining it on the south, they had to go round to the west side. There they could read about [Turan Melek’s own commission](chapter:divrigi-and-the-workshops-of-konya#divrigi). Its inscription named her father and called the foundation a house of healing. A later endowment deed for the mosque also names Ahmed’s mother, Fatma Hatun, as one of its donors. The mosque portal alone would be a poor guide to the women in this family. Inside, a craftsman has also left his name. The woodworker Ahmed ibn Ibrahim signed [the pulpit at Divriği](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#divrigi) inside a star of his own design.

## Who qualified for shoes on the road {#caravanserai}

East of Kayseri, Karatay laid down the obligations of his inn in 1247. Among the salaried staff in [the foundation deed for the caravanserai](chapter:roads-and-caravanserais-of-rum#karatay-deed) were a cook, a veterinarian and a shoemaker. The cook was to earn twice as much in silver as either of the other two. All three received the same allowance in grain. A poor barefoot traveller considered of good character could qualify for shoes. Meals carried different conditions. The founder specified that passers-by might eat, women and men, enslaved and free. The rents from fifteen shops in the village helped pay for what a traveller ate or carried away.

## An inn and a house sale in Bursa {#bursa}

When [Orhan built a mosque outside Bursa’s walls in 1339](chapter:early-ottoman-bursa-and-its-markets#orhan), his endowment included the Emir Han. Its windowless storerooms lay downstairs. Each chamber above had a window and a hearth. Rents helped finance the endowment. How another inhabitant used her property is recorded in [court proceedings from August 1480](chapter:early-ottoman-bursa-and-its-markets#court). Nefise owned a house with a cellar. Gülşah sold it on her behalf, while Gülbahar represented two orphans on the other side of the transaction. Both representatives had once been enslaved. The terms allowed Nefise to rent back the house and recover it on repaying the price. The recorded sale alone says little about whether her family moved out.

## A harbour and its householders {#galata}

On 1 June 1453 Mehmed II granted [a charter to the Genoese of Galata](chapter:constantinople-and-galata-after-1453#galata-charter), on the northern shore of the Golden Horn. In one surviving copy Greek text sits below the sultan’s monogram. The inhabitants could continue to use their churches. Their bells were to fall silent. In December 1455 the sultan wanted other kinds of information. His officials entered [a bread oven under the name of the widow Marya Maryana](chapter:constantinople-and-galata-after-1453#survey). It stood in one of her three houses and someone could have earned a living there. Marya may have baked herself or had a tenant who did. The officials needed to know who owned the premises, an entirely different question from who had made the bread.

## Two people to a bowl in Jerusalem {#jerusalem}

In 1557 Hürrem Sultan’s endowment prescribed the order in which food was to be served at her kitchen in Jerusalem. Staff ate before the guests of the hostel. The poor followed. At [the serving of meals in Jerusalem](chapter:hurrem-and-the-kitchens-of-jerusalem#portions) even a fraction had a visible measure. Half a ladle meant that two people shared a bowl containing one full ladle of soup. Each was entitled to a loaf alongside it. The charter provided for four hundred poor people. The scribe could scarcely foresee who would share a bowl with whom on any particular evening. The person running the kitchen could, however, look up how much bread to have ready.
