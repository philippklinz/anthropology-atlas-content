# From Toulouse to Toledo

A woman’s name appears in the record of an assembly at Toledo. Queen Baddo speaks in the first person. She has accepted the profession of faith, she says, and signed it with her own hand. The record, preserved in later copies, also names her husband Reccared and the bishops present. What brought a Visigothic king and queen together with bishops from Hispania and Gaul to debate religion in a city on the Tagus in 589? Less than a century earlier, a centre of Gothic kingship stood across the Pyrenees at [Toulouse on the Garonne](chapter:alaric-rome-and-aquitaine).

## A king permits a council

In September 506 bishops gathered at Agde, near the mouth of the Hérault on the Mediterranean. King Alaric II had given permission. Caesarius, bishop of Arles, presided. Priests and deacons had come too, representing bishops who could not attend in person. These men belonged to the Nicene church. The Visigothic court had a tradition of Homoian Christianity. Among other things, the two traditions differed in how they described the relationship between God the Father and the Son. A king and his bishops could disagree on that question and still find ways to do business together.

## Who died near Poitiers? {#vouille}

In 507 the Frankish king Clovis defeated Alaric II near Poitiers. Alaric died, and the victors took most of Visigothic Aquitaine. Decades later, Bishop Gregory of Tours wrote about the men who fell. Some had come from the Auvergne with Apollinaris. Gregory singled out members of senatorial families among the dead. Asking only about Franks and Goths makes it easy to overlook the ties these men had to particular cities and families in Roman Gaul. Gregory also says that after a winter in Bordeaux, Clovis took Alaric’s treasure from Toulouse. He gives no inventory of what it contained.

Gregory placed the battlefield at the tenth milestone from Poitiers. From which road were those miles counted? Both Vouillé, northwest of the city, and Voulon, farther south, have been proposed. The shaded area around Poitiers therefore covers several possible locations. Identifying a single field within it would require more evidence than the texts supply.

For Clovis the road from the battlefield led to the shrine of Saint Martin in Tours. [What did the Frankish king do with his victory?](chapter:clovis-and-his-bishops#vouille)

## The cost of going to court

It took generations for Toledo to emerge as the seat of royal government. One piece of evidence for what kings actually did there carries a precise date. On 24 November 546 Theudis issued a decree at Toledo on the cost of litigation. He addressed officials and judges. Bringing witnesses to a hearing could itself be expensive. The decree instructed judges to examine such expenses and regulated how much the men who delivered summonses could charge for their horses. Theudis ordered the text to be added to a collection of Roman law. Four decades before Reccared, Toledo thus appears as a place where a king intervened in the everyday conduct of lawsuits. For someone weighing the cost of making a claim, such charges could determine whether a day in court was worth the trip.

## Working glass on the Tagus

In 578 King Liuvigild founded a town upriver from Toledo and named it after his son Reccared. At Reccopolis a shopping street climbed towards the palace and church. South of the palace complex, excavators found three furnaces for working glass. The earliest belongs to the foundation phase and continued in use into the first half of the seventh century. The town that a king named for his son also gives us a glimpse of craftspeople whose own names have been lost. The finds include glass vessels, pieces of crucibles and workshop waste. Researchers have compared samples picked up in the workshops with fragments from residential districts. The two sets allow different questions about the glass a craftsperson handled and the vessels that reached someone’s table.

## Whose words survive?

In May 589 Baddo and Reccared met clergy at Toledo, including men from the parts of Gaul still under Visigothic rule. Reccared himself had become Catholic in 587. The council set the court’s profession of faith on record. Its decisions reached into local congregations, whose members were to recite the Creed together in church. Jewish families faced quite different consequences. One canon required the baptism of children born to Jewish men and Christian women. The proceedings cannot tell us what happened in any particular household. It matters, then, whose signatures are preserved. We have Baddo’s statement in her own name. The parents affected by that ruling have little chance to be heard in the document.
