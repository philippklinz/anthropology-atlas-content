# Building Together on the Andean Coast

Circular plazas and large stone buildings stand in the dry coastal valleys of present-day Peru. People filled in some of the plazas and built new ones in nearly the same spot. Others lay between the ocean and the fields of a river valley. Three sites show how archaeologists read these buildings and tiny traces of food.

## Sechín Bajo

In the Casma Valley, excavators uncovered the earliest of the three buildings they studied. A rectangular platform was extended on several sides. To its east, people built a series of sunken circular plazas. One was almost twelve metres across inside and fitted within an older plaza. The builders dismantled part of the earlier staircase. This lets archaeologists distinguish two phases in the same spot.

Look for the stairways and curved walls in the excavation photograph. The exposed features were used at different times. A charcoal sample from the area of the fourth circular plaza was calibrated to 3634–3377 BCE. A sample from a hearth on the platform gave 3018–2896 BCE. The dates help establish when different activities took place. The date when the first stone was laid remains open.

## Áspero

Move to the mouth of the Supe River on the map. Áspero's stepped stone buildings stood close to the Pacific. In front of the Huaca de los Ídolos, excavation revealed traces of an older circular plaza beneath a later one. At Huaca Alta, a fishing weight still held a fragment of plant-fibre cord. Remains of anchovies, sardines and other marine animals show how people made use of the sea.

A second clue lies in dental calculus. Researchers analysed samples from nine individuals from Áspero and identified starch grains from sweet potato, squash and maize, among other plants. Two of the nine burial contexts were directly dated. The others were assigned to phases using their archaeological context. The starch grains give clues to plants that people ate. How often someone ate them or where they grew is difficult to tell from these traces alone.

## Caral

From the coast, the Supe Valley leads inland to Caral. In the photograph, the circular plaza stands in front of the broad terraces of the Pirámide Mayor. Behind the retaining walls, part of the stone fill came in mesh bags made of plant fibre. The bags, called shicras, went into the building with the stones they carried.

Every radiocarbon sample belongs to a particular context. A sample from a layer underneath the circular plaza yielded a calibrated midpoint around 2627 BCE. A sample from the construction of the plaza wall gave a midpoint around 2237 BCE. Each midpoint summarizes a range of possible ages. Where the samples were found helps distinguish the plaza from the layer beneath it.

Marine animals and cultivated plants were also found at Caral, consistent with lives that connected the river valley and the coast. Compare the two photographs of circular plazas. Their forms are similar. The walls give little away about who organised the work or what people did when they gathered.
