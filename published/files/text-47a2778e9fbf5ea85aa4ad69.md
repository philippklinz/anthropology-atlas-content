# Farms and hearths on the North Atlantic

Tiny white chips of burnt bone were scattered across the floor of a tenth-century house in Reykjavík. Some lay along the walls. They may have ended up there when someone cleared out the hearth. In Newfoundland, on the other side of the Atlantic, fifteen flakes lay beside a hearth. Someone may have sharpened a stone edge there and carried the tool away. Both jobs left something for the excavators. Neither left the name of the person who did it. Looking closely at the ground in each place gives us an unusually intimate view of life around an ocean crossing.

## What went under the benches {#adalstraeti}

The lower parts of a longhouse survive beneath Aðalstræti, a street in central Reykjavík. The house belongs to the tenth century, long before there was a town here. When archaeologists followed its eastern wall they found a small annex at a doorway, complete with paving. They identified it as a probable porch. We can trace part of the path someone took from outside into the hall, where a hearth occupied the middle of the floor.

The excavators lifted the floor deposits a square metre at a time. They processed the soil with water and caught the heavier fraction in a mesh with openings just one millimetre across. It retained tiny white chips of bone that would have been easy to overlook. Only about three per cent of the collection could be identified more closely. Cattle, sheep or goats, pigs and horses were all represented. It would be a poor way to count the household's livestock. One bone in a hot fire can break up into dozens of pieces.

The chips had kept their positions, however. Clayton Tinsley and Thomas McGovern suggested that people had put bone in the fire and subsequently cleared out the hearth. Some pieces were trodden into the floor. Others ended up along the walls or under the benches. When the house fell out of use, its turf roof collapsed and covered the deposits. Even this little domestic chore, for which we have no written account, can be followed a short distance across the room.

## Fifteen flakes by Black Duck Brook {#black-duck-brook}

People lived at the northern tip of Newfoundland for thousands of years before Norse sailors reached it. Dorset people once camped along the southern shore of the bay at L’Anse aux Meadows. Farther east, on a terrace west of Black Duck Brook, excavators uncovered a shallow hearth. Todd Kristensen and Jenneth Curtis studied it in the wider history of the island's Indigenous communities, which included ancestors of the historically documented Beothuk.

Fifteen small flakes of the same grey-green chert lay about a metre west of the hearth. Their size and material suggest that someone had been touching up the edge of a stone tool. No finished blade was recovered here. An iron nail lay about a metre southeast of the hearth. The excavation plan fixes each find in relation to the fire. Whether the people who left the nail and the flakes ever sat there together requires quite different evidence.

Two samples of charcoal from the hearth gave different radiocarbon ranges. The outer limits fall roughly between AD 900 and 1150. The map shows that whole window. Elsewhere, on the upper terrace, bird bones included cormorant and black guillemot. They give us a reason beyond making stone tools to think about the bay. Birds may have drawn people to its shores in the warmer months.

## A nail from the bay's own bog iron {#newfoundland}

By the time Norse seafarers reached Newfoundland, families from Iceland were already living along the fjords of southwest Greenland. Written accounts place the migration of one group with Erik the Red around 985. The areas known as the Eastern and Western Settlements both lay on Greenland’s western side.

At the Norse camp at L’Anse aux Meadows the remains of turf walls still marked out the buildings. In the cooking pit of one house the excavators found a bronze ring-headed pin for fastening a cloak. Another building yielded a stone lamp and a spindle whorl. This small weight gave a hand spindle momentum as the spinner drew out the fibres. Clothing for a crossing meant work ashore, and somebody had brought along the tools to do it.

Iron could be made here as well. Tests on a nail and on slag, the waste left by smelting, traced the iron to local bog ore. A considerable number of ship nails and pieces of worked wood fit the interpretation that people repaired boats at the camp. Three of the timbers preserve the year 1021. [The ship-timber chapter follows the dated rings to their outer edge](chapter:viking-ships-and-settlements#newfoundland). We have no equally precise calendar date for the cloak pin or for the hearth across the brook.

The Icelandic Vinland sagas give the voyagers names. They differ even on how many expeditions were planned. The Saga of Erik the Red tells of one and the Saga of the Greenlanders of four. Their surviving manuscripts were written centuries after the houses stood here. The whorl can bring its user's work close to us. There is no way of putting it in the hand of a particular traveller named in a saga.

## Cattle, goats and a sieve at the Farm Beneath the Sand {#farm-beneath-the-sand}

In western Greenland a farm stood by a broad floodplain inland from a fjord. Sand and gravel later covered its buildings, hence its modern Danish name, Gården under Sandet, the Farm Beneath the Sand. Work left its mark even in the fields outside the houses. Two laboratories investigated animal traces in a soil core. In a sample dated to about 1305–1365, the second laboratory independently identified DNA from cattle, sheep and goats. The result tells us which livestock were represented there. Herd sizes are a different question.

Bones from the farm suggest what people kept their animals for. Inger Bødker Enghoff examined, among other things, the eruption and wear of cattle teeth. The ages of the animals point to dairying. The inhabitants also relied on seals across successive generations. Harbour seal predominated among the identifiable seals in the earlier deposits, then harp seal in the late phase. Even an inland farm had ties to the outer coast.

What about fish? Fish bones turned up only in the sieved samples. Many were poorly preserved, so a decision made during excavation had a great deal to do with whether fishing would be visible at all. Arctic char was the commonest identifiable fish, followed by cod and capelin. The 166 fish bones tell us that work on fresh or coastal waters mattered here. Turning them into fixed proportions of the family's diet would ask too much of this sample.

## A wedding and its witnesses {#hvalsey}

The history of these farms runs far beyond the Viking Age. At Qaqortukulooq in southern Greenland stands the ruined church of Hvalsey. Its tall chancel window has a rounded arch in the east gable. The other windows have flat stones across their tops. In September 1408 Sigríður Björnsdóttir and Þorsteinn Ólafsson were married here. We know their names because a question about that day subsequently mattered in Iceland. Had the marriage been duly contracted?

In 1409 two clergymen, Eindriði Andrésson and Páll Hallvarðsson, set their seals to a declaration at Garðar. They had proclaimed the couple's intention to marry on three Sundays in the hearing of local people and visitors from abroad. In Iceland in 1414 four men testified to having been present at the wedding. A kinsman of the bride made a further declaration in 1424. The ceremony thus had an unusually long life on paper, in more than one country. The couple returned to Iceland. The people who stayed in Greenland slip out of view in these letters. Archaeological finds and radiocarbon dates place inhabitants in the Eastern Settlement as late as the middle of the fifteenth century.
