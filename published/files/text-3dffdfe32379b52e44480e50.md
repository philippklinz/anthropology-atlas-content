# Who governed the Panama Canal?

Guillermo Guevara Paz wanted to know why the only flag flying outside a school on Panamanian soil was that of the United States. On 8 January 1964 he went to ask the students keeping watch over it. In pursuing a story for his school newspaper, he had crossed a boundary. His Instituto Nacional answered to Panama. Balboa High School, a short walk away, stood in the US-administered Canal Zone. The distinction between them went back to a phrase two men had put their names to in Washington sixty years earlier.

## A city declares its independence {#independence}

On 4 November 1903 residents, clergy and representatives of public bodies gathered in Cathedral Square in Panama City. Notices posted and handed out around town had invited them there in the name of the city council, chaired by Demetrio H. Brid. Supporters of independence had declared a break with Colombia the day before. The council now committed its decision to paper. Its act invited the other towns of the former Colombian department to join and entrusted José Agustín Arango, Federico Boyd and Tomás Arias with the provisional government. The local *Star and Herald* still expressed doubts about Colón. The declaration was signed in that port on 5 November.

Colombians heard a different claim to independence in the canal dispute. Earlier that year their Senate had rejected an agreement with Washington over US rights following the failure of the [French canal venture](chapter:the-french-panama-canal-project#concession). The Colombian envoy Rafael Reyes later argued that his country's constitution barred the surrender of sovereign rights over national territory. Its government, he said, had been willing to negotiate again. In his account, the US Navy's intervention to keep Colombian forces from reaching Panama had been decisive for the outcome.

## Two signatures and ten miles of land {#zone-treaty}

Panama's new government appointed Philippe Bunau-Varilla, a French engineer who had worked on the canal, to represent it. He and US Secretary of State John Hay signed their convention in Washington on 18 November 1903. Panama ratified it on 2 December. It took effect with the exchange of ratifications on 26 February 1904.

Under Article I the United States guaranteed Panama's independence. Under Article II Panama granted the United States the perpetual use, occupation and control of a strip extending five miles either side of the future canal's centre line. The two cities of Panama and Colón and their adjoining harbours were expressly excepted. Article III gave the United States the powers it would have if sovereign within the zone and excluded Panama from exercising them there. Panama could claim the land as part of the republic while American authorities governed it. Some powers also reached across the line on the map. Under Article VII the United States would pay for water supply and sewerage in the two cities and could recover the cost through charges to users.

## A school reporter and a torn flag {#flag}

The Balboa students Guevara Paz interviewed wanted their flag in front of their school. They had no objection to another beside it, he told his schoolmates. On 9 January 1964 students from the Instituto Nacional set out for Balboa with a Panamanian flag. Francisco Díaz, secretary of their student association, had secured written permission from the headmaster and received the flag from him. It had been carried at earlier protests. Passing Gorgas Hospital, the young people fell silent so their singing would not disturb the patients.

Guevara Paz spoke some English and was able to talk to the police. As a small delegation approached the flagpole he lost sight of them. They came back with the flag torn. For what happened in the crowd his newspaper account explicitly turns to the testimony of Carranza, the standard bearer. People on both sides died in the days that followed. Panama's foreign minister Galileo Solís recalled the ambassador from Washington and broke off diplomatic relations. In April 1964 the two governments restored relations and agreed to discuss the causes of the conflict. In September 1965 Presidents Marco Robles and Lyndon B. Johnson jointly announced that their governments were negotiating a replacement for the 1903 treaty. The Instituto Nacional preserved the flag. Half a century later visitors to the former Balboa school could see an object whose history the students themselves had helped to record.

## Two treaties on the ballot {#treaties}

On 7 September 1977 Omar Torrijos, head of Panama's government, and US President Jimmy Carter signed two treaties at the Organization of American States in Washington. The canal treaty would end US control in stages. The separate neutrality treaty committed Panama to keep the passage open for the peaceful transit of ships of all nations on equal terms. That commitment would outlast the transfer itself.

At the signing Torrijos voiced a reservation of his own. He feared future governments might use the neutrality agreement as grounds for lasting intervention in Panamanian affairs. At home, opponents questioned the short time allowed for debate and the prospect of being bound by the neutrality treaty in perpetuity. The National Guard controlled the media. On 23 October, forty-six days after the signing, voters could choose a yes or a no to the pair of treaties together. About two thirds of the valid votes favoured approval. The US Senate consented to each treaty separately in 1978.

## The last postmark at Cristóbal {#transition}

A green twenty-five-cent stamp on an envelope from the port of Cristóbal still bears the words Canal Zone. The round postmark is dated 30 September 1979. Another impression marks the last day of the zone's postal service. The treaties took effect the following day. Under Article XI Panama resumed full jurisdiction over the former zone. Transitional provisions governed the jurisdiction of the courts in certain cases for another thirty months.

The Canal Zone postmark disappeared before American management of the waterway did. A US government agency, the Panama Canal Commission, ran operations until the transfer. Five of its nine board members were Americans and Panama nominated four Panamanians. Article III prescribed a succession for its chief administrator. A US citizen would serve first, with a Panamanian deputy. From 1990 a Panamanian was to head the agency. Training Panamanians in the administrative and operational trades also formed part of the preparations for the handover.

## Two ceremonies and an hour set by treaty {#handover}

On 14 December 1999 President Mireya Moscoso and former President Carter exchanged diplomatic notes at Miraflores Locks before international delegations. Article II of the canal treaty still determined the hour at which responsibility changed hands. On 31 December a crowd gathered on the steps of the Administration Building at Balboa. A large clock counted down the time. Louis Caldera, chairman of the Canal Commission, represented the United States. At noon, Panama time, the republic assumed full responsibility for running the canal.

Panama had amended its constitution and set up an autonomous canal authority. Its staff took charge of a waterway whose future Panamanian law now placed in the hands of its citizens. When [canal enlargement came to a vote](chapter:a-wider-canal-and-its-fresh-water#referendum), they could decide on the building project. Their parents and grandparents had first had to settle who could make such a decision at all.
