# Inside the workshops and foundations of Konya and Divriği

Two men named Ahmed meet on the wooden pulpit of the Great Mosque in Divriği. One had the authority to commission it. The other knew how to fit his signature into a carved star, where it can still be read. At the hospital next door a woman had the building erected in her own name. Beyond the town walls, the people who worked the fields sustained the mosque long after the carvers had gone.

## Two sultans on a pulpit door {#konya-pulpit}

In 1155 a master from Ahlat finished the pulpit that stands in the Alâeddin Mosque on the hill above central Konya. A *minbar* has a doorway, steps and a raised place from which to preach. Its solid sides look like interlacing ribbons. Each piece of wood was cut to a geometric shape to fit into a pattern with its neighbours. Smaller patterns were carved in relief on each exposed face. At the stair rail the wood gives way to an open lattice.

Above the door an Arabic inscription in angular Kufic script names the Seljuk sultan Mesud I. His son Kılıç Arslan II appears on the moulding around the entrance. A Quranic verse in the pediment asks to whom sovereignty will belong on the Day of Judgement. The art historian Semavi Eyice suggested that work began under the father and a tribute to the son was added at the succession. A separate inscription high on the pulpit dates its completion to Rajab in the year 550 of the Islamic calendar. Sultans were still rebuilding the surrounding mosque in the early thirteenth century.

The same [pulpit in Konya in 1155](chapter:manzikert-and-the-cities-of-rum#konya) also belongs to the story of a city under Seljuk rule.

## Turan Melek names her hospital {#divrigi}

At Divriği, far to the east, two members of the Mengücekid house commissioned adjoining buildings in the same year. On the north portal of the Great Mosque, an inscription records the order of Ahmed Shah, son of Süleyman Shah, in AH 626, or 1228–29. Higher up on this entrance, another inscription honours the reigning Seljuk sultan Kayqubad I. At this door the [local prince](chapter:turkic-dynasties-in-anatolia#divrigi) could claim the foundation and acknowledge a more powerful sovereign.

The hospital adjoins the mosque to the south and has its own entrance from the west. Above the doorway Turan Melek names her father, Bahram Shah, gives the same year and identifies the institution she had ordered. The Arabic term *dār al-shifāʾ* means a house of healing. Someone entering from this side reached a covered court with three halls that opened onto it. Parts of the building rose to a second storey. The plan tells us little about which rooms held patients or who treated them. Turan Melek’s own public role is clear from the stone.

## A signature in a twelve-pointed star {#divrigi-minbar}

More than a decade after the date on the portals, Ahmed Shah ordered a wooden pulpit. His name and the year AH 638, which overlaps 1240 and 1241, appear along the lower edge of the balustrade. The other Ahmed can be found lower down on the east side. He signed inside a twelve-pointed star which identifies him as the son of Ibrahim and associates him with Tiflis, modern Tbilisi in Georgia. His route to Divriği and the source of his timber are unknown.

Leaves and interlaces share the woodwork with prayers and traditions about the Prophet. The carver cut deeply enough to leave the plant forms standing out in rounded relief. In places he cut right through the wood. From a distance his small signature dissolves into the pattern, while anyone standing to preach at the head of the stairs was plainly visible. A different craftsman left his name in the fabric of the mosque. On the dome before the prayer niche, Hürrem Shah, son of Muğis, identified himself by a name linking him to Ahlat on Lake Van. Two signatures connect the building to two quite different towns.

## Fatma Hatun and the village harvests {#mosque-deed}

In July 1243 Ahmed Shah and his mother Fatma Hatun registered an endowment for the mosque with the judge of Divriği. The surviving text assigns rights in the revenues of the villages of Bahtiyar and Savrun, among other settlements and stretches of cultivated land. For families working beyond the town, the deed set aside part of what their fields produced each year. The mosque’s upkeep would continue to make demands on the countryside.

The document runs to twenty-two lines in Arabic. Scholars disagree on whether the surviving sheet is the original or an abridged copy made centuries later. Fatma Hatun appears in it as a co-founder. Her name occurs on none of the building inscriptions. At the neighbouring hospital it is the donor’s inscription that survives, while the original endowment deed is lost. As a result, we can say more about the villages that sustained the mosque than about the resources which paid for the treatment of patients in Turan Melek’s hospital.

## A school under a tiled dome {#konya}

Back in Konya, at the foot of the hill, the Seljuk statesman Jalal al-Din Karatay had a madrasa, a college of advanced instruction, built in 1251–52. Its door stands near the southeast corner. Beyond it a square pool occupied the centre of a covered court. An opening about five metres across admitted air and light directly above the water. To the west a large teaching hall opened onto the court, flanked by smaller classrooms for use in winter. The students had vaulted cells along the north and south sides.

A tile worker faced different problems covering a straight wall and a curved ceiling. At the foot of the walls, turquoise hexagons fitted edge to edge. Higher up, small pieces of tile made lettering, foliage and stars that reach up around the central opening. In the corners where the square court makes the transition to a round dome, inscriptions name prophets, among them Moses and Jesus. The stars overhead may have called God’s creation to mind. Evidence for what the college taught comes from a different source.

## Five teaching days and money for lamp oil {#foundation-deed}

In July 1253 a deed laid down how the endowment would pay for teaching at Karatay’s college. Food, lighting oil and candles had first claim on the proceeds. Of what was left, one tenth went to the teacher and another to the administrator. There were provisions for a teaching assistant, a doorkeeper and the attendant who kept the premises clean. The students, too, had a claim on the endowment for their maintenance.

The schedule called for five days of instruction each week, with Tuesdays and Fridays free. The teacher had to belong to the Hanafi school of law. His subjects included legal reasoning and hadith, the traditions about the Prophet’s words and actions. Students could come from any of the four Sunni legal schools, although their actual numbers are unknown. Karatay had already made [separate provision for travellers east of Kayseri](chapter:roads-and-caravanserais-of-rum#karatay-deed). A much later set of [kitchen rules for Hürrem’s foundation in Jerusalem](chapter:hurrem-and-the-kitchens-of-jerusalem#portions) likewise spells out who could claim a share. At Konya, before a teacher could begin a lesson, someone had to open the door, sweep the floor and lay in oil for the lamps.
