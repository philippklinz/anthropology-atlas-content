# A village on the Jagst

There is a small wooden wedge in a bed from Lauchheim. Someone may have pushed it in because the joint wobbled or creaked at night. Later, gable boards were added to both ends and the bed became a coffin. We do not know how long anyone slept in it. We can, however, identify the winter in which people felled the oak for the burial chamber. How can we date one funeral more closely than an entire house in the same village?

## Down the slope to the farmsteads {#cemetery-on-the-slope}

Two archaeological sites lie barely two hundred metres apart just west of modern Lauchheim in south-western Germany. People buried their dead on the slope from the late fifth century onwards. Archaeologists recorded more than 1,300 graves in this field, now called Wasserfurche. The houses stood further downhill on a low terrace closer to the river Jagst. This is the field called Mittelhofen, where the sixth-century map stop takes you. Much later buildings left traces over the earlier ones. Reading every outline on the excavation plan as part of a single village gives its inhabitants several centuries’ worth of neighbours at once.

Once the topsoil is stripped away, a timber house often appears as a sequence of dark patches. These are the pits that once held its structural posts. In one part of the excavation, at least three dwellings succeeded one another. Each new house stood close to the site of its predecessor. A farmstead contained more than its main house. As long as people still used the fence and outbuildings, only part of the yard was available for rebuilding. Some long buildings may have provided living quarters and space for cattle under the same roof. Counting the posts will hardly tell us how many people gathered round the hearth in the evening.

## What can a loom weight tell us? {#houses-by-the-river}

Small buildings dug partway into the ground left particularly conspicuous traces. Clay loom weights came from such sunken buildings at Mittelhofen. On an upright loom the long threads of the warp hang from a beam. A weight at the bottom keeps a bundle of them taut so that the weaver can pass the crosswise thread, the weft, between them. Buried in the soil, the weight outlasts the threads it once held.

Archaeologist Valerie Schoenenberg asks a further question. Was an object still lying where someone last wove cloth? Or did earth and rubbish carry it into the pit after the building went out of use? A weight in the fill offers good evidence that people in the settlement worked textiles. To locate a particular loom, a find on the old floor would be more telling. A lump of clay can seldom tell us who sat or stood at the loom either.

## Worn around the neck {#necklace-on-the-slope}

In grave 90, back up on the slope, excavators found a disc brooch at chest level together with beads from a necklace. The state heritage office dates the burial to the first half of the seventh century. Corinna Eberth counted over 22,000 beads across the cemetery. Some sets contain just a few beads, while others have more than three hundred. On some glass beads, a whole pattern appears in a tiny slice. Glassworkers assembled rods of different colours into a cane, cut thin sections across it and applied the patterned slices to a bead. The burial tells us where the beads ended their journey. It leaves open where the workshop stood and how many hands each piece had passed through.

## When a fence enclosed the dead {#graves-inside-the-fence}

Towards the end of the seventh century, the journey to a burial place changed for some households. Small groups of graves lay beside six farmsteads at Mittelhofen. At the south-eastern edge of the village, Schoenenberg could distinguish several successive fence lines around one yard. Its earliest enclosure held none of the excavated graves. A later fence enclosed four, including two with chamber timbers dating to the last third of the seventh century. Entering the farmyard now meant coming close to the dead. A fence line alone can tell us little about who held title to the land or how the people buried there were related.

## One bed, two ways to tell time {#the-bed-in-grave-27}

The wood in grave 27 survived in waterlogged ground. In the photograph of the bed you can compare the arcs cut into the long sideboard with those on the gable. Red and black paint once picked out the patterns. Scraps of leather and plant fibres survive from the padding. The gables were added for the funeral. Tree rings date the felling of the oak, worked green for the chamber, to the winter of 703/704. The excavator therefore places the burial early in 704. The small wedge in the bed belongs to an earlier moment. In [Childeric’s grave at Tournai](chapter:childeric-at-tournai), a signet ring bore a king’s name. At Lauchheim we have the handiwork of someone who may simply have wanted an undisturbed night’s sleep.
