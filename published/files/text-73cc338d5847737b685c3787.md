# A caliph in mosaic and a schoolboy in Fustat

A teacher wrote to his pupil’s father. The boy had settled down to his studies straight away. Then another child had got up and broken his writing board. The report came from a Jewish community in Egypt. The teacher wrote in Arabic, using Hebrew letters. A Latin chronicler of the period might have used the word “Saracens” for Muslims of very different origins. It tells us little about the language of this letter or the religion of its writer. The cities of the caliphates brought together people living under the same ruler who taught their children to read different scripts. The things they left in writing range from a gold mosaic to a note about the broken board.

## Which year did the caliph leave in place? {#jerusalem}

Walk along the octagonal arcade inside the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and you can follow a ribbon of Arabic script. It speaks of one God, of Muhammad and of Jesus, son of Mary. The words also address people who worshipped Jesus as the Son of God. For this message its patrons chose mosaic. Its plant forms and shimmering surfaces would have been familiar from churches around the eastern Mediterranean. One such interior appears in the chapter on [the sixth-century Hagia Sophia](chapter:lives-in-the-byzantine-empire#hagia-sophia-537).

The building inscription carries the year 72 of the Islamic calendar, equivalent to 691/692 CE. The patron of the shrine was the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik. More than a century later an Abbasid caliph, al-Mamun, had his own name inserted in the band. The old date remained. The disagreement between the name and the date helps us recognise the alteration. What the year originally meant is another question. Fred Donner connects it with the completion of the building. Art historian Marcus Milwright thinks the foundation of the shrine in that year more likely. Both are reading the same number.

## Making room for a court on the Tigris {#baghdad}

In 750 the Abbasids had displaced the Umayyads at the centre of the caliphate. In 762 al-Mansur began building a new residence on the Tigris. The palace and mosque were to occupy the heart of the settlement. This inner district also included offices and a kitchen. Feeding a court required space for work that can easily disappear from a list of caliphs. Farther out there were shops and houses. Four gates opened through the circle of fortifications.

One gate bore the name of Kufa, another Basra. The other two pointed by name towards Khurasan and Damascus. To someone approaching the city, the names located it in a much wider world. Geographers including al-Yaqubi later described the Round City in detail. The regular plan usually shown in books is a reconstruction from accounts like these. The point on the map locates the approximate area in western Baghdad. The settlement on the Tigris soon extended far beyond its original enclosure. Even the caliphs took up residence at other sites.

## How many cubits of water? {#rawda}

In 861 the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil commissioned a gauge on the Nile island of Rawda. An octagonal marble column scored with measuring marks stands inside a stone-lined well. The well is round at the bottom and rectangular at the top. A staircase winds down the walls. As the water rose inside, the marks gave its level in cubits. The column itself is nineteen cubits long. Here it is still possible to see how someone could obtain an important piece of political information by taking a reading.

For people working along the canals, the level of the flood had a bearing on the preparation of dykes and irrigation works. The administration used it to help estimate yields and assess taxes. A particular mark on the column suggested different things to a family farming a plot and to an official trying to reckon income. Qur’anic verses about water and vegetation are carved into the walls of the well. A prayer for plenty and a measurement that affected taxation were only a few steps apart.

## What the synagogue kept {#fustat}

When the Fatimids founded the new city of Cairo to the north of Fustat in 969, life continued in the communities of the older town. A Hebrew inventory of 1080 lists textiles for a synagogue. It distinguishes a black-and-white cloth given by Halfon from a grass-coloured piece given by another donor. An old piece and some red brocade warranted a line too. Such details helped someone recognise the items later and remember the people who had contributed them.

The teacher’s letter about the broken writing board survives too. It can only be dated to the eleventh or twelfth century. The year 1080 on the map belongs to the inventory. What connects the two documents is their survival in the Cairo Geniza, a repository for discarded writings in a synagogue in Fustat. The father learnt the name of the child who had broken the board and that the other pupils knew what had happened. His response is missing from the letter. Our knowledge ends just where the conversation in the family was about to begin.
