# A Day of Making a Living

What was there to eat before people worked fields? Some traces are so small that they only appear under a microscope. Others survive on a stone blade or in the chemistry of a tooth. Three sites show how those clues complement one another.

The stops are far apart. They illustrate different environments and different ways of obtaining food. BP means years before 1950. The map shows present-day land and approximate site locations.

## Madjedbebe

Charred plant parts survived at the Madjedbebe rock shelter in what is now northern Australia. Among the tiny remains, the team identified fragments of fruits, seeds and underground plant parts. The layer contains more than a thousand charred non-wood plant remains. Some bear traces that the researchers associate with processing.

Using a plant for food requires knowing where it grows and which parts can be eaten. Consider the difference between that knowledge and what survives in the ground. Soft parts often decay, and burning changes which fragments survive. The number of preserved fragments cannot tell us what share of the whole diet came from plants.

The researchers place these remains in a phase of around 65,000–53,000 BP. How securely the measured sediment ages relate to the early archaeological layer remains debated. You can compare questions when you meet this site again in “Across Seas and Land Bridges.” That chapter explores early presence in Sahul. Here we ask how people used plants.

## Ohalo II

People used a site by the Sea of Galilee in present-day Israel around 23,500–22,500 BP. It was later submerged. Among the preserved remains are grains of wild cereals. A grinding slab and microscopic traces offer clues to how the grain was processed. A study of around 17,000 fish remains reveals another food source.

Look at the blade in the image. The stone is in the middle. On the right, the cutting edge has a polish consistent with working plants. On the left, traces along the edge may result from fitting it into a handle. Researchers can study those traces even though the complete handle is missing.

Gathered wild grain can be ground. The researchers also discuss possible trials of cultivation. The finds do not establish the presence of domesticated cereals. In “Living at the Edge of the Ice,” the same site reappears, this time with hut floors and hearths.

## Taforalt

At Taforalt cave in what is now north-eastern Morocco, researchers examined human and animal teeth and bones. The dated burials in the study span around 15,100–13,900 calendar years BP. Different isotopes, or forms of an element, help researchers compare diets. Some of these signals enter the body through food.

Blue triangles in the figure represent humans. Other symbols show measurements from herbivores and canids. The researchers consider several signals together. They infer that plants played an important part in the diets of the people sampled. The graph does not show a list of meals or percentages of calories from plants.

## What survives a meal?

Compare the three stops. At Madjedbebe, parts of gathered plants survive. At Ohalo II, food remains and tool traces complement one another. At Taforalt, chemical signals accumulated over time offer a view of individual diets. What can each of these methods tell you about daily life?
