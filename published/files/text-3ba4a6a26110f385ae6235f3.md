# What Viking ship timbers remember

A rivet hole can tell you how a ship was put together. An extra hole beside it, serving no purpose in the surviving plank, can tell you that somebody came along later to make a repair. With a longship from Roskilde Fjord it is possible to follow work like this across decades. The names of the people who did it have gone. “Viking” only gets us so far in looking for them. In Old Norse the word could mean someone who went raiding. The Scandinavian societies of the period also included the woodcarvers, shipwrights and households who equipped a ship or decided to give an old one a different use.

For the people who lived in the trading towns, on the farms and in the workshops, follow [People of the Viking Age](chapter:reading-the-viking-age).

## Four sleighs for two women {#oseberg}

In 834 a ship was brought ashore at Oseberg, in what is now Norway, for the burial of two women. It had been built some fourteen years earlier, with fifteen openings for oars along each side. Four sleighs were among the things placed in the burial. On three, even the runners carry carvings of creatures whose bodies twist around one another. These sleighs had seen use before going into the ground. Someone had carved those figures for things that would be dragged along the ground in ordinary use.

There are two questions one might ask of such a grave. What work had to be done before the mound could be closed? The ship and sleighs have a good deal to say about that. What offices did the dead women hold, and for whom had these things originally been made? The wealth of the burial points to women of considerable standing. Neither name survives. Calling one of them a queen would give an answer the evidence has yet to supply.

The [burial chamber and pieces of reworked silk](chapter:oseberg-ship-burial#oseberg) offer another way into the lives and work represented in the grave.

## The names Harald put on stone {#jelling}

At Jelling in Denmark a royal family had another way of ensuring people remembered their names. Around 950 Gorm had a small rune stone set up for his wife Thyra. On the larger stone erected by their son Harald Bluetooth around 965, both appear again as his parents. The inscription then turns to Harald himself. He claims to have won Denmark and Norway and to have made the Danes Christian. An image of Christ occupies one face. To take in the whole monument a viewer has to walk around it.

This was a king making his claims public. Christian objects from Denmark and an account of the baptism of an earlier Danish king, Harald Klak, at Mainz in 826 take us well back before he had these words cut. The two stones show what one family wanted people to know about it. They leave the pace of religious change in individual communities a question for other sources.

On [Gorm’s smaller stone for Thyra](chapter:jelling-and-the-royal-builders#thyra) you can read how the generation before Harald commemorated the same woman.

## Three trees ending in the same year {#newfoundland}

On the northern tip of Newfoundland, people left worked wood at the Norse site of L’Anse aux Meadows. Three pieces from three different trees bore cuts made with metal tools. The outer surface of the timber, including the last ring the tree had formed, survived on each one. That turned out to be crucial. A burst of atmospheric radiocarbon in 993 had left a recognisable signal in trees in widely separated places. Researchers identified the signal in the wood and counted the growth rings outwards to the edge. In each case the tree had been cut in 1021.

That gives us a year when people worked wood here. The first vessel may have arrived earlier. Indigenous communities had made use of the bay over thousands of years. On its southern shore, for instance, are Dorset camps dating to over two centuries before the Norse presence. Knowing the year when three trees were cut is quite a different thing from knowing the date on which people from different shores first met.

Charcoal from [an Indigenous hearth at Black Duck Brook](chapter:farms-across-the-north-atlantic#black-duck-brook) gives a wider date range. The excavation records show what can be said about these visits to the bay.

## Who built a longship on the Irish Sea? {#dublin}

In the bed of Roskilde Fjord archaeologists found parts of another ship. The rings in its timbers gave especially good matches with sequences from Ireland. An oak used for the keelson, the beam supporting the mast inside the hull, was felled in May or June 1042. The ship was built then or soon afterwards in eastern Ireland, probably near Dublin. On the map you can see the likely area of construction. We cannot locate the shipwrights’ yard.

Neither the name of the person who ordered it nor the name the crew gave it has come down to us. Today it is called **Skuldelev 2**, for the Danish place where it was found. The museum estimates about thirty metres of hull and room for sixty oars. A person who intended to take such a vessel off the beach had to be able to bring a great many people together at once. What sort of town could supply the materials and the labour? [Change the point of view to the Dublin waterfront.](chapter:iona-and-the-gaelic-seas#dublin)

The [houses and yards at Fishamble Street](chapter:dublin-on-the-liffey#fishamble) give a sense of life in Dublin beyond the shipbuilders’ work.

## A hull across the channel {#peberrenden}

Extra nail holes and replacement planking reveal a series of repairs. Even after a major repair in the 1060s wear continued to accumulate on the outer faces of the bottom planks. In Roskilde Fjord, probably in the 1070s, the longship was given its last job. Sunk with other ships, the hull helped close off the Peberrenden channel. The same timbers that had carried rowers for decades now stood in the way of an approaching boat.
