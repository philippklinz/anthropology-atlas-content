# A hospital patron at Divriği and the traders of Bursa

The doorway of a hospital in Divriği bore a woman’s name. The adjoining mosque recorded a man’s. These two entrances make a good starting point for anyone interested in the inhabitants of medieval Anatolia. The woman belonged to a ruling family. Stonecutters, woodcarvers and scribes worked under the same rulers, and some left names of their own. From the principality to which Divriği belonged we move into the sultanate of the Seljuks of Rum and then into Ottoman cities. A hospital, a school and a trading inn help explain what those differences could mean in daily life.

## Two doorways and two patrons {#divrigi}

In 1228/29 a large building complex went up below the castle at Divriği. Emir Ahmet Shah of the Mengücekid dynasty founded the mosque. His wife Turan Melek endowed the hospital adjoining it to the south. We can identify her contribution so precisely because of a building inscription over the entrance. A short political history of the district might give the name of its emir. Reading the words at the hospital door gives a different answer to the question of who paid for a public institution here.

In the prayer hall, piers carry a series of vaults of different designs. Around the portals carved leaves and rosettes project boldly from the stone. Other parts of the building called for a woodworker. Ahmet Shah commissioned the minbar, the pulpit in the mosque, in 1241. A carved star on its eastern side bears the name of its maker, Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Tiblisi. His name points to Tbilisi. On its own it tells us little about the language he spoke with the other people on site. Between them the names and materials give a sense of the number of different trades a princely commission could assemble.

## Over a pupil’s head in Konya {#konya}

Farther west a senior official named Jalal al-Din Karatay endowed a madrasa in Konya, a major centre of the Seljuk sultanate of Rum. By the time his school went up in 1251/52, several years had passed since the Mongol victory over the Seljuks at Köse Dağ. Court offices and influential local patrons survived the defeat. An endowment document drawn up in July 1253 records when Karatay’s school had been built.

Anyone coming to study traditions of the Prophet and Qur’anic interpretation entered a courtyard covered by a dome. An opening in the dome admitted light and air. Directly below is a square pool. To the west a large vaulted teaching space opens directly onto the court. Enclosed rooms were available in winter. Hexagonal turquoise tiles covered parts of the lower walls. Higher up, craftspeople set smaller pieces of tile into inscriptions and patterns. The surviving surfaces give some idea of what a pupil could inspect at close range and what required a considerable tilt of the head. A visit to [the brick domes of Isfahan](chapter:cities-and-courts-of-iran#isfahan) provides a comparison from the Great Seljuk court in Iran.

## Which way to face for prayer {#hagia-sophia-1453}

In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople. An immense, centuries-old interior was available when he converted Hagia Sophia into a mosque. People using it for Muslim worship needed to orient themselves towards Mecca. The prayer niche, or mihrab, marks that direction. It is slightly askew in relation to the main axis of the church. The pulpit from which the sermon was delivered stood near the apse. A visitor looking that way can still pick out two different uses of the same space.

Other features of the mosque date from the reigns of later sultans. A photograph showing all four minarets takes in the work of several building campaigns. The Ottomans also added buttresses to the outer walls. The decision of 1453 changed who could use the building to worship and how. Keeping it in use for centuries required continuing work on the inherited fabric. The [account from the old cathedral](chapter:lives-in-the-byzantine-empire#hagia-sophia-1453) leads back through the same door in the same year.

## Who lived off an inn full of silk? {#bursa}

Bursa preserves another commission of the Ottoman dynasty. Mehmed’s son Bayezid II had the Koza Han built there. The trading inn opened for business in September 1491. Bursa had long profited from the trade in raw silk. It lay on the route linking Iranian suppliers with customers farther west. People buying silk to weave or sell needed storerooms, customers and somewhere to put their animals when they arrived.

The plan allows some of those needs to be read off the building. Two storeys of rooms face the main courtyard. The lower annex had stables and warehouses. A small prayer room stands over a fountain in the middle of the court. Part of the inn’s purpose lay a long way from its traders. Its income was assigned to mosques and schools in Istanbul. A merchant taking rooms in Bursa therefore contributed to institutions across the Sea of Marmara. That flow of money is harder to see on a plan. It takes the terms of Bayezid’s foundation to bring it into view.
