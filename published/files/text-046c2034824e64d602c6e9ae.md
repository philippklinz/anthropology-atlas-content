# Ravenna under Theoderic

A lead water pipe from Ravenna bears the name of a king. In two short lines Theoderic takes credit for giving something back to the city. The object itself tells us what it was. Water once flowed through this pipe into the palace district. Several such pipes came to light during work at the junction of what are now Via di Roma and Via Guaccimanni in the 1930s. Who laid them? The stamp names the king. We have to look elsewhere for the people who shaped the lead, kept the water running and drew on it for their own needs.

## Two commanders outside Verona

When Theoderic entered Italy in 489, Odoacer had ruled there for thirteen years. In 476 he had deposed the young emperor Romulus Augustulus and sent the imperial insignia to Constantinople. In Italy he took the title *rex*, or king. Theoderic had emerged from a contest among Gothic military leaders in the Balkans, and the eastern emperor Zeno had made him consul. Now that same emperor commissioned him to remove Odoacer. Both commanders had had to make a place for themselves within the political and military world of Rome.

Theoderic defeated his opponent on the Isonzo and again near Verona. The anonymous chronicler known as the *Anonymus Valesianus* describes Odoacer’s camp on the plain outside Verona. A visitor standing on the Adige today can still identify the city. The account gives few clues to which field became a battlefield in 489. The shaded area on the map accordingly surrounds the city and marks where this episode of the campaign took place.

## Grain behind the walls

After further fighting Odoacer withdrew to Ravenna in 490. Theoderic camped outside it in a pine wood, according to the chronicler. Ravenna had access to the Adriatic, and a naval blockade supplemented the siege only in 492. The chronicler claims that during the siege a Roman measure of wheat cost six gold pieces. His figure conveys the gravity of the shortage to his readers. To a family inside the city the war would already have made itself felt while the armies were still outside the walls.

In 493 the two leaders agreed to share power. Soon after he entered Ravenna, Theoderic killed Odoacer. His army acclaimed him as king in Italy. Ravenna passed from a city under siege to a seat of his government. In Rome the Senate and Pope Symmachus welcomed him in 500. The chronicler records grain distributions and describes how the king set aside revenue from the wine tax for buildings and walls. Wine sold in Rome could now pay for the stone and mortar of the same city.

## Who brought the water back?

In his chronicle Cassiodorus records under 502 that water had begun flowing through Ravenna’s repaired aqueduct. The date comes from the text. The lead pipes carry the king’s name and allow archaeologists to follow a conduit into the palace district. A later order from Theoderic’s administration survives in the collection of letters compiled by Cassiodorus. The king tells landowners to clear saplings from the aqueduct by their roots. He wants water to reach the baths and people who need a drink as clear as it was at the spring. A king could celebrate its return. Whether it still flowed years later also depended on someone pulling up the saplings in time.

## The hands in the mosaic

In Theoderic’s palace church, now the basilica of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo, the palace appears again as an image. Curtains hang between its columns. Hands are visible on several shafts. They once belonged to whole figures, probably members of the court. Mosaicists reworked the image when the church was rededicated after the eastern Roman reconquest. Were these figures originally welcoming their king, or had they raised their hands in prayer? Scholars have made a case for each reading. The surviving fingers give little away about the people’s names.

Theoderic died in 526. His grandson Athalaric was still a child, and his mother Amalasuntha governed on his behalf. In letters announcing the succession the new rulers called for loyalty and promised to honour the rights granted under Theoderic. Only in 540 did Belisarius enter Ravenna and take possession of the city for the emperor Justinian. The war continued elsewhere in Italy. The succession of 526 also mattered in Hispania, where another grandson of Theoderic, Amalaric, became king. The [chapter from Toulouse to Toledo](chapter:visigoths-toulouse-to-toledo) explores the history of that kingdom. Seen together, the pipe and the mosaic preserve two decisions that outlasted him. A craft worker set a king’s name in lead. Another workshop, long afterwards, left just a handful of courtiers’ hands.
