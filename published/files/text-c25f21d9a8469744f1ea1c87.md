# Rice, Millet and Early Pots

What can a broken rice spikelet, a storage pit fill and a soot-darkened sherd tell us? At three sites in East Asia, these finds help explain how people obtained, stored and prepared food. The map dates range from approximately 11,100 to 6,600 BP, measured before 1950. Each stop poses a question, while the timeline shows the varying ages of the finds.

## Tianluoshan

Much survived in the wet ground beside the lower Yangtze in present-day Zhejiang. At Tianluoshan, excavators found wood, acorns, water chestnuts and abundant rice remains. Direct dates on nuts and rice grains place the three contexts studied here between about 6,900 and 6,600 BP. Grasses and sedges, including kinds that occur in rice fields today, support the evidence for cultivation in a wet setting.

Dorian Fuller and colleagues compared the attachment points where grains grew on the plant. When ripe grains fall away naturally they usually leave a smooth scar. Grains that stay attached until threshing tend to leave an irregular break when separated. Spikelets harvested before ripening can look different again, so the researchers counted them in their own group. Across three successive contexts, the share of bases classified as the domesticated type rose from about 27 to 39 percent.

Open the chart and compare the two proportions. The first counts rice among all plant remains recovered by sieving. The second considers a trait within the rice remains. Both proportions change. They are counts of finds and cannot tell us the share of calories people obtained from rice. Acorns and water chestnuts remained part of the food supply.

## Cishan

Farther north is Cishan in present-day Hebei. The pit fills preserve heavily degraded plant remains. Researchers compared the shapes and sizes of phytoliths, tiny silica bodies from the husks. Many forms point to broomcorn millet. In a group of younger samples the researchers also found forms they attributed to foxtail millet.

How old is the early material? Houyuan Lu and colleagues assigned some of the pit samples to about 10,300–8,700 cal BP. Zhijun Zhao questioned the context of the oldest dated material, which may have come from pits exposed by erosion along the edge of a terrace. An archived sample recovered during the earlier excavations gave an age of about 7,670–7,600 cal BP. The map distinguishes the disputed early dating from the younger sample. The date when people first domesticated broomcorn millet remains open.

## Torihama

The last stop takes us to the lakes near Wakasa Bay in Japan. At Torihama, a team looked for chemical traces of fats in the vessels. Charred deposits from the Initial Jōmon group shown here, roughly 11,100–8,000 cal BP, contained molecules that testify to heating foods from the water. In younger vessels the evidence for freshwater products becomes clearer.

The pots reveal part of the kitchen. The soil also preserved acorns, water chestnuts and the remains of wild animals. Those foods are much harder to see through the sherds alone. People at this site combined pottery with fishing, hunting and gathering. To learn how people elsewhere used their pots, we need evidence from those places.
