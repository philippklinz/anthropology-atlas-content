# The Uluburun Shipwreck

An old clay tablet from Anatolia records what merchants paid for tin and for their pack animals. Centuries later, part of a ship's cargo survived off the coast near Kaş. Follow the three map stops from the wreck to an ore district in Cyprus and a glass workshop in Egypt.

## Uluburun

The seabed east of Cape Uluburun slopes steeply. When the ship sank around 1300 BCE, objects scattered down the slope. Underwater excavators found parts of the wooden hull, stone anchors, clay vessels and the heavy cargo. Under some of the anchors, sections of hull survived in the sand. Wood and datable objects give clues to the date. The precise year of the sinking remains open.

The finds included about ten tonnes of copper and one of tin. Both metals travelled as ingots. Pots, glass and other goods lay with them on the seabed. The positions of the finds help reconstruct the cargo. The ports visited on this particular voyage can only partly be inferred.

## Apliki

Look at the copper ingot in the Bodrum museum. It is flat and extends at two corners. Many ingots from the wreck have four such projections, recalling the outline of a spread animal hide. Other ingots are round. Metal in a recognisable shape could be counted, weighed or worked further.

Researchers compared the tiny amounts of lead in the ingots with measurements from ores. The ratios of lead isotopes give clues to the geology of the raw material. For many Uluburun ingots they point to an ore district in northwestern Cyprus. Apliki is a reference site in that district. The exact deposit that yielded the copper and the harbour where the ingots were loaded remain open. Tin calls for a separate material investigation.

## Amarna

The cargo also included thick discs of coloured raw glass. The photograph shows two ingots with weathered surfaces. Researchers measured trace elements and strontium isotopes and compared their results with finds from different regions. The sampled ingots match glass produced in Egypt. Some groups are chemically so similar that they were probably made in the same batch.

In a courtyard at Amarna, excavators found fragments of ingots, unfinished glass beads and small fireplaces. These show another part of the work. A chunk of raw glass could be turned into many beads in a workshop. The precise workshop that supplied the ship remains unknown. Amarna shows how people of the time worked with materials like these.

The wreck preserved many goods in one place. The laboratory measurements and the excavation at Amarna help you follow different parts of their histories from there.
