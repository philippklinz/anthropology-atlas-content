# The Goths in the Black Sea World

A Roman emperor died near the lower Danube in 251. Pieces of a gold coin bearing the portrait of his wife later came to rest in a grave in what is now southeastern Poland. What happened between these two places? An ancient chronicle, the objects from the grave and an excavation in a port on the Black Sea each give us a different part of an answer.

## Abritus

In the summer of **251**, a Gothic army defeated a Roman force near Abritus in present-day Bulgaria. Emperor Decius and his son and co-emperor Herennius Etruscus died. Dexippus of Athens was a contemporary. His account survives in fragments. Inscriptions have helped to locate the Roman city near Razgrad. The battle took place in its vicinity.

Farther north, coin specialists Aleksander Bursche and Kirill Myzgin examined a find from **Ulów**. Grave 19 contained nine fragments of a Roman aureus, a gold coin issued under Decius for Herennia Etruscilla. Cut pieces of gold wire and foil lay with it. The empress can still be recognised on the broken coin in the published illustration. Bursche and Myzgin compared finds like this in Poland and Ukraine with the events on the Danube. They suggest that gold from the imperial treasury entered circulation after the defeat. We can count the pieces in grave 19. Whose hands the coin passed through between minting and burial remains a matter of interpretation.

## Olbia

Olbia, a city of Greek foundation, stood on the estuary of the Southern Buh on the coast of present-day Ukraine. A Ukrainian-Polish team excavated part of the old citadel in trench **R-23**. A piece of a thin-walled dish lay in the hearth of Building 1. Its shape and red coating match fourth-century tableware made in workshops in what is now Tunisia. Archaeologist Serhii Didenko sets the sherd alongside transport amphorae, grey pottery turned on the wheel and hand-built vessels. He classifies roughly seven tenths of the ceramics in this particular assemblage as local and three tenths as imports. A broken dish in a hearth makes a connection between two coasts. What someone once served on it is beyond the reach of this find.

The name **Cherniakhiv–Sântana de Mureș** describes a distribution of related pottery, workshops and burial customs stretching from the Carpathian arc far into what is now Ukraine. It comes from sites near Kyiv and in Transylvania. People whom Roman authors called Goths were among those who lived in this world. A potter might copy a popular shape, a merchant might bring in finished vessels, or a family might pack up its pots and move. Similar sherds could enter the ground by any of these routes.

## Komariv

Farther inland, on the middle Dniester, people worked glass at Komariv. Excavations uncovered a glass furnace, pottery kilns, dwellings and pits used in domestic life or craft production. Olga Rumyantseva and colleagues examined **42 samples of raw glass**. Among the colourless pieces they could distinguish glass of Egyptian and Levantine origin, made at the eastern end of the Mediterranean. The glassworkers also remelted used glass. The evidence points to a workshop active in the fourth and early fifth centuries. The material records the origins of its ingredients. It leaves the language spoken beside the furnace an open question.

At **275** the map picks out part of an early concentration of finds on the Southern Buh. At **350** it shows two selections from what had become a more widespread distribution. These areas group finds from several generations. Drawing a political boundary for a particular year or tracing a single caravan would require quite different evidence. Set the year to **376**. People on the lower Danube were then seeking refuge inside the empire. At Olbia the pottery assemblage in R-23 is dated to the fourth and first half of the fifth centuries. The year 376 falls within that dating window. What the refugees offered the Romans, and why the agreement later broke down, is the subject of [the chapter on the Danube crossing](chapter:danube-crossing-and-adrianople).
